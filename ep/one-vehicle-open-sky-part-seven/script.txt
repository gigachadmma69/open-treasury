Chapter one. A chorus, and its limit.

Six parts in, here is where the series stands. The Lotus Sutra gave us the road: one vehicle, everyone arrives, and the arrival is the discovery of what was already there. Garab Dorje and the Great Perfection gave us the ground, the claim that there is no road at all. Part 5 let the Lotus set its own clock on fire, and Part 6 crossed to the Guhyagarbha and found the same hinge from the other side: the three realms as primordial Buddha-fields, creation without creation, knots tied in the sky. The working claim is that the road and the ground are one thing seen from two altitudes. The junction.

This part is called Seventeen Voices, One Throat. The title promises something, and before anything else I owe you the limit on that promise.

The Seventeen Tantras are the heart texts of the Great Perfection in the Nyingma tradition: the scriptures that Garab Dorje's lineage is said to have carried, and that later masters quote as the final authority. I have not read them. Nobody has been able to hand me a free, clear English translation of them. The English versions that exist are published books under copyright, and the Tibetan originals survive as manuscript scans that would need months of careful collation before anyone could honestly claim to render them. A quick, confident translation by me would be a claim to a reading I have not done, and this series does not make claims like that.

So what this part is, exactly, is a chorus assembled from what can be verified. We will look at the tradition's own list of the Seventeen, labelled as the tradition's claim. We will read one complete short tantra, from a related cycle, in a careful public translation. We will read Longchenpa, the great fourteenth-century systematizer of the Great Perfection, and Mipham, the nineteenth-century master who defended it, in the same translator's English. Every quotation is attributed and checked word for word.

All of those translations are by Adam Pearcey, published by Lotsawa House under a non-commercial licence. So this episode is non-commercial too: no support link, no ads, and the feed marks it with that licence.

One more boundary, as always. These are view-level readings. We stay with what the texts say about reality. Nothing here is a practice instruction, a transmission or a replacement for a teacher.

The question for the part is the one the series keeps returning to. When many voices from the Great Perfection speak, across eight or nine centuries and in very different genres, do they say one thing? Let us listen, with the limit in plain sight.

Chapter two. The corpus as the tradition counts it.

What are the Seventeen Tantras? In the tradition's own description, they are the tantras of the pith instruction series, the section of the Great Perfection called the man ngag sde. A transmission announcement from Wisdom Publications, which I have read, describes them as the unsurpassed, utterly secret section of that series. They were gathered in the Nyingma canon, the Collection of the Old Tantras.

The tradition then lists them. The Vima Nyingtik list, as the Rangjung Yeshe wiki prints it, runs like this. One, the Tantra Reverberation of Sound, which is also rendered the Sound Tantra and, by some, Word-Transcending, and is called the root tantra of the group. Two, the Tantra of Graceful Auspiciousness. Three, the Tantra of the Heart Mirror of Samantabhadra. Four, the Blazing Lamp Tantra. Five, the Tantra of the Mind Mirror of Vajrasattva. Six, the Tantra of Self-Manifest Awareness. Seven, the Tantra of Studded Jewels. Eight, the Tantra of Pointing-Out Instructions. Nine, the Tantra of the Six Spheres of Samantabhadra. Ten, the Tantra of No Letters. Eleven, the Tantra of the Perfected Lion. Twelve, the Pearl Garland Tantra. Thirteen, the Tantra of Self-Liberated Awareness. Fourteen, the Tantra of Piled Gems. Fifteen, the Tantra of Shining Relics. Sixteen, the Union of Sun and Moon Tantra. And seventeen, the Tantra of Self-Existing Perfection.

Two things about that list. First, it is not the only list. The same source notes that the names and the count vary from teacher to teacher and from tradition to tradition. Some say seventeen, most say eighteen, and some add one more for nineteen. The Khandro Nyingtik version includes tantras that the Vima version does not. So when someone says the Seventeen Tantras, they are pointing at a family of texts with a stable center and a fuzzy edge.

Second, the tradition describes what each one is for, in images. The announcement says the Sound Tantra is like a key, because its explanation opens the doors to all the scriptures. The Beautiful Fortune is like a discus, a wheel that cuts away delusion. The Mirror of the Mind of Samantabhadra is like a sword, because it severs deviations. Those are the tradition's images, as the announcement reports them. I am passing them on as a description, not as a reading of the texts themselves.

Then there is the origin story. In the Khandro Nyingtik account, as Erik Pema Kunsang renders it, the master Shri Singha gave Padmasambhava the empowerments of the Dakini Heart Essence and the eighteen tantras. That is a tradition-claim about how the texts came to Tibet. Some scholars date such transmissions differently, and we do not settle that here.

What matters for us is simple. The Seventeen are a family, with a name, a place in the canon, a story of transmission, and a reputation as the last word. Their reputation is what the rest of the tradition's voices quote, and it is in those quoting voices that we can actually listen.

Chapter three. A tantra in forty seconds.

There is one short tantra we can read whole. It is not one of the Seventeen, and I want to say that clearly. It is the Tantra of Self-Emergent Awareness, from the Khandro Nyingtik, the Heart Essence of the Dakinis, and it belongs to a cycle called the Six Tantras of Liberation upon Wearing, as it is printed in the Rinchen Terdzö. Adam Pearcey translated it in 2025 with support from the Tsadra Foundation, and Lotsawa House offers it under the non-commercial licence.

The whole tantra, apart from its colophon, is one speech. It opens with a homage, and then the teacher, Samantabhadra and consort, speaks. The translation: "Ema! Retinue of self-awareness, listen." Then comes the line the whole text turns on: "Actual self-emergent wisdom, singular and all-embracing, naturally arisen, relaxed, free of rigid notions or ideas."

Notice how little there is to hold onto. There is no path in that sentence, no stage, no practice. It names something, self-emergent wisdom, and describes it only by what it is free of.

The next lines carry the claim. In Pearcey's English: "When this is free from all grasping at extremes, all the phenomena of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are released in the expanse of self-emergent rigpa's expression."

Read it against Part 6. The Guhyagarbha said the three realms are primordial Buddha-fields, and that bondage is non-existent. Here a different tantra, from a different cycle, says that all the phenomena of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are released in the expanse of awareness's own expression. Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are named together, and neither is left outside. The grammar is not the same, and I do not want to flatten the difference: the Guhyagarbha says what the realms are, and this tantra says where they are released. But the direction is the same.

Then the sentence about the title of the cycle. "That which brings buddhahood through liberation upon wearing has not emerged, does not emerge and will not emerge." Hold that for a moment. The tantra belongs to a cycle named for a result, liberation upon wearing, and in its own voice it says that the result has not emerged and will not. That is a very Great Perfection thing to say: the thing that liberates is not an event that happens.

The tantra closes by saying it is self-emergent from the expanse of rigpa that is Ever Excellent, which is the meaning of the name Samantabhadra. Then a seal, and a prayer that it meet someone destined for it.

That is the whole tantra, and it is shorter than this chapter. I wanted you to hear that a text can be that short and still be called a tantra. The point of having read it is not to be impressed by its brevity. The point is that every claim in it is a claim about what is already so.

Chapter four. Longchenpa's view poem.

The second voice is one of the most important in the whole tradition. Longchen Rabjam, or Longchenpa, in the fourteenth century, systematized the Great Perfection more fully than anyone before him. Many of his works are vast. The one we read is the opposite: a short poem called The Heart of Definitive Meaning, signed with his name as a yogin, Natsok Rangdrol, written on the slopes of Gangri Thökar, and translated by Adam Pearcey in 2025 for Lotsawa House.

It begins with a homage to Samantabhadra and then says what is being honoured. In the translation: "The awakened mind of awareness, pure from the beginning, does not come or go but is universal and all-pervasive."

Pure from the beginning. That phrase is the technical center of the whole system, the word the tradition calls kadak. It is the claim that awareness was never stained, so nothing needs to be cleaned. The next line says it does not come or go. Put that beside the Guhyagarbha's "Phenomena come and go through an absence of coming and going," and the Lotus's Buddha who appears to die and has not. Three texts, three centuries and cultures apart, all saying that the coming and going belong to the display.

A little further on, Longchenpa gives an image I find unusually clear. "Just as amidst the vast expanse of space, whatever appears and exists, be it the environment or its inhabitants, manifests clearly without ever staying from this space, all the phenomena of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are clear within awareness."

Everything in the world, the places and the beings, appears within space and never leaves it. In the same way, all of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa appear clearly within awareness. Notice, again, that both words are named. The poem does not ask you to get rid of saṃsāra. It says saṃsāra, like nirvāṇa, is clear within awareness.

And then the poem names the key. "The risings of responsive awareness, rigpa's own self-expressions, are freed by themselves as they dawn in pure and simple dharmakāya." The word that matters is freed by themselves. Self-liberation is the Great Perfection's signature claim, and here it is in one line: what arises is liberated as it arises, by itself.

This poem also contains instructions, short imperatives about how to leave things as they are. I am not going to read those aloud. They are practice, and a podcast is not the place for them. What I will say is that the poem's view and its instructions are the same sentence in two moods, and if the view calls to you, a teacher is the right place to take it.

What the poem gives us, for our purpose, is a voice from deep inside the tradition that repeats what the tantra said, in a different register. That is one reason the title says one throat.

Chapter five. Mipham and the mirror.

The third voice is later and sharper. Ju Mipham, in the nineteenth century, was a scholar of such range that he defended the Great Perfection in the language of the Middle Way, the school of emptiness that the whole of Tibetan philosophy argues with. The short poem we read, The Vajra Mirror, is subtitled Eliminating Doubts Concerning Self-Awareness. Adam Pearcey translated it in 2016. The colophon says Mipham wrote down whatever came to mind during a break, staying at the mountain hermitage of Karmo Taktsang. So it is a quick, forceful piece, the kind of thing a scholar writes when the argument has been bothering him.

The target is a certain kind of Madhyamaka reader. Mipham opens by saying that those of little learning and no experience believe that self-awareness in Dzogchen occupies an inferior position to the Madhyamaka's wisdom of equipoise. And then he turns on them, directly: "Ah! You who presume to follow the Middle Way," he says, tell me: when you remain in the dharmadhātu, is that state interrupted, or is it not?

Follow the argument at the level of the view. If your meditation on emptiness is interrupted, then it is not really resting in the nature of things. If it is not interrupted, then something is experiencing it, and what is that? If it is mind, then since the nature of things is the nature of all phenomena, do things themselves appear without obstruction before the mind or not? If they do, how has dualistic perception faded? And if they do not, Mipham asks, what is this so-called experience of the mind? "Can you put your finger on it?"

He then gives his answer. Experience of the nature of things, he says, is individually and reflexively known. In his words: "The dharmadhātu, which is beyond conception, is experienced individually and reflexively in space-like meditative equipoise."

That is the Dzogchen claim, argued as a Madhyamaka. He denies that self-awareness is some limited, lesser thing, and says: "But here, self-awareness is great emptiness." The Mind Only school, he adds, believes consciousness to be real. Here, nothing like that is going on.

I include this voice for a reason. A chorus that only agrees is not much of a chorus. Mipham shows the Great Perfection answering an objection from inside the tradition's own philosophical world, and what it answers is exactly the worry the series has been carrying: that a claim about what is already so might just be vague. His answer is that it is not vague; it is the most exact thing you can say about what experience is.

The poem ends with a sentence I like: you can come to realize, through settling in the space of unity, "what would be the fruit of many aeons of philosophical speculation." That is the same economy as the Lotus's jewel in the robe. The fruit was never far.

Chapter six. The instruction that is not one.

One more short text from Mipham, and then the chorus is assembled. Lotsawa House prints it under the plain title A Dzogchen Instruction, translated by Adam Pearcey in 2026. The colophon dates it to the eighteenth day of the eleventh month of the Fire Bird year, which the note says corresponds to February 9, 1898. It is barely a page.

The title says instruction, and much of it is instruction: how to settle, how to remain, what vision to have. As before, I am not reading those lines aloud. A podcast cannot give what they point to, and pretending otherwise would be a kind of theft. But the poem keeps stopping its instructions to say what is so, and those lines we can hear.

It opens with the strongest one: "Naturally arisen awareness, which is genuinely innate, is and always has been the true condition of all things."

Is, and always has been. Not will be, once the instruction is followed. Not becomes, at some stage of the path. The opening claim of an instruction text is that there is nothing the instruction produces. Whatever the practice is for, it is not for manufacturing awareness.

Then, in the middle of the poem, two more view sentences. "All phenomena are spontaneously perfect within the ground, which is genuine, free and open, deeply at ease, just as it is." And again: "Naturally arisen dharmakāya, primordial, beyond the mind, is unobscured by the webs of conceptuality or doubt."

Dharmakāya, the dharma body, the ultimate in the language of the sutras, here named as naturally arisen and primordial. And then the widest sentence in the poem: "All phenomena are perfect within a state of equality, the totalistic sphere of naturally arisen, pristine wisdom."

Totalistic is an unusual English word, and I am glad the translator kept it, because the claim is unusual. Not some phenomena. Not the purified ones. All of them, in a state of equality. We have now heard that sentence three times in this part, in three different English phrasings: from the tantra, from Longchenpa, and now from Mipham's instruction. Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa released in awareness's expanse. Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa clear within awareness like the world in space. All phenomena perfect in equality.

The poem closes as a blessing. It asks that the fortunate who practice this approach be blessed by the vidyādhara gurus, protected by the yidam deities, cleared of obstacles by the ḍākinīs and guardians, so they may complete the path and accomplish their own and others' welfare. Notice the frame: the most radical claim in Tibetan Buddhism arrives wrapped in devotion, lineage and care for others. Whatever else that means, it means the tradition did not experience this view as a casual thing.

So the chorus stands: one tantra, and three masters' voices across five centuries. Time to ask what they add up to.

Chapter seven. The same sky, the same house.

Now set the chorus beside the two texts the series has already lived with, and do it carefully, because this is the move the whole junction depends on, and it is easy to do badly.

Start with the images. The Lotus Sutra's master image for our situation is a house on fire, and a father finding skillful ways to get his children out. The Great Perfection's master image, in every voice we have heard, is space. Longchenpa: everything that appears and exists, the environment and its inhabitants, manifests within the vast expanse of space and never strays from it. Mipham's instruction: rest in what is like the heart of space. The Guhyagarbha, in Part 6, tied its knots in the sky, and unmade them there. A burning house is somewhere you must leave. Space is nowhere you can leave. The two traditions choose opposite shelters for their central metaphor, and a serious reading has to feel that opposition before it resolves anything.

Then look at what the images carry. The Lotus's house turns out to be the Buddha's own, the father is not lying about the treasure, and the children were never finally in danger of being nothing but his. The road was one vehicle all along. The Great Perfection's space turns out to be awareness itself, pure from the beginning, and everything in it, saṃsāra and nirvāṇa named together, is clear within it, released in it, perfect in it. Different grammar, different centuries, different genres: a Mahayana sutra speaking in parables of emergency, tantras and poems speaking in declarations of primordial completeness.

Here is where I refuse to flatten them. The tantra speaks as Samantabhadra, the primordial Buddha, and its authority is scriptural. Longchenpa speaks as a realized yogin signing a poem on a mountainside. Mipham speaks as a scholar defending a view against rival schools. A sutra addresses a vast assembly. These are not interchangeable voices saying interchangeable things, and a listener should keep the differences: of idiom, of claim, of what each tradition thinks a path is.

But the junction claim, the working claim of the whole series, is that the road and the ground are one thing seen from two altitudes. And this part has added something real to that claim. The Great Perfection voices do not merely resemble the Lotus's ultimate chapters. They repeat the specific structure: everything included, nothing excluded, the end already present in the ground. When the Guhyagarbha says the three realms are primordial Buddha-fields, and the tantra says saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are released in rigpa's expression, and Longchenpa says both are clear within awareness, the same house is being described from inside, as space.

Chapter eight. Two objections.

A chorus like this invites two objections, and both deserve full voice.

The first: this hardens into a slogan. Everything is already perfect. All phenomena are equal. Say it often enough and it stops being a view and becomes a mood, and then it becomes an excuse. If everything is perfect, why help anyone? If saṃsāra is released in awareness's expanse, why get out of bed? The tradition knows this danger cold. It is exactly why these texts were restricted, sealed, given only with authorization, and it is why I am not reading their practice lines. But notice what the texts themselves do. Mipham does not assert the view and move on; he argues for it against the best philosophical opponents of his day, in their own technical language, and he wrote the Vajra Mirror precisely because the slogan version was circulating among people he calls undereducated. Longchenpa wraps the view in homage and frames it as his own realization, offered for those who can hear it. The tantra seals itself and prays to meet a destined reader. The tradition's own behavior says: this sentence, casually held, is not this sentence.

Still, the objection stands at the level of us, the listeners. A podcast can repeat the slogan and someone can take it as permission to be cruel or lazy. So here is the series rule, again, in plain terms. This is a reading of what the texts say about reality. It is not instruction, and it is not a license. A claim about what is ultimately so does not tell you what to do, and the people who lived inside these texts behaved as if conduct mattered enormously.

The second objection is the vagueness worry, and it is the one the series has carried since Garab Dorje. Pure from the beginning. Nothing to do. Already free. Is any of that falsifiable, or is it a way of speaking that can absorb anything and so says nothing? Mipham's answer, from the Vajra Mirror, is that the claim is not vague from the inside: the nature of things is experienced individually and reflexively, and nothing could be more exact than that. But I want to be honest about what that answer can and cannot do. If you have not had the experience, you are taking the exactness on report. The chorus cannot hand you its evidence. What it can do, and what this part has tried to do, is show that the report is stable, argued, repeated across centuries, and held by people who subjected it to the hardest scrutiny their world offered.

That is not proof. It is not nothing, either.

Chapter nine. One throat.

Count the voices. A tantra of the Khandro Nyingtik, spoken by Samantabhadra and consort, claiming to predate every path. Longchenpa in the fourteenth century, the greatest systematizer the Great Perfection ever had, condensing his realization into a page of verse. Mipham in the nineteenth, twice: once dueling the Madhyamikas in their own idiom, once writing a page of instruction dated to a February day in 1898. Five centuries at least between the first voice and the last, and behind them all, the Seventeen, the texts we could not read, which every one of these voices treats as the final word.

What do they say, in one sentence each? The tantra: self-emergent wisdom, naturally arisen, free of ideas, and all of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa released in its expanse. Longchenpa: the awakened mind of awareness, pure from the beginning, all phenomena clear within it like the world in space, what arises freed by itself as it arises. Mipham the debater: self-awareness is great emptiness, the nature of things known reflexively, not a lesser wisdom but the most exact one. Mipham the instructor: naturally arisen awareness is and always has been the true condition of all things, and all phenomena are perfect in equality.

One grammar. Awareness primordially pure. Everything included, nothing excluded. Nothing produced by effort. What arises, liberated as it arises. Five centuries, four voices, three genres, one throat.

Now hold that against the Lotus. The Lotus says the road was always one vehicle, and that arrival reveals what was there all along: the father was never in danger, the jewel was sewn in the robe, the Buddha never died. The Great Perfection says there is no road, because the ground was never left. Part 5 let the Lotus burn its own clock, and we found the sutra itself admitting that its teaching timeline is a skillful device. Kadak, purity from the beginning, is what the road looks like when you stop measuring it. That is the junction, and after this part it no longer rests on one tantra read against one sutra. It rests on a chorus.

And the honesty stays where it was. The Seventeen themselves remain unread by me, because no translation I can verify and freely use exists. What this chorus shows is how the tradition's own masters heard them. That is evidence of one throat, not proof of it. Proof, in this tradition, is not something a podcast can hand over. It is the thing the texts keep pointing at, and pointing away from themselves toward.

Chapter ten. Summary, and what is next.

Here is what this part did. It named its limit first: the Seventeen Tantras, the heart scriptures of the Great Perfection, have not been read in this series, because no free, verifiable English translation exists, and a quick confident rendering would be a lie about work not done. It gave the tradition's own list of the Seventeen, labelled as the tradition's claim, with its fuzzy edges: seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, depending on who counts. It read one complete short tantra from a sister cycle, forty seconds of speech in which all of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are released in the expanse of self-emergent rigpa. It read Longchenpa's poem: awareness pure from the beginning, the world clear within it like things in space, whatever arises freed by itself. It read Mipham twice: the scholar arguing that self-awareness is great emptiness and the most exact wisdom there is, and the instructor opening with the claim that naturally arisen awareness is and always has been the true condition of all things. It set that chorus beside the Guhyagarbha's knots in the sky and the Lotus's burning house, and it let the two hardest objections speak: the slogan that excuses, and the vagueness that cannot be handed over.

The working claim stands, strengthened and still honestly held: the road and the ground are one thing seen from two altitudes.

One debt to record. The true rendering of the Seventeen from the Tibetan, with the manuscripts collated and every choice defensible, remains an open project. It is slow work, measured in months or years, and if it ever joins this series it will arrive as its own thing, announced as what it is, never smuggled in early as a claim. I am naming it here so the limit of this part stays on the record as a debt and not as a branding decision.

Next is Part 8, the last part: One Vehicle, One Taste. The confrontation itself. We will put the Lotus and the Great Perfection face to face, the convergences first, then the honest differences: cause as path against result as path, a vehicle for everyone against a teaching for the destined few, the guru, the question of method. The strongest objections to the whole junction get their full force. And then we go back to the burning house and the open sky, and read them one more time, with eight parts behind us, all the way down to the two sentences this series began with.
