We begin with a colophon, the small print at the end of a Tibetan text, because that is where this episode honestly starts. A colophon is a closing note, sometimes by the author or a compiler, about how a piece came to be written. This one is Shabkar's own. Here it is, in our rendering from the Tibetan. This was written when many poor people, who had nothing to eat and nothing to wear, came again and again to beg at the door of my retreat hut.

Compassion for all beings who had been my mothers, welded up in my heart until I could not bear it, and tears came to my eyes. No storm, no dramatic night, no invented dialogue. The songs colophon tells us people were hungry, they knocked more than once, and the hermit cried. With that small documentary fact is the ground this whole episode stands on, and we will keep returning to it. [?]

The hermit was Shabkar Tsokdruk Rangdrol, who lived from 1781 to 1851 in Amdo, the Tibetan cultural region of the northeast around Rebgong. He was a yogi, a wanderer, and above all, a singer. Tibet had seen singing saints before, Milarepa most famously, and Shabkar is often compared to him.

But Shabkar is not a mascot for a brand of cheerful spirituality, and this episode will not treat him as one. He was a man who wanted, with his whole heart, to be left alone in the mountains and who kept failing at it, because the world kept knocking. A word about the kind of book his life comes down to us in. His autobiography belongs to the Tibetan genre called Namthar, a liberation narrative.

A namthar is a crafted thing. It tells a life as a path toward awakening, shaped to teach, and it is not independent documentation of every event it reports. When we tell you that something happened to Shabkar, we almost always mean his autobiography, or the tradition around it, says it happened. We will mark that difference as we go, because he deserves readers who know what kind of evidence they are holding.

The autobiography, as the Columbia essay describes it, traces his spiritual development from birth through the age of 56, and the journey it maps is geographic as much as inward, from his home in Amdo to Lhasa, to Nepal to Mount Kailash and back. Across the Tibetan cultural region, he dedicated his life to meditation as a teenager, the essay says, and grew into a renowned and influential lama.

But the book's own center of gravity is not the itinerary. It is the voice. Again and again the prose stops and the man sings, and the songs move between grief, delight, and dry humor. That mixture is the sound of this episode's subject, and we will trust it more than any summary, including ours.

Songs fill roughly half of that autobiography, and that is not decoration. In a culture where teaching traveled by voice, a song was a vehicle, memorable, repeatable, singable by people who would never open a book. Shabkar sang to teach, to admonish, to argue, to grieve, and sometimes by his own account for the pleasure of it.

His modern readers at Columbia University, in an essay we draw on in this episode, remark on how the songs braid grief and exuberance together with a sly humor running through them. Songs were how this hermit, who claimed to want no audience, ended up with a very large one. The essay's account of how the songs work is worth slowing down for, because it will govern how we listen.

Length is the first thing. They run long, spinning single images from the natural world, plants, animals, mountains, weather, out to great length, a fewer built as acrostics. Admission works by repetition. One line comes back again and again, until the person it was aimed at cannot claim not to have heard it. [?]

Much of the singing is funny, puns, exaggeration, a deliberate casualness, and the essay reads the humor as a vehicle, the playful surface carrying a serious lesson underneath. Asked a direct question, he might answer with a song that declined to answer it straight. One warning of the essays we take seriously, the songs were written down for posterity, so even their air of joking spontaneity should be read with a grain of salt.

A man composing his own liberation story is composing it, and some songs are not playful in the least. They are about the certainty of death and the uncertainty of when, and those will matter in the next chapter. The four songs are a fraction of what he wrote, and the fraction should be named as such. The telling begins at home, in Rebgong in Amdo, the northeastern marches of the Tibetan world, a country of farming valleys and nomad grassland, monasteries and clan territories, where a family's survival was a daily negotiation with weather, animals and neighbors.

It is the world of every later chapter, the beggars, the hunters, the feuding clans, the guard dogs all belong to it. Shabkar's Horizons would eventually stretch to Lhasa and Kailash, but the moral laboratory of his life was this one crowded, demanding homeland, and his leaving it is the first experiment.

The refusal comes first in the telling. Shabkar's widowed mother wanted what widowed mothers in his world wanted, a wife for her son, a settled household, a man at home. She could point to relatives who practiced the Dharma and stayed nearby doing it. Shabkar wanted none of it. He rejected marriage in the strongest terms his autobiography confined, and he rejected the compromise version of religion too, the kind you can fold into domestic life. [?]

For him, practice meant leaving, no encumbrance, nothing held back. So he left, not only his home village, but his mother and his sister, and according to his own account, he did not leave honestly. He promised to return soon, knowing he would not, and later reasoned it through in the open, in his own book.

All beings have been his mother in past lives, so the claims of this one mother could be weighed against all the others, and a lie told for the sake of the Dharma, he judged, was a minor offense. We report that reasoning because he reported it. We do not dress it up, and this episode offers no one advice to abandon their family.

What the autobiography gives us is retrospective narration. The older writer arguing the young renunciants case on paper, and winning the argument at a price he had not finished counting. Consider also what leaving could mean in material terms, because the doctrine can make it sound weightless. Leaving could carry material costs for a household, alongside the emotional loss.

Our source does not itemize his family's finances. Her pleading, as the autobiography reports it, did come armed with practical examples, the relatives practicing at home. Renunciation narratives float easily into abstraction. This one stays honest by keeping the grief, not an invented ledger at its center. Notice to the shape of his refusal, because it explains the man. His mother had offered him a middle way, and middle ways were on offer all around him in the form of relatives who practiced and stayed home.

He rejected the middle way as a dilution, not a balance. For the young Shabkar, the Dharma was apparently not something you fit around a household. The whole chapter is the bill. The later songs let us hold his fierce commitment beside the grief of leaving. We cannot prove that one caused the other. The tension is part of the story, not an explanation that cancels its cost. What filled the years between the leaving and the return, according to the same account, was exactly the life he had chosen, and it kept succeeding in ways that made returning harder.

Time and again in his songs and his prose, he expresses the desire to practice in solitude. When he finds solitude, the essay says, he sings songs of praise and joy, but solitude kept being interrupted by his own growing reputation. More and more people sought him out for teachings. The hermit who had fled society was being drawn back into it, and the return to his mother kept sliding down a list that the world kept lengthening.

The price came due slowly. Years passed. The return he had promised kept being postponed, each postponement easy, until the news was the kind that cannot be postponed back. He got home too late. His mother had died, and he never saw her again in this life. The Columbia essay calls his sorrow and regret at this a rare and meaningful crack in an otherwise almost superhuman spiritual life, and that seems exactly right.

The ideals had been airtight. The grief got in any way. It is worth pausing on where this scene survives, in his own book. A liberation narrative is supposed to edify, and regret is not the usual currency. Yet the autobiography keeps the scene, keeps the reasoning that led to it, keeps even the uncomfortable argument about the lie.

Whatever else we say about the namthar genre, in this case the crafted narrative chose to preserve its hero's failure at the exact point where his doctrine and his heart collided. That preservation stands in thematic connection with the mother song in the next chapter. And it is why we treat the episode as real evidence, not of what a camera would have seen, but of what the man himself could not stop reckoning with.

He had a sister too, whom he also left behind. The record we have read says little more about her, and we will not invent a subplot to fill the silence. The silence itself is part of the cost accounting. The story remembers the mother. The others who paid for his freedom are mostly offstage, and honesty requires noticing that they are.

Hold the two facts side by side because the rest of his life is the friction between them. Fact one, he believed as a matter of doctrine he never abandoned that every being had been his mother and that singling out one mother was a failure of vision. Fact two, the one mother he singled out anyway in memory and in grief was his own, and he had missed her death.

He did not resolve that tension by giving up the doctrine. He did not resolve it by giving up the grief either. He sang it. That is where the next chapter begins with the song he addressed of all people to himself. The song is short and it is not addressed to us. Its colophon says so plainly in our rendering, "Tsokdruk Rangdrol spoke these words to himself on one occasion when remembering his mother, and it opens by naming its listener.

The first line of Tibetan is an instruction to a single person and that person is the singer. Listen, Tsokdruk Rangdrol. You say that the mother who bore you in her womb in this life was kind, but the beings who in birth after birth without number were your parents and did you good. Why do you not remember their kindness even once?

What has happened to you? That is the whole song in embryo. He is not preaching at a crowd. He is cross -examining himself and the charge is partiality. Because the suffering he is forgetting he goes on is not abstract. Heat and cold, hunger and thirst, servitude, the lame, the blind, beggars, the sick, all these wretched beings who have been your mothers.

Can your mind really throw them away? Then he grounds the argument where he always grounds it in the teaching he received. All beings have been kind mothers. If it is true, as the Buddha said, that there is no difference between the mother of your former lives and the mother of this life, then why has a difference arisen for you between the mothers of before and the mother of now?

Think. What use is the distinction you are making? Two honesties are required of us here, and we will keep both. First, the premise of this song, that all beings have literally been one's mother across rebirth, is Shabkar's Buddhist premise. It is the ground he stands on, not an empirically established fact, and this episode presents it as his, neither endorsing it as proven nor apologizing it away.

Second, notice what the song does not say. It never says his love for his actual mother was bad. It says the love stopped too soon at one door step. What it attacks is the monopoly. To let go in your mind of all beings who have been your mothers, and to remember a single mother alone, that is clinging infatuation.

Do not imagine it is compassion arising. As long as partiality and attachment remain, do not expect liberation from samsara. While attachment remains, do not say you have renounced this life's affairs. Here the audacity of that, aimed at a hermit. [?]

He has left household life, he lives in retreat, and he tells himself not to claim renunciation while one attachment, the most forgivable one imaginable, still organizes his heart. The song standard for renunciation is not the leaving of places. It is the leaving of preference. [?]

By that standard, the door of the hermitage matters less than the door of the mind, and he knows he is not finished walking through the second one. And notice the destination the song names at its climax, to weep for all beings with love and compassion and the awakening mind is to enter the ranks of the great vehicle, the Mahayana. The song does not say, master a philosophy and you enter. It says, let your grief reach everyone and you enter.

Compassion is not the decoration on the path here. It is the door of the path. And the song knows about the tears. It stages them and asks whose tears they are. If you abandon all who have been your mothers and weep at remembering your old mother, then the lamas, the buddhas and their heirs, whoever looked on, would feel shame in their hearts.

Indeed, the essay's summary of the autobiography says he interacts personally with animals, at least as much as with humans, and that his travels took him from Amdo to Lhasa to Nepal to Mount Kailash.

The hermitage was a base camp, not a hiding place. The road kept requisitioning him. The essay gives us one more piece of the picture. As his reputation grew, the man who had fled society found himself mediating feuds between clans in Amdo, feuds that had run in rounds of bloodshed and intervening in wrongs he witnessed on the road, including, on one journey, a man deceiving children and selling them into servitude, whom Shabkar rebuked with both Buddhist reasoning and appointed [?]

reminder of the authorities in Lhasa. His fame eventually reached further than Lhasa. When he and his companions plan to donate a sizable amount of gold to a holy site in Nepal, the Qing courts representative in the region, the Amban, sent intelligence reports on his activities to the court, and the court responded that his religious efforts were permissible. [?]

Think about that arc. The runaway teenager becomes, in the eyes of an empire, a movement to be assessed and cleared. The pattern is the same as at the Hermitage door. He did not go looking for these things. They knocked. And a lifetime of the mother's song's discipline meant he could not pretend the knockers were strangers. So the Hermitage door turns out to have been the whole world in miniature. [?]

Beggars, hunters, feuding clans, and eagle with its prey. The knock kept coming and the answer kept being the same. But Shabkar's refusal to rank beings had a second front, quieter, and in its way just as radical. He refused to dismiss other teachings merely because they differed from his own. The next chapter is a song he's saying about that refusal, and we give it entire. [?]

The song had no title of its own. The title it travels under now was supplied by its modern translator, Rachel Pang, whose 2023 translation we use to audit the rendering you're about to hear. Shabkar simply sang it. Where it survives is the third volume of his collected works in the Shechen Publications edition of 2003, and the long -heading the collected works give it is worth hearing about because it is a stage direction. [?]

The heading describes the song as part of a garland of joyful songs sung while he was wandering in retreat without partiality to urge himself and others toward the Dharma. Self and others urged together on the road in retreat. That is this song's native habitat, not a debating hall, but a man walking singing at his own party spirit first.

It opens with a prayer, and the prayer already contains the argument. To all the lineage lamas of Madhyamaka, Dzogchen, Mahamudra, Zhije and Cho, I pray, bless us that we may be without partiality toward the systems of tenets. Five names, Madhyamaka, the philosophy of emptiness, Dzogchen, the great perfection, Mahamudra, the great seal, Zhije, the pacification, Cho, the cutting.

These were not five flavors of one school. They were distinct traditions with distinct methods and in shabkar's Tibet, distinct partisans. He asks all of their teachers together for one blessing, impartiality. Then comes the image the song is built around. We checked this couplet closely against the Tibetan and our rendering follows it closely.

The state of genuine complete Buddhahood is like the peak of a mountain. Madhyamaka, Dzogchen, Mahamudra, Zhije and Cho are like paths coming from the four directions. The people who practice them travel those paths, though not in the same way. The teachings are a little different from one another, just as the paths are not identical.

Now watch what he does with disagreement, because this is where careless readers turn him into a poster for the idea that all teachings are the same. He says the opposite. If the teachings being slightly out of a court, one party says of another, your teaching is a false teaching, and disparages it. [?]

That is as if the paths of the four directions, which differ in size, in how pleasant or hard they are, in being crooked or straight, should disparage one another. Your path is not a path. It is a false path. The teachings differ. He says so twice. What he forbids is not difference but contempt.

The song is not a claim that the paths are interchangeable. It is an argument that a path is being different from yours, slower than yours, rougher than yours, does not make it a false path. And he is honest about the differences that matter most to travelers, the practical ones. The ways of practicing are not the same. [?]

It is like this. Some ride a good horse, some load a pack animal, some shoulder the load themselves and go. The difference between fast and slow is great. The one on horseback arrives first. The ones with pack animals arrive next, and the ones carrying loads arrive last.

Yet whatever teaching one takes up in practice, to attain the state of Buddhahood is like reaching the mountain peak by whichever of the four paths one has climbed. Read that slowly. A hierarchy of speed survives in this song. He is not pretending every vehicle is equally swift. What does not survive is the conclusion his contemporaries kept drawing, that slower means false, that different means damned.

Arrival is a rival. The summit does not ask which side you came up. Two small words carry the song's whole theology of difference, and the Tibetan keeps the modest. The teachings are cung zad, a little, not quite in accord. A little. He neither inflates the disagreements into damnation, nor dissolves them into nothing. [?]

And the practical instruction that follows from it, in our rendering, is to practice with pure perception whichever of them you yourself are inclined to. Inclination is honored here, not overridden. The song does not assign anyone a path, does not merge the paths into a blend, does not tell the horsemen to carry loads for humility's sake. [?]

It tells each traveler three things. Your path is a path, theirs is too, and contempt is the one move that loses the mountain for everyone. For a song sometimes recruited into modern slogans about all traditions being one, it is remarkably uninterested in oneness. It is interested in conduct on the road.

Then the song does something revealing. It reaches for authorities, and we must be precise about whose voices these are. The next two passages are quotations inside Shabkar's song, saying he attributes to earlier masters. They are his attributions, presented here as he presents them, not as independently collated historical records. [?]

In the teachings there is no black and no white. To take sides among tenets and slander the Dharma cuts the very rope by which one climbs to liberation. So said the Lord of Siddhas, Milarepa. Since wrongdoing against the supports, the Dharma and holy beings is beyond bearing, abandon the force of partiality and hatred, and spread jeweled radiance of pure perception over everything. [?]

So said Panchen Chokyi Gyaltsen. Sit with Milarepa's image for a moment, since Shabkar chose to quote it, a rope. To climb to liberation is, in that figure, to be hauled up a rock face on a rope, and sectarian slander does not merely slow the climb. It cuts the rope. This song focuses its criticism on contempt.

Shabkar can imagine a teaching being slower, rougher, even slightly out of accord, and still call it a path. What he cannot imagine surviving is contempt, because contempt severs the climber's one connection to the summit. And notice the Panchen's antidote, which Shabkar also chose to quote, pure perception, dag -snang, spread over everything like light from a jewel.

That same phrase, pure perception, becomes the entire subject of the next chapter's song. The Mountain Song argues for it. The sunlight song simply stands inside it. A yogi of the mountains quoting Milarepa, and a Panchen Lama, one of the great institutional figures of the Gelug school, in the same breath, in support of the same point.

That is the song in miniature. The mountain does not care which lineage taught you to climb. And he closes by turning the whole thing into a prayer for everyone else. May everyone act just so. Tsokdruk Rangdrol prays to all. Having prayed to all the lamas of old, I sang this song. Through the blessings of the holy ones of former times, may there be no partiality toward the teachings.

The Tibetan colophon is one line. This was spoken by Tsokdruk Rangdrol." just a singer on the road asking the teachers of five traditions to bless his listeners out of a habit, contempt that he clearly believed could cut the rope entirely. The next chapter takes up the shortest of our four songs, sung in retreat, where the same refusal of contempt appears not as an argument, but as weather, sunlight, falling on snow.

The last of our four songs is the shortest and the gentlest, and in some ways the strangest. The Lotsawa house series notes tell us it was composed while he was on retreat on Mahadeva Island in Lake Kokonor, and that the text also appears in his autobiography. Like the non -sectarianism song, it was untitled.

The sunlight of pure perception is its modern name. Rachel Pang translated it in 2014, and her translation audited ours. The song is only four verses. Here is the whole of it in our rendering. Long ago, in the land of snows, through the kindness of the holy ones of old, many profound teachings spread.

But most practitioners of the Dharma, taking them as at odds with one another, like heat and cold, practice attachment, aversion, and partiality. Some of the holy ones have said that Madhyamaka, Dzogchen and Mahamudra are like sugar, molasses, and honey. Each is as good as the other.

That image is really in the Tibetan sugar, molasses, honey. Three sweetnesses that no one ranks by insulting the others. He does not say the three teachings are one teaching. He says they are sweetnesses of different kinds, and that some of the holy ones, note not he himself claiming the coinage, said each is as good as the other.

Then he steps forward, and the step is a confession, not an attack. Therefore I have listened to and reflected on all of them without partiality. Partisans, you who practice with attachment and aversion, please do not scold me. Please do not scold me. After the mountain song's argument, this little song pleads.

He knows what his impartiality could cost socially. A man who studies everyone's teachings fits no party neatly and partisans defend their own. The plea is the sound of that exposure. Impartiality in a partisan world invites suspicion. And remember where he is singing it from. Retreat on an island in a great lake, about as far from the debating courtyard as his world provided.

That setting matters to the song's meaning. His impartiality was not a salon position, adopted where pluralism was fashionable. It was forged in the same solitude where he wept for his mothers, and it has the hermit signature all over it, a light to keep unobstructed. The mountain song argues with opponents he might meet on the road. [?]

The sunlight song has no opponents in it at all, only weather and sweetness and the fear of being scolded, which he answers in advance by pleading, gently, in verse. And then the closing verse, the one that gives the song its name. When the sunlight of pure perception spreads across Madhyamaka, Dzogchen and mahamudra, those towering white snow mountains, a stream of blessings will surely flow.

Sunlight does not argue with the mountain. It does not improve the mountain or correct it. It falls on the whole range at once. And what the mountain gives back, meltwater, streams, is figured as blessing. Pure perception in this image is not a technique for winning debates about teachings.

It is a change of light. One thread ties this little song back to the mother at the center of the episode, and it is a single Tibetan word, drin, kindness. The mother songs are built on it. The mothers are drin can ma, the kind mothers, the ones whose kindness must be remembered. This song opens with the same word in a different direction.

The profound teachings spread into bet, it says, through the kindness, drin, of the holy ones of old. Teachers, in shabkar's accounting, are also mothers. The teachings arrived the way food and warmth arrived, as somebody's kindness, unearned by the receiver, which may be the deepest reason contempt for a teaching felt to him like a family offense. [?]

To curse a tradition was to curse a kindness that had fed somebody. The man who would not rank his mothers would not curse the kindnesses that had fed his mind either. The songs are one song at that depth. Remember the kindness, all of it, and let the remembering widen until it changes the light.

Now the scholars care because this small song sits inside a large claim that is often made too loosely. The non -sectarian movement in Tibetan Buddhism, called rime, is usually associated with the great figures of 19th century calm in the east. Jamgon Kongtrul, Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo and their circle.

Rachel Pang's research in her 2014 study of Shabkar's rime activities and her 2015 reconsideration of the term argues that Shabkar was doing this work earlier in Amdo before those better known calm figures and that his activity belongs in the movement's history. Her research also reports something equally important. [?]

No identified formal contact between Shabkar and the Kham movement. Two springs rising separately. Part of Pang's contribution is lexical, and it disciplines episodes like this one. The Tibetan term at stake, ris med, literally without partiality, has a semantic range wider than the capital M movement it is often translated as.

In her 2015 reconsideration, she traces it across basic attitude and practice and into context of mind and reality themselves. A disposition of non -partisanship that individuals could hold and enact, in study, in teaching, in whom they would learn from, long before and far beyond any coordinated campaign. [?]

Her semantic range for the term is wider than the three strands this episode follows. Those three are our selection, drawing on her analysis, not her definition. Shabkar fits that older, wider sense precisely. His impartiality was a practice, not a platform. He studied across traditions, taught whoever knocked, and praised rival schools in song.

This evidence presents his non -sectarianism through study, teaching, and songs, not as the founding charter of a centralized organization. Retrofitting him as the chairman of a movement he predated would be exactly the kind of sectarian bookkeeping, ours instead of theirs, that his songs exist to refuse. So let the record be stated exactly.

The evidence presented here does not make Shabkar the founder of a centralized rime organization. The sources checked for this episode do not establish a meeting with Patrul Rinpoche, the other great wandering teacher of that century. There are traditional reports that Patrul tried to meet him, and they are worth repeating only as what they are, traditional reports, not a verified meeting.

What the document support is more interesting than the legend anyway. A hermit in the northeast, working from songs and personal example, arriving at the same refusal of contempt that the famous Eastern masters would later take up in their own ways. Pang also cautions and we follow her against melting his position into a modern slogan, his impartiality lived inside a Buddhist framework with Buddhist premises about liberation. [?]

It was not a general claim that all religions are identical and chapter 6's song never says they are. Sunlight on snow then is not a program. It is a disposition with a pedigree. The disposition of the man who had already refused to rank his mothers, refused to rank the beings at his door, and now refuses to dismiss other paths up the mountain, which brings us to the end and to the question of what holds all of this together in one life.

Four songs and we have heard them all. Let us lay them side by side one last time, not as quotations now, but as the shape they make together. The mother song widened one woman's kindness until it covered every being who ever lived. The compassion song stood at a hermitage door and turned that wideness into urgency.

Today, not tomorrow. The mountain song refused to curse other paths. The sunlight song refused even to argue. It changed the light instead. Renunciation and responsibility. Clarity and kindness. Different paths, no contempt. A man left the world as completely as any one of his century managed to leave it, and the leaving kept failing, beautifully, in every direction.

His grief followed him into the hermitage. The beggars found his door. The hunters needed new livings. The feuding clans needed a mediator. The rival schools needed, in his hearing, not refutation, but sunlight. Run the spine from the opening of this episode through it one final time, with the debts acknowledged.

Drawing on Pang's analysis, we followed three strands of impartiality, and the life as the sources let us see it, kept all three under strain. Places without a fixed home. The teenager who fled the household. The pilgrim of Lhasa and Kailash. The island retreatant. The hermit whose hut had adored the world had learned, compassion without preferred beings, the mother songs widening grief, the door songs emergency val, the hunters new livelihood, the aunt and the eagle and the [?]

child being sold, each met as somebody. Respect without sectarian hostility, five traditions prayed to in one breath, sweetnesses beside sweetnesses, sunlight that does not argue. The frame holds because the life kept testing it at its weakest points. The mother he could not reach, the solitude he could not keep, the schools he would not curse.

Impartiality in Shabkar is not serenity. It is a discipline that kept costing him something, which is how we know it was one. One thing must be said clearly at the end, because a high view can be misheard as an escape hatch. Shabkar's Dzogchen was real. His tradition counts him a master of it, and the songs we excluded from this episode include works of direct instruction that circulate widely.

But nothing in the four songs we read suggests that the view dissolves the visitor. If all beings are empty of fixed nature, the hungry man at the door is not therefore imaginary. In Shabkar's life, the highest view and the most concrete mercy are not in competition. The view is what made the mercy impartial, no preferred beings, and a refusal to dismiss other paths merely because they differed from his own.

This episode has stayed at that level deliberately, context and conduct, and offers no practice manual, because the songs themselves point at conduct. Remember, feed, refrain, respect. One more limit, easy to miss, belongs in the open. The autobiographical account, summarized by Best, runs through age 56.

We have not audited later life sources here. Old territories of the later life therefore stand outside the record this episode rests on, and we note the edge of the map rather than drawing past it. A final honesty about scope. This episode is a bounded thing. One secondary account of a long autobiography, four songs in substantial renderings with documented selection, two scholarly studies drawn on for one strand of his activity. [?]

Shabkar wrote vastly more than we have touched. His collected works run to volumes, and whole territories of the life, the retreats, the teachings, the long later years stand outside our four songs entirely. We chose the boundary on purpose. Better four songs actually read, word by word, than a whole life summarized from summaries.

But the boundary should be visible. What we have given you is a door into the house, not the house. The songs at least are sized for carrying, and a door, as this episode has had reason to know, is not a small thing in Shabkar's world. Here's our own closing image, and we mark it as ours, not his, a door, a mountain, and morning light. [?]

The door is the one the beggars knocked on, again and again, until the man inside understood that solitude is not a wall, but a place you return to between knockings. The mountain is the one with paths on every side, each path really different, each really a path, the peak indifferent to our quarrels about the root, and the light is the light that falls on all the slopes at once, asking nothing, ranking nothing, melting the snow into streams that anyone downstream can drink. [?]

He sang about all three. We have only arranged them. He asked, in the compassion song, to awaken quickly, quickly, for the sake of the mothers. The record says he spent the rest of his life acting as if the prayer had a deadline, only he could hear, teaching, mediating, persuading hunters, feeding the conversation between schools that preferred to shout.

If the hermit could not ignore the world, perhaps that is because, on his own account, the world was his family, knocking. The songs remain. They were built to be carried by voice, and they have been carried across two centuries into this episode, into your ears. What they ask of a listener is unfashionably simple, and he aimed it at himself before he ever aimed it at us.

Remember everyone's kindness. Let no grief stay small. And when the knock comes, answer the door. Credits spoken plainly, because this episode stands on other people's work.
