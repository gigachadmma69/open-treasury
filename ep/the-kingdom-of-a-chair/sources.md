## Claim map

### Chapters 1-3: the chair and a disagreement

Stevenson, CHILD'S PLAY, downloaded text approximately lines3510-3578. Child acts a story with improvised stage properties; chair used as mount/castle/dragon; visitor reclaims chair; gardener continues ordinary work amid imagined landscape. These are the essay's scenes, not current observations or universal child facts. The provisional title with a 'broken chair' was rejected: the final title The Kingdom of a Chair does not invent a broken-chair source anecdote.

Stevenson argues that need for props/external enactment can show an imagination not yet able to complete the event privately. Script explicitly labels that his contention, not modern psychology. Baudelaire's chair-coach scene praises minimal staging as imaginative abstraction and challenges demands for mechanical theatrical realism. Course uses the disagreement, does not manufacture agreement between authors.

Our synthesis: objects constrain/enable activity while leaving room for a player. Musicians/instruments and theatrical convention are comparisons, not causal history claims or scientific evidence. No instruction to climb furniture or swing real swords. No assurance that all children tolerate interruption.

### Chapter 4: the unused gift

Baudelaire late essay, paragraphs beginning 'Il y a d'autres parents...' and 'Du reste, il y a des enfants...'. Parent stores gifted object for later; quoted friend says by adulthood he had something else to do; children can arrange collections and request no touching. Script paraphrases and explicitly rejects Baudelaire's suspicion of child collectors as a general judgment. Historical narrator's scene, not independently verified family testimony. Preservation/use/permission argument is ours.

### Chapter 5: soul question

Baudelaire paragraph beginning 'La plupart des marmots veulent surtout voir l'ame...' describes manipulations and breaking open toy, cessation of activity, ends:
'Mais où est l'âme?'

Script preserves French words, uses straight apostrophe rather than typographic curly. Exact punctuation before question may differ in spacing only. Our new translation: 'but where is the soul?' No modern English translation used. It is explicitly labeled our own in spoken script.

Mechanical breakdown description is critical reading, not a recommendation. Script bars dismantling, mentions hazards and distinguishes safe educational inquiry from destructive reenactment. Joint inventory versus play occasion is our interpretation, not a complete theory of cognition or object sentience. No device instructions.

### Chapter 6: wealth barrier and rat

Baudelaire middle essay describes wealthy/poor child separated by gate, fine toy neglected while attention turns toward live rat in poorer child's container. Script preserves scene as literary account, explicitly not independent witness verification, refuses class-marked contempt and animal distress as entertainment. No recommendation to acquire/cage/shake a living animal. Does not romanticize deprivation or say poor children are inherently more imaginative.

### Chapter 7: strongest objection

Stevenson's essay opening contains broad period assertions about sensation, development, terror and maturity; not adopted. Baudelaire's remarks about girls' domestic/social play are sexist; script names and rejects that judgment rather than reproducing it as humor. No developmental diagnosis, universal superior toy, anti-technology claim, or guilt for needing structure/accessibility. Modern ethical interpretation is ours.

### Chapter 8: detail can be fantasy

V&A Sage account explicitly frames dolls' houses as imagined/idealized rather than transparent reality records and describes designers returning separate rooms for modular assembly. Script attributes and limits the claim, no current exhibit visit recommended. No replication of artist room designs or photographs. Comparison of meticulous miniatures with rough props is original synthesis, not a measured superiority result.

### Chapters 9-10: original exercise and close

Optional three-invitation exercise with safe owned/permitted object: story role, pattern/arrangement, quiet looking. Original, not historical ritual, therapeutic intervention or validated developmental tool. No purchase, dismantling, climbing, throwing or damage; adult age-appropriate safety decisions where children involved. No private person or unwilling participant. Explicitly allows preference for a structured/specific object instead.

Closing visitor returns to Stevenson's image but includes possibility actual child may mind, avoiding an inferred universal response. 'Player completes an occasion' and final 'somewhere to begin' are original commentary, not source quotations.
