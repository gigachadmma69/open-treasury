Part Four. What Counts as a Substance. This is the fourth episode in our study of Rongzom's Great Memorandum, the Open Treasury edition. A word about what you are hearing. The English in this episode is our own study rendering of the Tibetan, made by an AI and prepared for scholar review. It is not a published translation and carries no license. Where a reading is unsettled, we say so aloud, and we do not build any claim on it.

In Part Three we asked what counts as a thing. Rongzom answered by sorting out the Indian schools by what each one treats as a real substance. This episode stays on that same passage and slows down. We are working from a printed edition of the collected writings, read directly from the page image, and where the print is worn we say that too.

First, the word itself. The Tibetan word is dzay, and it translates the Sanskrit dravya. In everyday Buddhist philosophy, a substance is something that exists by its own nature, not merely as a label laid over other things. Rongzom is asking which schools say what exists in that strong sense, and which say only that it is named.

He opens the survey with the schools of the Listeners. Some of them, he says, hold that all the things they list are substances. The printed page then gives a short phrase that we can read with confidence: some of these teachers hold that the five aggregates, the heaps taught by the Buddha, are together one substance. That is the reading of our print. A longer, more detailed version of this sentence appears in a second, poorer copy, which we cannot confirm, so we leave it out.

Next come examples. The page gives a chariot and a seat as the kind of ordinary object under discussion. The middle of this line is too worn to read, so we say only this: the examples are of things assembled from parts. They are the standard test cases for the question of whether the whole is a substance over and above its parts.

Then the page turns to a school that lists its substances by number. Our print reads, and we are confident of these words: the four great elements, and space, and then a few more members that we cannot read, together making nine substances, each held to be a substance by its own nature. We do not name the remaining members or the school, because the page does not let us read them.

A correction before we go on. In Part Three we said the memorandum records twenty-one substances, with mind among them. On rechecking the printed page, that number is not settled. We could not read it word for word in either of the two copies we have, so please treat the count of twenty-one as unconfirmed. The rest of Part Three stands as it was, and the notes for that episode now carry this correction. In this episode we only say what the page lets us read. We are saying this plainly because we would rather correct ourselves than leave a number standing that we cannot verify.

The next page turns to the origin of the word. A scholar of this text, in a dissertation on Rongzom, points to a passage explaining it from ordinary life. We give the idea, as that scholar cites it from the modern edition, and not as our own reading. In the world, ordinary people take gold and silver to be valuable, so they grasp and keep them, and for that reason a valued thing is called a substance. In our print we can confirm one word of this passage, the word meaning fit, as in fit to be held. The rest of those lines is too worn to read with certainty.

That is as far as we can responsibly go today. In the next episode we continue from this page once we have settled more of it. Thank you for listening. These are our own study renderings, AI work for scholar review, and unsettled readings are marked aloud.
