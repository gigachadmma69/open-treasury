Two acrobats are balancing on a bamboo pole. One stands on the shoulders of the other. This is a bad moment for a philosophical misunderstanding.

The master proposes a plan. You watch after me, and I will watch after you. Together we will perform the trick, collect our reward, and get down safely.

The assistant disagrees. Each should watch after themselves.

At first this sounds like a victory for the people who say: sort yourself out. You cannot rescue someone else while you are falling off a pole.

But this story comes from a Buddhist discourse, the Sedaka Sutta, and the Buddha does not leave it there. He connects care for oneself with care for others in both directions. Cultivating your own practice protects others. Patience, harmlessness, goodwill and sympathy toward others protect you.

The pole is not divided into a selfish end and a compassionate end. These people are balancing together.

That is where we will begin a difficult question. Should we wake up first and help afterward? Or should we commit ourselves to everyone else's liberation, even if that means remaining until the last suffering being is free?

One answer can become an excuse for indifference. The other can become an obligation so enormous that being alive feels like failing an infinite exam.

And there is another complication. A person can say the right things about either answer while still arranging the world around themselves. I must perfect my realization before I help you. Or: I am carrying the pain of every living creature, so please notice how good I am.

Neither sentence tells us whether anyone is being helped.

We will read the strongest versions, not the easiest targets. The Buddha's account of awakening and teaching. Shantideva's vows. Kshitigarbha's explicit promise to put others first. The Diamond Sutra's astonishing combination of universal liberation and no being liberated. Then public teachings on recognition in Dzogchen and ordinary mind in Mahamudra.

Those are related conversations, not interchangeable systems. We will not make a single ancient author say everything a modern listener would like to hear.

Our question will also become more ordinary. Can someone recognize the nature of mind and still laugh, eat lunch, miss a train, use the toilet, and care about the person beside them without adopting a professional expression of cosmic sorrow?

My answer will be yes to ordinary life, no to indifference, and much more careful about what recognition means.

But an answer earned too early is just a preference wearing a robe.

For now, keep the two acrobats in view. If either loses balance, the other does not win.


Put on your own oxygen mask first. As a metaphor, this has an obvious strength. A person who cannot breathe is poorly placed to help someone else breathe.

But notice how quickly the word first can expand.

It can mean: steady yourself enough to respond intelligently. It can mean: develop a reliable practice rather than living entirely through other people's emergencies. Or it can mean: complete the whole spiritual path before anyone is allowed to ask you for anything.

Those are very different proposals.

The first is often sensible. The last would postpone ordinary kindness until an extraordinary qualification had been obtained. No listening until enlightenment. No soup until Buddhahood. The kitchen would be suspiciously quiet.

Recognition is equally slippery. It might mean understanding an idea. It might mean a fleeting experience of openness. In a particular tradition it might mean a genuine recognition of mind's nature under a teacher's guidance. Or the speaker might be using it for complete awakening, with confusion permanently exhausted.

If we allow those meanings to slide into each other, almost any argument becomes easy. Someone defends a first glimpse by borrowing the authority of a Buddha. Someone criticizes superficial experiences and imagines they have disproved the possibility of realization.

The Dzogchen master Tulku Urgyen Rinpoche makes a distinction that will matter throughout this course. In the public teaching published as As the Clouds Vanish, he distinguishes intellectual understanding, experience, and realization. He also distinguishes recognizing, training, and stability.

A taste is not nothing. It is also not the same as having finished the meal.

That food comparison is his. It matters because it respects the possibility of direct experience without making every experience final. A person can encounter something important and still need training, correction and help.

So let us make two different claims available. One: we should not make the recognition of reality depend on solving every problem in the universe. Two: recognizing something does not exempt us from learning how to live.

You can agree with both.

We also need to keep different Buddhist uses of bodhisattva apart. In the early discourse we will read, the Buddha describes himself before his awakening. In Mahayana texts, bodhisattva covers a path with profound stages of wisdom and compassion. The word is not a reliable way to dismiss someone as a spiritually well-meaning beginner.

Nor will this course assess anyone's attainment. Public confidence, concentration experiences, a moving speech or a good joke will not let us inspect another person's realization.

We are investigating claims, not awarding invisible medals.


In the Noble Search discourse, the Buddha describes seeking what is not subject to birth, aging, illness and death. He recounts studying under teachers, mastering what they offered, and finding that these attainments did not bring the release he sought.

Then he describes reaching release. Only afterward does the account turn to whether he will teach.

This supports a real point for the recognition-first argument. In this narrative, he does not first clear every realm of every suffering being and receive awakening as his final reward. His awakening makes a different kind of help possible.

But the narrative is more interesting than an efficient leadership diagram.

At first he hesitates to teach. What he has discovered is difficult to understand. People delight in attachment. Teaching something they cannot grasp would be wearisome.

Brahma Sahampati asks him to teach. The Buddha surveys the world with compassion and sees different capacities, pictured as lotuses at different depths in a pond. Some beings will understand.

Then he teaches.

The reason for acting is not that universal success has become guaranteed. There are beings who can benefit. That is enough to open the work.

We should not erase the religious setting of this account. Brahma, divine knowledge and release from rebirth belong to its world. We can understand its practical force without pretending the text was originally a secular guide to managing our attention.

Its practical force is also not: help only when everyone is ready. The Buddha looks for whom he can teach and how.

The oxygen-mask intuition survives this reading. Finding release is not a betrayal of those who still suffer. It can be the condition for helping them more effectively.

What does not follow is that everybody else should do nothing useful until full awakening. The story concerns the Buddha's discovery and teaching of the path. Turning it into a ban on an ordinary person's kindness would require another argument.

Nor does the account prove that compassion begins only after awakening. It tells us about this sequence, in this narrative. It does not give us permission to rewrite every Buddhist account of the path around one slogan.

So the first conclusion is modest and strong. You do not need to purchase liberation with a receipt showing that all other beings have already been liberated.

The second is equally important. Once there is someone you can help, the fact that you cannot help everyone does not make helping that person pointless.

Our two acrobats have not left the pole. One person's balance matters to the other. But balance is for living, not for spending eternity admiring how balanced you have become.


Now consider the argument that the whole project is incoherent because samsara has no beginning and no end.

We need to separate those two claims.

In the Stick discourse, in the fifteenth collection of the Connected Discourses, the Buddha says that a beginning point of this wandering is not discernible. Beings wander, obstructed by ignorance and bound by craving.

The discourse does not end by announcing that release is impossible. It urges disenchantment, dispassion and release.

Whatever further metaphysical commitments we make, an undiscoverable beginning is not a proof of an unavoidable future. Those are different directions of the argument.

Even as bare logic, a sequence with no first member can have a last member. That does not prove anything about the actual structure of samsara. It shows why the inference needs more than the word beginningless.

And an individual's release is different from a prediction that the total universe will be permanently empty of every confused being on a particular future date. The texts we are comparing do not all answer that global question in the same way, and this course will not manufacture a census of the infinite.

There is still a serious objection to making your own awakening conditional on universal completion. What precisely is the condition? How could you know it had been met? Why should ending your own confusion wait for the removal of everyone else's?

Those questions deserve an answer. They are stronger than simply saying that no beginning means no ending.

But a vow may not be a forecast. There is a difference between promising that the work will be finished by Thursday and refusing to declare a class of people unworthy of care.

Imagine a commitment never to abandon the people who need you. It does not require you to predict that nobody will ever need anything again. It names a direction for your conduct, not a date for closing the building.

That is an interpretation, and we will test it against texts that support it and one that makes the issue more difficult.

We should also keep the lower realms in focus. In these traditions, hell beings, hungry ghosts and animals are not merely colorful names for unpleasant moods. The texts describe realms of existence and consequences of action. A modern psychological reading may be useful, but it should be labeled as a reading.

Concern for these beings can enlarge compassion beyond the humans who resemble us. It can also become abstract enough that the person asking for help in front of us disappears.

Nothing about a vast horizon requires losing the near distance.

If the universe cannot be finished this afternoon, there may still be something worth doing before dinner.


Now let the other side speak without a comic voice.

Shantideva's third chapter of the Bodhicaryavatara begins with rejoicing. He celebrates acts that relieve the pains of lower realms, the happiness of those who suffer, liberation, the awakening of Buddhas and the stages attained by bodhisattvas.

This is already a correction to the idea that only one sort of rescue has value. The chapter celebrates many kinds of good, including awakening itself.

It then widens the scale of the wish. May I be medicine for the sick, resources for the poor, a guard for the unprotected, a guide, a bridge, a lamp, a place to rest.

Notice the variety. These are responses to different needs. A bridge is not an advertisement for how much the bridge suffers. It allows a crossing.

But we should not soften the chapter into a pleasant list of volunteer opportunities. Its giving is radical. It includes surrendering body, possessions and virtue. Some verses speak of accepting violence and abuse. These are demanding religious aspirations, not a safe instruction for an ordinary listener to submit to harm.

You are not required to make yourself available for abuse to understand the force of self-transcending care. We can name the severity of the text without either endorsing its literal application in every situation or pretending it is absent.

The strongest argument here is that the self can appropriate liberation just as easily as it appropriates money or praise. I want my peace. I want my freedom. Everyone else is an interruption to my very advanced project.

A universal vow challenges that ownership. It asks whether the wish to wake up includes the freedom of other beings or merely a superior refuge for me.

Shantideva does not end this chapter by excluding ordinary happiness. He invites beings to lasting happiness and, before that, ordinary joys.

That matters. The person who is hungry need not wait for a final metaphysical solution before food counts as good. Nor must every laugh justify itself as an immediate escape from a lower realm.

The famous aspiration in chapter ten, to remain while space and beings remain and relieve suffering, carries the same refusal to write anyone off. We have consulted it here through Ringu Tulku's public teaching archive, not invented a new translation.

The challenge to recognition-first is therefore formidable. If your recognition makes other people's pain less relevant, perhaps you have recognized an unusually comfortable idea about yourself.

The challenge does not prove that sorrow is wisdom. It asks whether wisdom has room for someone besides its owner.


It would now be convenient to say that nobody ever meant postponing Buddhahood. All those enormous vows were only figures of speech. Case closed.

The Kshitigarbha Sutra will not cooperate.

In its fourth chapter, two kings consider how to help their people. One vows to attain Buddhahood early in order to guide them. The other puts the people's deliverance first and his own Buddhahood afterward. The text identifies the second with Kshitigarbha.

Here, in one story, are both sequences we have been arguing about.

The sutra does not need a modern critic to discover their difference. It stages the difference itself.

Earlier, in chapter one, a previous-life vow also places the liberation of suffering beings before the speaker's own Buddhahood. The narrator emphasizes that vast ages later he remains a bodhisattva.

So yes: explicit others-first language exists. A fair course must let that fact disturb its preferred synthesis.

The official Fo Guang Shan publication introduces Kshitigarbha as vowing not to attain final enlightenment until the hell realm is emptied. That is a real tradition of devotion, not something to be explained away because it complicates a tidy answer.

What can we reasonably say about it?

First, a narrative of limitless self-giving can express a willingness to go where others would not go. Its power may be the refusal to secure a private exemption from the worst suffering.

Second, recognizing that ethical force does not require treating the narrative as a universally binding scheduling instruction. A listener can admire its courage while questioning whether delaying awakening is the wisest literal strategy.

Third, the text's own two kings prevent a cheap equation of awakening-first with selfishness. One king explicitly seeks Buddhahood in order to help. The motivation matters even where the sequence differs.

And fourth, Kshitigarbha should not be silently exchanged for Avalokiteshvara. They are not two spellings of the same figure. A claim about one vow needs its own source before it becomes a claim about every bodhisattva.

This leaves a genuine tension. We should not dissolve it by calling one side emotionally immature or by calling every objection selfish.

My own judgment is that refusing to abandon beings is a better general guide than requiring your awakening to await a verified universal emptying. But that is my judgment. It is not a replacement verse secretly added to the sutra.

A text can correct us without converting us to every possible reading of it.

We have now earned the right to look for another kind of vow. Not because the difficult one never existed, but because the tradition contains more than one way of relating awakening to service.


In a public teaching on Nagarjuna's Precious Garland, Venerable Thubten Chodron discusses verse four hundred and eighty-five. Its aspiration is to remain for the sake of even one being not yet liberated, even after attaining unexcelled awakening.

That last clause changes the argument.

Awakening is not being withheld until compassion has finished its job. Nor is compassion made obsolete by awakening. The commitment continues afterward.

We should be precise about the source. We are using the verse as quoted and explained in her published teaching. We are not claiming to have established a fresh critical edition of Nagarjuna's text.

Her explanation explicitly challenges the assumption that remaining for beings means declining full awakening forever. She says Buddhahood increases the capacity to benefit them. Remaining refers to continuing to help in their world, not remaining bound by one's own confusion.

That is a Mahayana explanation, not a neutral account every Buddhist school would endorse in every detail. But it directly defeats the claim that universal commitment must always be the opposite of awakening-first.

The image changes from a queue to an open door.

In the queue, everybody else must finish before I am allowed through. At the open door, release is not a reason to lock the entrance behind me.

There is also an important difference between willingness and method. I may be willing to give up my own comfort for someone else's freedom. It does not follow that making myself less capable is the best way to help them.

We know this distinction outside religion. A person caring for someone they love might be willing to stay awake all night. That willingness is meaningful. It does not prove that never sleeping again would be an improvement in care.

That example is ours, not a claim that ancient bodhisattva vows were originally advice about sleep. It makes the logical point visible.

The same caution runs in the other direction. A person might genuinely need solitude and training. That does not turn every retreat into selfishness. The question is what the withdrawal serves and how honestly its obligations are handled.

The strongest reply to the postponement objection, then, is not to pretend nobody used postponement language. It is to show that a vast compassionate vow can also be a vow of awakened activity.

And the strongest reply to private escape is not to demand permanent anguish. It is to refuse to make freedom into abandonment.

There is no need to keep yourself drowning in order to remain interested in people who need a hand.


The Diamond Sutra takes the difficulty somewhere stranger.

In its third section, the practitioner resolves to lead all kinds of beings to complete liberation. Then the text says that, although countless beings are liberated, no being has been liberated.

If we hear only the first part, the task can look like an infinite rescue company. If we hear only the second, we can turn emptiness into an excuse to close the company and keep the money.

The text gives us both together.

In the following section it speaks of giving without basing the gift on an object of fixation. Giving remains in the picture. Its relationship to grasping is being transformed.

A useful reading is that beings are not independent, fixed things, and the helper is not a permanent heroic owner of the rescue. The translator Shraddhapa explicitly explains the passage through dependent arising rather than the nonexistence of everyone who might need help.

That explanation is an interpretation of the sutra. It is not permission to look at a person in distress and announce that nobody exists.

Pain does not need an eternal owner before a kind response makes sense.

Nor must helping produce a permanent certificate saying: I, the compassionate one, saved you, the unfortunate one. That certificate can become another burden handed to the person supposedly being helped.

Avalokiteshvara enters our question especially clearly through the Heart Sutra. There he is called a bodhisattva and teaches the emptiness of the five aggregates. The Buddha commends his teaching.

That is a poor fit for the idea that a bodhisattva is merely someone with a large heart who has not yet encountered wisdom. Here the compassionate figure is the speaker of profound wisdom.

We should not flatten every account of Avalokiteshvara into a single biography, either. The scholar Chun-fang Yu surveys scriptures that present different statuses, including future Buddhahood and prior Buddhahood with present bodhisattva appearance. That is her textual survey, not a new claim from us about the one view supposedly held by all Buddhists.

We do not need to settle all those accounts to see the central point. Wisdom and compassion are not assigned to rival characters who must compete for the title of the highest path.

So if someone says that recognition is Avalokiteshvara, we should hear a possible poetic claim, not a quotation we have established from the sutras. Perhaps they mean that genuine recognition is not sealed off from compassionate responsiveness.

That is worth investigating. It does not mean that a moving phrase has dissolved all differences between recognizing mind, realizing emptiness, taking vows and completing Buddhahood.

Poetry can open a door. It cannot do every job in the building.


Recognition-first now has a better case than merely getting your own needs met first. It can mean that the nature of mind is not manufactured by completing a cosmic list of charitable acts.

But recognition-first has a temptation of its own. If the nature is already present, why train? If it cannot be improved, why apologize? If everything is empty, why stop doing what I enjoy?

Tulku Urgyen's public teaching refuses that shortcut. The distinction between recognizing, training and stability remains. He uses a seed image: recognition needs conditions for cultivation. Devotion and compassion support that training.

He also warns about the flattering conditions of being admired as a special practitioner. Praise can carry someone away just as difficulties can.

This is a useful warning because certainty about our own insight is remarkably available. We do not need to be good at much else to become good at explaining why correction no longer applies to us.

Mingyur Rinpoche's public account of learning Dzogchen makes the same point through a family conversation. He remembers his father undercutting his elaborate performance of meditation. But when he asks whether this means practice is unnecessary, the answer is no. Familiarization, bodhicitta, devotion and dedication still matter.

The same narrative can make us laugh at spiritual posing and stop us using the laugh to escape the path.

We should also be cautious about the word natural. It does not mean that every familiar impulse is wisdom. A bad habit can feel extremely natural. So can a defensive explanation composed at impressive speed.

Dzogchen Ponlop Rinpoche's public discussion of Mahamudra calls ordinary mind simple and ordinary, but distinguishes it from fixation on what convention calls normal. The point is not to worship our habits because they happen to be ours.

The traditions have their own demanding accounts of realization, methods and prerequisites. A public introductory course is not a substitute for qualified guidance, and it will not manufacture an initiation or certify a moment of recognition through a verbal trick.

What it can do is protect a distinction that ordinary life badly needs. Being fundamentally capable of wisdom does not mean every present behavior is wise.

You can be more than your confusion without being finished with confusion.

For the purposes of how we treat one another, a claimed insight should not cancel accountability. Someone else's boundaries do not depend on accepting our spiritual self-description.

That is our ethical floor, not a secret test for ranking realizations.

The seed matters. So do water, shelter, time, and what actually grows.


There is an image of spiritual seriousness in which every face must look as though it has just received the total suffering of the cosmos by registered post.

It is a demanding expression to maintain while ordering a sandwich.

Humor can puncture that performance. It can remind us that eating, digestion, awkwardness and the need to take a shit have not been abolished by our most impressive vocabulary.

The point is not to become proud of being irreverent. That is another costume, usually easier to buy.

There is a useful little joke here. Eating is lovely. Only eating, with no possibility of ever letting anything out, would be a terrible arrangement. As an image for spiritual life, it is less elegant than a lotus and occasionally more helpful. Take in teachings, experience and correction. But let go of the need to collect them into an increasingly important self. That is our metaphor, not a physiological theory of enlightenment.

Mingyur Rinpoche recounts Nyoshul Khen Rinpoche joking about spiritual powers. Why be particularly impressed by levitating a few feet when people already travel around the world in airplanes? In the account, the real emphasis falls on familiarization with awareness and its qualities, not a spectacular performance.

We have a real public source for that joke. We do not need to invent a tale about an unnamed master doing something outrageous to establish that wisdom can have a light touch.

But no laugh is an attainment certificate. A charming person can be cruel. A solemn person can be kind. Someone may cry because they care, because they are grieving, because they are overwhelmed, or for reasons we do not know.

The course will not promote dryness of eye as a higher spiritual rank.

The target is the demand that distress must be performed as evidence of goodness. Or, in reverse, the demand that a mature person must never visibly hurt.

Both demands make other people manage our preferred picture of awakening.

The public teaching by Tsoknyi Rinpoche on loving-kindness and compassion in Dzogchen begins with the wish for full enlightenment for the benefit of beings. It also links love with experienced well-being, and insists that this love should be extended rather than kept privately.

That offers a different emotional possibility. Well-being need not be stolen from suffering people. It can become a basis for how we meet them.

This is his teaching within its tradition, not a medical claim that grief or depression is a spiritual failure. We are not importing the article's subtle-body explanations into a diagnosis, and we are not prescribing its practices here.

Our ordinary question is simpler. Can I be present without demanding that you cheer up? Can I enjoy a moment without needing everyone else to be fine first? Can I help without presenting my distress as the main event?

A joke can make space. So can silence. So can tears.

Their value depends partly on whose need they answer.

Compassion does not require a uniform. Neither does freedom.


Let us return to the acrobats, because the pole can now hold more than a slogan.

The assistant's self-attention is not a retreat from relationship. It is part of what makes the relationship safe. The Buddha then adds the reverse direction: patience, harmlessness, goodwill and sympathy are also ways of caring for oneself.

This is not an ancient endorsement of every modern theory of self-care. It is a specific discourse about practice and mutual protection.

But it gives us a useful way to test our own plans.

Take an imagined person who is repeatedly helping beyond their capacity. They are exhausted, resentful, and beginning to treat the people they help as a debt that can never be repaid. Saying that all beings matter does not tell them which task to accept tonight.

A finite promise might be more honest: I can bring food tomorrow. I cannot answer messages all night. I can help you find someone better placed to assist.

Now take the opposite person. They have discovered a language of awareness in which every request sounds like a threat to their practice. They are always about to become free enough to care, but the date keeps moving.

A finite promise can help here too. You do not need complete Buddhahood to keep an appointment or wash the cup you used.

These are invented examples, not diagnoses of people we have never met.

If you want a small reflection, choose one real commitment already in your life. No spiritual intensity is required. Ask what would make your participation clearer and less harmful. Better attention? A smaller promise? A boundary? An apology? Following through on what you already offered?

Choose the smallest honest next step. Do not add a solemn universal vow merely because a course is ending. Religious vows deserve informed intention, not emotional momentum from a narrator's voice.

And if reflection makes you feel unreal, frightened or detached from ordinary life, stop the exercise. Return to familiar surroundings and ordinary support. Nothing in this course requires making yourself unsteady to prove that you understand emptiness.

The acrobats need not solve the history of the universe before attending to the next movement. They do need to know that movement affects someone else.

That does not shrink compassion to whatever is convenient. The larger vow can keep challenging whom we exclude. The finite action can keep challenging what we actually do.

The horizon and the next step are different things. Confusing them can make us either grandiose or inert.

On the pole, neither is particularly useful.


We began with two apparent choices. Wake up, then care. Or care so completely that you postpone waking up until everyone else is free.

The texts have made that opposition harder to maintain.

The Buddha's awakening precedes his teaching in the Noble Search account. That supports the possibility of helping from release, rather than treating release as a selfish theft.

Shantideva makes a different danger impossible to ignore: freedom can be imagined as private property. His response is a vast commitment that includes ordinary joys as well as liberation.

Kshitigarbha prevents us from pretending that explicit postponement language never existed. The two kings in his sutra represent different orders of aspiration, and we should not flatten them into agreement.

The Precious Garland verse, as presented by Thubten Chodron, gives us continued commitment after awakening. The Diamond Sutra keeps the universal wish while undoing the fixed identity of rescuer and rescued. The Heart Sutra gives the compassionate bodhisattva a voice of wisdom.

And the public Dzogchen teachings distinguish recognition from stable realization while making compassion part of the path, not a rival department.

These are not identical doctrines. They do not become identical because our conclusion sounds nicer that way.

My conclusion is that you should not make ending your confusion conditional on eliminating the suffering of the universe. Nor should you make the suffering of the universe a reason to dismiss the person beside you.

Beginningless does not by itself prove endless. An infinite aspiration does not have to be a prediction of a final empty universe. A first recognition is not automatically full Buddhahood. A compassionate vow is not automatically a refusal of wisdom.

And nobody owes us a continuous display of sadness to show that they care.

That last point has a companion. Nobody owes us a continuous display of ease to show that they are free.

The freedom to be ordinary includes laughter and the unphotogenic necessities of having a body. It also includes the possibility of being moved, making mistakes, accepting correction, and doing the next useful thing without turning it into a monument to yourself.

You can refuse the theater of compulsory sorrow without refusing sorrowing people.

You can value recognition without pretending there is nothing left to learn.

You can hold a horizon wider than your capacity while making promises small enough to keep.

The two acrobats are still balancing. The infinite vow has not removed the bamboo pole. Recognition has not removed the other person.

Attend to your balance. Attend to theirs.

Then, if you can, come down safely together.
