The World That Was Never Impure. Reading Rongzom, part two.

A person finds a jewel and leaves it in a drawer because it looks unimportant. Nothing about the jewel has to become precious. Something about the person's understanding has to change. But if we use that image carelessly, we may imagine a perfect object hidden behind an imperfect world. The text we are about to read resists that easy solution.

Establishing Appearances as Divine is by Rongzom Chokyi Zangpo. This full reading uses Dominic Sur's translation, made in 2024, revised for and first published on Lotsawa House in 2026. The translation is used under Creative Commons Attribution NonCommercial 4.0. The explanations are new editorial commentary and are identified as such. Footnote numbers are not spoken. The source, its notes, and the license are linked in the written companion.

In the Black Snake Discourse, Rongzom questioned whether a reflection needs to be defeated. Here he asks whether the appearances we dismiss as ordinary are already inseparable from the purity of awakening. These are related arguments, but not interchangeable slogans. The first loosens a claim to establish reality. The second gives reasons for speaking of appearance as divine. Our task is to hear the friction between them without smoothing it away.

Divine, in this title, does not mean created by a creator god. The text speaks in the language of a mandala, a sacred configuration of awakened body, speech, and mind. We are studying the claim and its arguments, not giving a deity practice or instructions for empowerment. This reading cannot replace the conditions a tradition places on its practices.

The work contains objections and replies. Some objections are forceful enough to sound like the author's own conclusion. Listen for the change of voice. A good reader must let the objection remain difficult before hearing the response.

Chapter one. No second world behind the first.

Rongzom's text.

In the system of the vajra vehicle of secret mantra, it is said that all worldly and transcendent phenomena without distinction are primordially perfected as the maṇḍala of vajra-like buddha-body, -speech, and -mind and, as such, not something to be achieved , as it were, in the here and now.

To this, one might object: "All these phenomena appearing within the experiential domain of sentient beings are not as they appear. They are fictions and therefore unsuited to being primordially perfected."

In response, we can say that they are only fictions, and, indeed, there are no other phenomena whatsoever that might be set forth or demonstrated other than the confusions of sentient beings. These phenomena, referred to as confusing (or fictive ) appearances, are all that there is. Consequently, non-Buddhists imagine a self of persons that exists permanently. Some Vaibhāṣikas assume the existence of the person is characterized by impermanence. Other Vaibhāṣikas, along with the Sautrāntikas, negate the self but insist upon the existence of phenomena such as the empty aggregates. The Yogācāra assert the existence of the characteristics of dependent phenomena that are empty of the imagined, the existence of which is qualified by emptiness. The Mādhyamikas assume that, ultimately, all phenomena are free from extremes of existence, non-existence, and so on, such that conceptual elaborations are eliminated. And in the secret mantra approach, the two types of truth are inseparable, primordially perfected, and so on. Sentient beings thus set forth and debate their individual views on the character of shared perceptions. They do not debate the existence of subject matter other than appearance.

Therefore, all these phenomena that appear are nothing more than confusion. Furthermore, it is not the case that some non-fiction is established when the fictions are eliminated. Fictions are perfected because of being totally pure in their essential nature. Since that is so, all phenomena are primordially manifestly complete and perfect. Thus, phenomena, appearing as various attributes, are in fact the maṇḍala of vajra buddha-body, -speech, and -mind, which, like the omnipresent perfection of awakening, is none other than the essence of total purity. The difference between sentient beings and buddhas is not based on phenomenal essence. Rather, like the perceptible appearance of specific causes and results within a dream, awakened beings and sentient beings merely appear as separate to the intellect through force of imagination.

Commentary. The opening does not say that ordinary confusion is a reliable guide. It says that there is no separate stock of phenomena waiting behind our appearances to become the subject of the debate. Then comes the harder claim: removing fiction does not establish a hidden non-fiction.

That is why the jewel image needs care. A secret perfect substance underneath ordinary things would be another thing to establish. The passage instead speaks of the purity of the very phenomena in question. It does not locate purity in a second universe.

The school names in the third paragraph map different accounts of persons and phenomena. Vaibhashika and Sautrantika name Buddhist philosophical positions. Yogachara gives a central role to accounts of cognition and the imagined. Madhyamaka is the Middle Way. We do not need a complete history of these traditions to hear the point: Rongzom places their disagreements within a shared discussion of appearance. These are his compressed characterizations, not exhaustive definitions.

The final sentence also prevents a cheap conclusion. Saying that buddhas and sentient beings do not differ in phenomenal essence is not saying that confusion and awakening are experientially interchangeable. A dream can contain different causes and consequences without establishing independently real objects. The analogy preserves a difference while questioning the kind of reality we give it.

Chapter 2. Can purity survive an objection.

Rongzom's text.

Here, one might point out: "Although thus explained in scripture, since it is uncertain whether this is intended literally or has some underlying purport, while it is possible to prove the essential purity of phenomena, it is illogical to assume that the nature of this subject, which appears as a phenomenal sign, is itself primordially perfect. If it were, there would be no basis at all for afflicted states of mind or conditioned existence. There is also no reasoning that would establish such a philosophical position."

From time immemorial, sentient beings, have assumed the validity of the objects appearing within their experience and have offered intellectually confused proofs and refutations about them. Indeed, dialectics is incapable of proving what is of deep and profound significance. Still, reality being inconceivable does not mean there is no technique for penetrating that deeper significance by means of discriminating awareness. Indeed, it is entirely unproblematic if the devout, accessing what is of deep and profound significance through faith alone, having accepted the validity of scripture and the teacher's pith instructions, access the deep and profound with confidence.

It might be contested that "unless something is logically established there can be no accessing it." To such a charge, we respond that it is provable by analogy. The reasoning of reality proves that phenomena are perfected as the maṇḍala of vajra buddha-body, -speech, and -mind. As a sūtra states: "Form is empty of its own nature. Why? Because that is its nature." All phenomena consist in purity by nature. Therefore, the reality of phenomena is devoid of any impure quality. Thus, purity of one's own ordinary body, speech, and mind, is one's own reality, as well; and that purity is perfected.

That is why ordinary body, speech, and mind, distinguished in terms of their ultimate purity, should be identified as the maṇḍala of vajra buddha-body, -speech, and -mind itself. They are inseparable from, without conceptual elaboration with respect to, and completely interpenetrating with, pure buddha-body, buddha-speech, and buddha-mind. When proving something like this by means of the reasoning of reality, the other three types of reasoning are superflous. This is because the reasoning of reality is the basis of the other three and because it is primary.

Commentary. The objection is not simply that the world looks unpleasant. It asks a logical question. If everything is already perfected, what makes affliction possible? And if the scripture can be interpreted in more than one way, why assume the strongest claim is literal?

Rongzom does not answer by promising that ordinary dialectic can first build an incontestable foundation and then deliver awakening on top. He questions that demand. Yet he also refuses the inference that, because reality exceeds discursive thought, no access or discrimination is possible.

There is room for a skeptical listener here. The availability of faith within this account does not mean that every teacher's claim deserves faith. The passage describes how scriptural trust and instruction can function within an accepted path. It does not establish the reliability of every claimant to spiritual authority.

The reasoning of reality turns on a connection between emptiness and purity. If phenomena have no intrinsically established impurity, their nature cannot be defined by the stains that appear. But pause before substituting the phrase everything is fine. That phrase would erase the distinction between a claim about reality and the painful circumstances through which beings move. The text is trying to change the basis on which purity and impurity are understood, not to congratulate suffering for existing.

Chapter 3. The unused jewel.

Rongzom's text.

Others might ask: "Since that reality is unestablished for us, are not other types of reasoning needed?" They may also be employed. For instance, the reasoning of causal activity brings comprehension of a cause by means of a thing's effects (or what it does). Just as we understand that medicine contributes to vitality and poison brings death, we may also observe (via the reasoning of causal activity) that whoever meditates upon body, speech, and mind as the maṇḍala of vajra buddha-body, -speech, and -mind obtains the accomplishment of pure body, speech, and mind. And this proves that all perceptible phenomena have the nature and power of vajra buddha-body, -speech, and -mind. Nor can this be validated by conceptual proof alone without relying upon the force of fact. Imagine someone who has found a precious jewel but has not yet had occasion to use it. He or she might not recognize it for what it truly is and set it aside as insignificant, not having observed its qualities. Later, if an expert in such jewels were to reveal its worth, the owner would clean and honor it, so that its extraordinary qualities emerge and would be evident thereafter. As with this determination of an actual jewel by observation of its function, the value it evinces ,  the qualities of the ordinary body, speech, and mind will only be observed when they are recognized and revered as divine. Until then, their qualities will not be observed. Yet when they are so recognized and revered, their qualities will be observed within the body as the basis itself.

Commentary. Here is the jewel in the text itself. An expert does not manufacture the jewel's capacity. Recognition lets its owner appreciate qualities that were not being used or understood. Rongzom places that example inside an argument from effects and functioning.

It is tempting to translate this into the modern advice to think positively. That is too weak and also misleading. The claim is not simply that a flattering description improves one's mood. He is arguing that an awakened way of approaching body, speech, and mind reveals capacities belonging to their own nature.

A critical reader can still ask whether a change in experience proves that nature, or whether the example assumes the interpretation it is meant to establish. The analogy makes the position intelligible. By itself it does not compel someone who rejects the background account of accomplishment to accept it. Distinguishing intelligibility from universally binding proof helps us appreciate the argument without exaggerating what it achieves.

Chapter 4. What a proof has to do.

Rongzom's text.

The reasoning of dependence also applies, whereby the cause of a thing leads to the realization of its effects, establishing production. In dependence upon a seed, there is a sprout. This is also used to validate conventions: the convention "bad" is validated in dependence upon the convention "good." Similarly, all perceptible phenomena are phenomenal appearances of mind as such. All pure and impure dimensions of experience are mental effects generated in dependence upon mental causes. Therefore, all pure and impure dimensions of experience are mental outcomes ("effects") generated via karmic predisposition; both are established as authentic. Designations of which is mistaken and which unmistaken are also established by the reasoning on dependence. Thus, we must be sure to realize that the pure domain of experience is the authentic perceptible appearance.

When there is such proof by means of these three types of reasoning, there is no need to prove anything by means of the reasoning of logical proof. This is because, apart from reasoning on reality, cause, and effect, there is no reasoning pertaining to either direct perception or logical inference.

What then, one may ask, is the purpose of the logical proof? For those of inferior faculties, the reasoning itself must be established beforehand in order to assess the meaning it constructs through the introduction of (i) a logical subject of an inference, (ii) something to prove about that commonly accepted subject , the predicated property to be proved, a comparison via (iii) analogical example; and the determination of (iv) pervasional relations obtained between inferential reasons and property predicated ( sādhya ), including counter-pervasional relations, all of which are elements that must obtain.

Commentary. Three patterns are already in play: reasoning about reality, reasoning from an activity or effect, and reasoning about dependence. The fourth is the articulated logical proof whose parts the translator now names.

The language of pervasion is unfamiliar but the basic requirement is recognizable. A reason must actually support the property being claimed. An example cannot merely resemble the conclusion in one attractive detail. It must illuminate a relation that holds for the case under discussion. The counter-pervasion tests the relation in the opposite direction.

Notice also the role of an agreed subject. If the two sides are talking about wholly unrelated things, a formal argument cannot get started. This will become crucial when the work asks whether the water seen by humans and the pus seen by hungry ghosts are appearances of a shared subject.

Rongzom's ranking of reasonings may offend the philosopher who wants a discursive proof to be the gate through which every truth must pass. That is part of the disagreement, not an embarrassment to hide. He is questioning whether discursive justification has the final authority its defender assigns it. At the same time, the existence of this treatise shows that he does not regard reasoning as worthless.

Chapter 5. Scripture enters the argument.

Rongzom's text.

There may still be questions. For instance, "While that is sufficient proof for some, there are those for whom the reasoning of logical proof does not settle the matter decisively, so prove it another way, through scripture!"

First, it ought to be established by scriptural proof for both parties. As the Viṣayāvatāra - jñānālokālaṁkāra Sūtra states:

Eternally unborn phenomena are tathāgata , All phenomena have similarities with sugata ; Yet, the intellectually immature fixate on features, Wandering in a world of phenomena that do not exist.

In that context, the logical argument is that "eternally unborn phenomena are tathāgata ." The logical subject of the syllogism is "all phenomena." The comparison via analogical example is "like the sugata ." "All phenomena are tathāgata " is the proposition.

To the question of whether this logic is undermined by direct sensory perception, the scripture states that "the intellectually immature fixate on features" (i.e., phenomenal characteristics), "wandering in a world of phenomena that do not exist." Just so, children, without mastery of language, grasp at optical illusions such as falling hairs and enjoy the wheel conjured by the spinning of a firebrand. Likewise, ordinary beings who are intellectually immature enjoy fixation on the features of objects in experience that are imagined by means of confused consciousness. This amounts to the enjoyment of non-existent qualities or phenomena. Therefore, the proof is as follows: all phenomena are tathāgata (i.e., consist in suchness), because eternally unborn phenomena are tathāgata (consist in suchness) as in the example of the bliss-gone ones ( sugata ) of the three times.

If asked "How are sentient beings and buddhas similar?" we say a bliss-gone one is not distinguished in terms of ordinary body, speech, and mind, but in terms of actual reality. As scriptures such as the Vajracchedikā Sūtra state, the noble person ( āryapudgala ) is distinguished in terms of the uncompounded. Just as a noble one attains nobility by attaining the unconditioned (i.e., nirvāṇa ), all phenomena are tathāgata due to attaining the nature of the unconditioned (i.e., śūnyatā ). The experiential realm of characteristics is not like that. In that way, those desiring to experience what does not exist end up experiencing phenomena that do not exist.

Commentary. The first scripture named here is the Sutra of the Ornament of the Light of Wisdom, whose Sanskrit title has just been read. The second is the Vajracchedika, often called the Diamond Sutra. We are hearing the passages as Rongzom's work presents them through Sur's translation. This reading has not collated their separate canonical witnesses.

Tathagata and sugata are titles associated with a buddha. Sugata is rendered here as bliss-gone one. The translator also explains the force of tathagata in this argument as consisting in suchness. Suchness points to how things are, rather than to an additional object located behind them.

That distinction is essential. The argument does not require us to imagine a pillar with a tiny enlightened personality inside it. It concerns what can be said of phenomena in their lack of intrinsic arising, compared with the unconditioned by which an awakened one is distinguished.

The firebrand image is another way of separating an occurrence from what is imagined in it. A moving point of fire can seem to make a wheel. The wheel's appearance does not establish a solid wheel. That is why an appeal to ordinary sensory appearance cannot simply end this debate. But identifying sensory error also does not establish every rival interpretation. The proof still depends on the scriptural and philosophical commitments its parties share.

Chapter 6. The river and its rival realities.

Rongzom's text.

While one may assume phenomena to be perfect in nature due to their absence of essence, one could question whether there is a rational logical proof that proves the maṇḍala of vajra buddha-body, -speech, and -mind at the level of mere appearance. That, too, is provable. That is, it is provable in an instant or provable in progressive stages. For those with minimal obsessive fixation on their own perceptions, understanding comes about by means of instaneous proof. For them, it is proved primarily through analogy.

Consider how pretas see a stream of water as pus. Some of them will have also heard how humans see the stream as water. Some pretas thus contend that pus is the actual reality while water is a wholly imagined form. For others, the argument is: pus is an impure appearance, and, for that reason, the water seen by humans is itself something authentic. They will therefore say: "Friends, this pus that fills the stream we pretas commonly perceive is seen by humans as water alone. If those empowered to use water, dedicate it and make a gift of it to pretas, it appears as water to us too, and exists as a resource. Thus, it is like the dedicated water we have obtained at various times in the past." When establishing the connection in the context of an instaneous proof, this constitutes a proper pervasional relationship.

Additionally, the statement, "if those empowered with water, dedicate and make a gift of it, all the water that is itself present in experience is just water, like the water we have heard about again and again", establishes proper definite pervasional relations here. The same applies to the appearances of ordinary physical bodies and environmental resources that people commonly perceive. Some have heard that completely pure beings see them as a divine maṇḍala, buddha-bodies and buddhified resources. They also know that it is taught in the secret mantra approach to the path that apparently ordinary physical bodies and environmental resources are the divine maṇḍala itself. On this basis, some among them assume the ordinary body and resources to be genuine reality while the divine vision is something wholly imagined. Others assume that, since the perception of the ordinary body and resources is impure, then, in accordance with what is seen by pure beings and exalted in the secret mantra approach to the path, divinity is itself the reality of things. Consequently, they will say: "Friends, these objective appearances of ordinary physical bodies and resources that are common perceptions for the likes of us are the divine maṇḍala itself. If those empowered to practice in the pure domain of experience make a gift of their yogic attainment of seeing appearances as divine, then, for people, too, these experiences will be present in perception and experience as divine. Hence, it is like in the past when, some among us, those for whom, from time to time, yogic attainment arose, attained divine realms of experience."

The proof stated here that is the determination of pervasional relations for this tantric pramāṇa is as follows: "If those with control over the pure domain of experience make a gift of their yogic attainment, then everything that is present as a resource within the divine domain of experience is divinity itself, just as it is the divine domain of experience for someone with yogic attainment. And, when those empowered to practice in the completely pure realm of experience make a gift of their yogic attainment, this domain of experience is present as a resource within the divine realm of experience."

The proof, moreover, is not undermined by direct perception, which is tainted by karmic obscuration. For example, in the past, when compassion stirred the gods, they offered gold to a destitute woman, but it became coal. Yet, for those who have a connection of karma and compassion, the appearance of gold will gradually emerge, just as the destitute woman was able to make use of some of the gold offered to her by Agni, the god of fire.

Commentary. Pretas are hungry ghosts in Buddhist cosmology. The scene asks us to imagine beings for whom a river appears as pus while it appears to humans as water. Some insist their own perception is the truth and the other perception an invention. Others admit the possibility that the more afflicted perception is not authoritative.

Within the story, a gift makes another experience available. Rongzom uses that pattern to discuss the relation between ordinary and pure vision. This is an argument employing a religious account of karmic perception and transmission. We are not offering instructions for making such a gift or claiming that an ordinary demonstration has independently verified the cosmology.

The analogy makes a powerful challenge to perceptual arrogance. The fact that a world appears this way to us does not, by itself, make our way of seeing the final tribunal. Yet the analogy has limits. It does not show that every unusual perception is purer, nor that intensity of conviction proves wisdom.

The gold and coal story makes the same point in another register: the gift received is conditioned by the recipient. That is a claim within the text's account of karma and compassion. It must not become a reason to blame a poor or ill person for what they suffer. The philosophical question is about the authority of an appearance, not permission to judge another person's worth.

Chapter 7. An argument must meet someone somewhere.

Rongzom's text.

If their own perceptions are not initially established as being true, those who are exceedingly obsessed with their own perceptions will not engage with the basis itself. Therefore, with such people, establish their ideas first, before gradually introducing different philosophical perspectives. The way to do so is exemplified by the two pretas mentioned above. On the basis of a contrary philosophical view, one argued as proof, "This river of pus that we pretas commonly perceive is not simply pus in reality. It is actually water, too, because of being an appearance or mental image, like this pus and water, which are themselves obtained after being dedicated and gifted to us." Also, "Whatever is a mental image is entirely appearance in character, like some pus appearing to the common preta and the water one obtains after it is dedicated. The great river filled with pus, too, is an appearance or mental image. These two images of water and pus, moreover, are not distinct. They correspond to a common appearance of fluidity, like the appearance itself of pus."

The following constitutes the comparable pervasion relation for establishng that logically: for persons who accept water and pus to be mental appearance, then, since these statements are logically established for both parties and that is accepted as a logical subject, this reasoning is established. Just so, after initially affirming them to be comparable, the character of the pus should be refuted thus: while both are comparable insofar as they appear as mental images, since the appearance of pus is impure, it is an error, and the water is not an error since it is a pure appearance. According to this view, what is impure is erroroneous; and what is erroroneous is impure.

Pure and fictive appearances should be regarded in a similar way. In other words, according to this very logic's determined pervasion, pus is fictive appearance, the appearance of error; wheresas water is not erroroneous; it is non-fictive appearance but appearance nonetheless.

Commentary. Rongzom now turns from the quick analogy to a staged argument. People fixed on their own perceptions cannot simply be told that everything they take for granted is false and expected to follow. The reasoning first establishes enough common ground for a disagreement to make sense.

But he is not offering a bland relativism in which pus and water are equally good because each is someone's experience. The argument retains a direction: afflicted appearance is erroneous, and pure appearance is not.

That direction is also where the philosophical pressure belongs. A critic can ask what warrants the equation between purity and correctness. If purity is defined only as whatever a favored group sees, the argument would be circular. Rongzom's wider effort is to connect purity with the nature of phenomena, the possibility of awakening, shared scriptural claims, and the examples of transformed perception. Whether that connection convinces us is the live question. Merely repeating the word pure cannot answer it.

Chapter 8. Neither a second object nor an arbitrary picture.

Rongzom's text.

Accordingly, what appears to ordinary beings moving through conditioned existence, even from the point of view that all phenomena are nothing but illusory appearance, these apparent bodies and objective resources composing body, speech, and intellect that are naturally dissatisfying, are not only features of a world giving rise to afflictive states. Rather, they are features of awakening embodying the completely pure field and domain composing buddha-body, buddha-speech, and buddha-mind, like what appears for pure beings. Moreover, it is said that apparent divinity for those who have attained sublime yogic accomplishment and as a vase, pillar, and the like for ordinary sentient beings, are mental images. Thus, anything and everything whatsoever, appearing as divine for some and appearing as vases, pillars, and the like, for others, is mental appearance, entirely appearance in character.

Objective appearances of physical bodies, worlds, and resources for ordinary beings are in fact the experience of mental images. The two images are not even separate on account of both being connected to the common experience of the physical bodies, worlds, and domains of experience comprising physical body, speech, and mind, like the experience itself of pilllars and the like. For those accepting mind as appearance, this logic is sound.

First, establish comparable pervasional relations in such a way. Thereafter, the character of suffering should be negated. Though both these appearances are comparable insofar as they are appearances of mental images, since appearances of suffering and worlds of suffering are impure appearances, they are fictions. And since appearances of freedom and worlds of freedom, awakening and maṇḍalas, are pure, they are not fictions. That which is mistaken pertains to that which is impure; and the impure is fiction. This pervasional relation is comparable. The following ought to be realized with cetainty: "The pure and the fictive, too, should be seen this way. According to this very logic of definite pervasional relations, the appearance of the ordinary is fictive, an appearing fiction. Deity is appearance that is unmistaken, an appearance of the unmistaken."

Commentary. We can now distinguish three proposals. One says ordinary things exist in their own right and a divine picture is placed on top. Another says ordinary and divine appearances belong to wholly different objects. Rongzom is arguing against both arrangements. He treats them as appearances within the same field of dispute, while refusing to make the afflicted appearance the final measure of that field.

This is where the earlier Black Snake reading returns. There, freedom was not secured by stamping on a reflection. Here, purity is not secured by manufacturing an additional object behind the appearance. In each case, the argument challenges the extra reality we supply to organize the task of liberation.

The connection does not make the two works identical. The Black Snake ends by denying a superior metaphysical thesis. This work actively defends the language of divine appearance. Rather than dissolving the tension, keep asking whether the positive language is functioning as another object of fixation or as a way of loosening the authority of the supposedly ordinary.

Chapter 9. What could two disputants share.

Rongzom's text.

The statement that each mental image is not distinct needs some further explanation. For some, while it is possible that in meditating on imagined vases, pillars, and the like, as divine, such objects may appear divine, possessed of buddha-body, buddha-speech, and buddha-mind. This is because being a pillar and so on is not the objects' character, just as the land may be filled with bones and the like during meditation on the unpleasant. Likewise, it is said that even appearances associated with meditation, such as the all-encompassing sense fields, are mastered forms, which, being instances of forms in the phenomenological field [i.e., imagined], are not said to pertain to the character of a thing.

Some say: "When appearance is divine, since it is only one's own appearances that become divine, the character of common appearance does not become divine." In response to such an objection, we must point out that common perception is the reason not even the images are different.

Just so, for pretas, appearance as pus derives from comparable individual karma and appearance as water also derives from comparable individual karma. Therefore, it is untenable to declare different bases. For both, the common appearance of fluidity is unmistaken. Thus, it is logically possible for both parties to take the river as the logical subject of a syllogism. If that, too, were to be refuted, because the appearance of pus itself is one's own appearance, there would be no logical subject of a syllogism that is established for both proponent and opponent in a debate.

Likewise, in common perception, in both the appearance of impure aspects of body, speech, and mind and in the pure appearance of vajra buddha-body, buddha-speech, and buddha-mind, a body-speech-mind triad qualifies the field and resources. Both are correct common perceptions, which means that positing appearance as the logical subject is tenable.

If common perception is refuted by stating that "every perception is different because of being one's own perception," then since things such as pillars are appearances of one's own, a logical subject would not be found that is established for both proponents and opponents. Therefore, the problem of different logical subjects in establishing appearances as divine does not apply.

Commentary. The objection is excellent. If meditation can make something seem divine, why should that change a shared world rather than only a private image? Rongzom does not answer by denying that there is any shared subject. He needs one for the debate itself.

His river example gives the parties common fluidity without requiring that pus and water be identical characterizations. Likewise, the common field of body, speech, and mind can be the subject of disagreement about purity.

We should not turn this into an unseen neutral substance underneath the appearances. The text is arguing that disputants can have a subject in common, not discovering a hidden material river immune to every characterization. Nor does it follow that there is one cosmic mind of which everyone is a fragment. Neither conclusion is supplied by the fact that a debate has an agreed subject.

There is a methodological lesson here beyond the particular doctrine. If two positions use the same word but attach it to unrelated subjects, their disagreement may be theatrical. A serious argument must show what is shared before it can say what is mistaken.

Chapter 10. Even a pure appearance is still an appearance.

Rongzom's text.

Yet one may still ask whether the divine associated with what is completely pure is described in terms of actual reality and gnosis; and, if so, how could it be the appearance of a mental image?

It might be said that the divine associated with what is completely pure is described in two ways linked with two aspects. Just as the reflection of a face in a mirror relates to the mirror insofar as the appearance is a quality of the mirror, but also to the face since it is a facial reflection. Similarly, perceptible appearance of divine buddha-body, buddha-speech, and buddha-mind are described in terms of gnosis and reality because they appear due to the potency of gnosis penetrating reality and the practice of the path of compassionate aspiration. Those divine appearances are also appearances of mental images qualified by habitual tendencies. Thus, they are included in the mind as such.

One might ask how the divine appears via habitual tendencies? Habitual tendencies associated with the two types of fixation appear as subject and obejct. Positive habitual tendencies appear as extraordinary buddha-attributes, such as major and minor marks of a nirmāṇakāya . What's more, habitual tendencies derived from views of the self cause one's mind-stream to appear distinct from the divine continuum, as if they were objectively separate.

It is the habitual tendency toward linguistic expression that causes characteristic marks to appear differently. The nature of production derives from habitual tendencies associated with the limbs of conditioned existence. The complete exhaustion of all habitual tendencies is not even the appearance of the completely pure domain of experience. The existence or non-existence of an awakened one's pure worldly gnosis should be analyzed in a similar manner, though it is an unimaginable phenomenon.

Commentary. This passage prevents the discussion from stopping at a beautiful vision. A divine appearance can be described in relation to gnosis and reality, yet remain an appearance associated with mind and habitual tendencies. The mirror example gives two relations without inventing two separate reflections.

Gnosis here is a translation term for a kind of knowing discussed within the Buddhist account of awakening. It should not be silently replaced by whatever a modern speaker happens to mean by consciousness, intuition, or awareness. Those substitutions could decide the argument before we have understood it.

The marks of a nirmanakaya are features associated with a manifested form of buddhahood. The text is interested in how even extraordinary appearances are characterized, conditioned, and known. It then says that the complete exhaustion of habitual tendencies is not even the appearance of the completely pure domain. That is an arresting qualification. The most magnificent image cannot simply be promoted into the final nature of reality.

If the Black Snake asks whether we must defeat an image, this passage asks whether we must enthrone one. An image of perfection can become a subtler possession. The text will not let the beauty of the image settle the question.

Chapter 11. Purity is not a personality hidden inside us.

Rongzom's text.

Again, a question arises: "When describing the nature of the completely pure divine realm, what is it that is described in terms of gnosis and reality? Is it the case that whatever it is does not exist in the nature of the fictions appearing to ordinary people?"

This too is incorrect. Even the consciousness of an ordinary sentient being is an intrinsically pure reality; and gnosis is not merely contingent upon the generation of bodhicitta . The presence of self-occuring gnosis is proclaimed in the sūtra collections acceptable to the likes of both of us. In accordance with this view, a Prajñāpāramitā text states that "even the consciousness of an ordinary being is qualified by inherent purity."

And the "Turning of the Dharma Wheel in the Realm of Suyamadevaputra" chapter of the Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra states: "Multitudinous world realms will be consumed in unimaginable fire. Space will not be consumed in fire; and so it is with self-arisen gnosis."

Although unnecessary, more citations could be offered to justify what is well known in the secret mantra approach to the path. So, since thoroughly course obscurations are purified, common appearance would be unmistaken, too, appearing as completely pure fields and resources. Therefore, the logical subject is not different.

Commentary. These paragraphs bring intrinsically pure reality and self-arisen gnosis into direct discussion. The sutra collections are not an ornament here. Rongzom appeals to sources that he says both parties accept. Prajnaparamita refers to the perfection of wisdom literature. The Gandavyuha is another Mahayana scripture, here cited through a named chapter. The quotations belong to Sur's rendering of Rongzom's work; their separate witnesses have not been checked for this reading.

There are difficult philosophical questions about exactly how to understand the relation between ordinary consciousness, purity, and gnosis. This public reading does not resolve those questions through a newly invented Tibetan translation.

One boundary is clear enough to keep. The passage does not license an easy equation between the ordinary personal self and an eternal spiritual substance. A claim that even ordinary consciousness is intrinsically pure is not automatically a claim that a permanent person survives unchanged at the bottom of experience. If we import that conclusion, it is our addition.

The fire-and-space image gives a contrast between what is consumed and what is not consumed in the cited account. It is not an empirical physics claim about the mind. We should let a religious analogy do its own work without recruiting modern science to certify it.

Chapter 12. What cannot be added at the end.

Rongzom's text.

Summarizing what is established, it is declared: " For as long as everything is separated into appearances such as space and time , perceived in association with completely pure bodies and resources or completely impure bodies and resources that endure in spacio-temporal terms, then, since they pertain to the experience of a single moment in the ground consciousness, there is no primordium that is an attainable quality capable of being attained , just as, for example, the properties of space are not affirmed in space." This syllogism, too, is a flawless proof.

The eightfold assemblage of consciousness, too, everything that grows from or typifies its activities of separation, is encompassed by the ground consciousness, which means these forms of consciousness are not different. Distinction between continuums of self and other, moreover, are divided in and by persons; they are not qualitatively different phenomena.

There is a range of different reasonings, too. Proving fictive experience to be authentic in that way is not without problems under the power of appearance itself. If the intelligent establish the divinity of appearance in this very manner it will not in fact be impossible to prove the proposition.

The Great Establishing Appearances as Divine was composed by Dharmabhadra. May it be virtuous!

Commentary. The conclusion is compact and difficult. The ground consciousness is part of the account of cognition being discussed, not a term we should quietly rename the eternal ground of the universe. The eightfold assemblage refers to a Buddhist analysis of consciousness. A full account would require more than replacing eight technical terms with a single attractive word.

The sentence about a single moment and an unattainable primordium is particularly compressed in this translation. We will not pretend that reading it smoothly settles its interpretation. The reliable minimum for this reading is that Rongzom resists treating the purity under discussion as a fresh quality to be acquired, and again denies that differences between persons establish qualitatively separate phenomena. Beyond that, an exact account of the passage calls for philological work this audio has not performed.

The author's closing confidence is also part of the text. Calling a syllogism flawless does not relieve us of testing it. Our responsibility as readers is neither automatic assent nor automatic dismissal. It is to identify what follows from the accepted premises, what those premises require, and what remains open.

Dharmabhadra, named in the final line, is Rongzom's Sanskrit name. With that colophon, we have reached the end of the complete work.

Two complete short works now stand beside one another. In the Black Snake Discourse, an appearance does not need to become an enemy. In Establishing Appearances as Divine, what appears ordinary does not have to be replaced by a second world before purity can be discussed. Neither claim is permission to ignore harm, to manufacture a spiritual identity, or to mistake a vivid experience for proof of realization.

The wider tantric conversation is already present. Rongzom's surviving writings include a major commentary on the Guhyagarbha, the Secret Essence tantra. That connection is established by the corpus, not guessed from a resemblance. In a much later Guhyagarbha aspiration, Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro links the indivisibility of the two truths with purity and equalness. That gives us a real later comparison, not evidence that Rongzom was citing a text composed centuries after him. The source link is in the companion. We have not read the tantra or its ritual instructions in this installment.

The same discipline must govern a dialogue with later Dzogchen works. A later formulation may help us hear a question, but it cannot be inserted into Rongzom as though he had written it. Similarity is not a citation. Later agreement is not proof of historical influence. And a later vocabulary should not erase the early author's own difficulties.

The next stage of this series is a corpus reading, not a promise to turn every catalogue title into an instant authoritative translation. We must distinguish Rongzom's own works, his translations of other authors, writings about him, subdivisions inside larger collections, and materials unsuitable for public reading. Anything not yet read remains marked unread.

For now, the river returns us to the snake. One mistake is to treat the appearance as a thing whose authority cannot be questioned. Another is to use the word illusion to stop attending. A third is to give an appearance the name divine and assume the work is over. These readings leave a more demanding task: to understand what our account of an appearance is doing, and what kind of freedom we expect that account to buy.
