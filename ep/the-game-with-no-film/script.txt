The Game With No Film
The Open Treasury, Narrated by Tara

Full transcript

On the second of March, 1962, in a drafty arena in Hershey, Pennsylvania, a town that smells of chocolate, the Philadelphia Warriors played a home game against the New York Knicks. Hershey was eighty five miles from Philadelphia, and the Warriors played there to win new fans. About four thousand people showed up. What they saw that night has never happened again. Wilt Chamberlain scored one hundred points in a single game.

Here is the strange part. There is no film. The game was not televised. No videotape exists. What survives is a photograph, Wilt in the locker room, holding up a sheet of paper with the number one hundred written on it, a radio recording of the fourth quarter, made by a college student in Massachusetts who rigged up an antenna to catch the Philadelphia broadcast, and the memories of the people who were there. That radio call now sits in the Library of Congress. The announcer, Bill Campbell, told his listeners, if you know anybody not listening, call them up, a little history you're sitting in on tonight.

So here is the question this course asks, and it is not a trick question. Did the game happen? Of course it did. Can you go and watch it? No. You can watch grainy footage of young Wilt from the turn of the sixties, his rookie season opened in the fall of fifty nine, but the hundred point game itself is gone from every screen on earth. It survives only as traces. A photo. A tape. A record book. A memory.

Now hold that fact, because it turns out to be the exact shape of one of the deepest questions in Buddhist philosophy. There is no time as a separate substance. No container, no river, no cosmic clock. And yet there is sequence. Wilt really did play before Michael Jordan. Causes really do ripen into effects. Some people, drunk on the ultimate view, say time does not exist and leave it there. That take is too thin, and the Buddha's own philosophers would say so. This course is the full answer. Moments, emptiness, the wheel of time, the fourth time, and then the honest conversation with Western philosophy and physics. Two truths, held together. By the end, the game with no film will tell you everything.

Chapter one. The puzzle in one lifetime.

Start where everyone actually starts, not with physics, but with the puzzle of a single lived day. Sixteen centuries ago, Augustine of Hippo sat with this in book eleven of his Confessions, and his honesty still stings. What is time? If nobody asks me, I know. If I want to explain it, I do not. He noticed that the past is not here, the future is not here, and the present, if you press on it, shrinks toward nothing. So where are the three times? His answer is one of the great lines in Western thought. Perhaps there are not three times, but three presents. A present of things past, which is memory. A present of things present, which is sight. A present of things future, which is expectation.

Sit with that. Everything you call the past, your childhood, last Tuesday, the hundred point game, is only ever touched as something present. A memory is a present event. A photograph is a present object. Expectation is a present tension. Nobody has ever stepped out of now.

And yet, and this is where the lazy reading of Augustine goes wrong, the gone-ness of the past does not make it nothing. Your memories are not hallucinations. The photograph of Wilt is not a fantasy. Something real happened, and it left the world arranged in a way it would not otherwise be arranged. The puzzle is not whether the past matters. The puzzle is what kind of reality it has.

Buddhist philosophy took this puzzle more seriously, earlier, and in more technical detail than any tradition on earth. Not because Buddhists are obsessed with metaphysics, but because the stakes are liberation. Karma is a claim about time, that what you do now ripens later. Impermanence is a claim about time, that everything conditioned falls apart moment by moment. And awakening, in the deepest tellings, is a claim about time too, that there is a knowing which does not move with the stream at all. So the tradition could not afford a shrug. It built, over two thousand years, at least five great answers. We will walk them in order, from the most concrete to the most radical, and each one will hand something to the next.

Chapter two. Moments, and the game of the four masters.

First answer: moments. The Abhidharma, the analytical heart of early Buddhism, took the flowing world and asked how small it cuts. Their answer: the ksana, the moment, the finest slice of time there is. Everything conditioned, they said, is momentary. Not long lasting things that eventually change, but a stream of discrete events, each arising, flashing its causal power, and ceasing, replaced instantly by its heirs. You are not a thing that persists through time. You are a sequence that pretends to be a thing, the way a film is a sequence that pretends to be motion.

Hold the film metaphor, it will keep working. A movie is still frames shown fast. The Abhidharma says experience is the same, except there is no projector, no screen, and no audience, only the frames, each one caused by the last. Causation is the only glue. Nothing travels from moment to moment. There is no passenger. There is only the handoff.

But if nothing persists, a scandal follows. If the past is truly gone, how can a past deed ripen now? How is anything about the past true at all? One great school, the Sarvastivadins, bit the bullet in the most interesting way. Their name says it: sarvam asti, everything exists. Past dharmas, present dharmas, future dharmas, all exist. Not in some museum outside time. Notice, and scholars are clear on this, the Sarvastivadins never posited time as a separate substance or container. No cosmic stage. Just the dharmas themselves, somehow real across the three times.

How? The great commentary, the Mahavibhasa, records four masters with four answers, and Vasubandhu retells the contest in the Abhidharmakosha. Dharmatrata said the dharma changes its mode of being while staying the same entity, like a gold vessel melted and recast: new shape, same gold. Ghosaka said each dharma carries all three temporal marks, and one is active while two wait, like a man in love with one woman who is not indifferent to the others.

Buddhadeva said the times are relations: a woman is a daughter in relation to her mother and a mother in relation to her daughter, same woman, different standpoints. And then Vasumitra, whose answer the judges crowned. A dharma, he said, differs only in position, in state, in activity. His image is a counting rod. The same rod set in the units place means one. Set in the tens place, ten. In the hundreds place, one hundred. The rod never changes. Its position does all the work. A dharma in the position of the future is not yet active. In the position of the present, it fires, it does its work. In the position of the past, its work is done. Same dharma, different position in the sequence.

Even the rival Sautrantikas, who rejected all this and said only the present exists, kept the core insight: time is not a thing. The whole magnificent argument, centuries of it, happens without anyone anywhere positing a time substance. Sequence, activity, position. That is all time has ever been. Remember Vasumitra's rod. It is the first real answer to the Wilt question: before and after are positions in a causal order, and positions are as real as anything needs to be.

Chapter three. Dharmakirti: to be is to cause.

Second answer, and for my money the single most useful idea in this whole course. Dharmakirti, seventh century, the logician of Nalanda, asked the most practical question in philosophy: what does it mean to be real? His answer cuts like a knife. To be real is to do something. To have causal power. In the Pramanavarttika he states it as a rule: whatever can actually produce an effect is what really exists; everything else exists only by convention, as a fiction we agree to use.

Feel how radical that is. A real thing is not a thing that sits there. A real thing is an event that makes a difference. Fire is real because it burns. A memory of fire burns nothing, so the remembered fire is not real in that sense. And notice what this does to time. Nothing can spread its reality across many moments, because causal power happens now or never. Whatever is real, Dharmakirti says, occupies exactly one location in time. There is no numerically same you lasting from breakfast to dinner. There is a series of exquisitely similar moments, each caused by the last, and the series gets a name, you, the way we name a river that is never twice the same water.

So on this view only the present moment is ultimately real, because only the present moment can cause anything. The past cannot act. The future cannot act. Done. Now the objection arrives on cue, and it is your objection: but the past matters. Memory works. Records work. Wilt really did score a hundred, and we know it. If the past does not exist, what is our knowledge about?

And Dharmakirti's answer is the jewel. Traces. You never touch the past directly, and you never needed to. A past event, while it was present, did what real things do: it caused effects, and those effects caused effects, in an unbroken chain that reaches into this moment. The photograph is the far end of a causal chain that started in a locker room in Hershey. The radio tape is the far end of a chain that started in Bill Campbell's booth. Your memory of your tenth birthday is the far end of a chain that started at a cake. Memory does not reach back and grab a vanished object. Memory is a present state with a pedigree.

This is why statements about the past can be true without the past existing. The truth lives in the chain. The game with no film still happened, because happening is what present moments do, and its happening rearranged the world, and the rearrangement is here, readable. Evidence is not a lesser substitute for the past. Evidence is what the past becomes. Every past you have ever known, personal or historical, has always been this: present traces, reliably caused.

And notice what this does to the be here now teaching. It stops being a greeting card. The present is not one small room you are trapped in, with the past lost and the future fantasy. The present is the only place where causation ever happens, which makes it the only place where anything can be done. The past is here, as trace. The future is here, as tendency. Now is not small. Now is the whole workshop.

Chapter four. Vasubandhu, and the dream of place and time.

Third answer: Vasubandhu again, but now in his Yogacara phase, pressing the question inward. If experience is all we ever have, what grounds the order of experience? His Twenty Verses opens with the claim that all this is representation only, and the objector immediately raises our theme. Verse two: if there is no outer object, then the fixing of place and time cannot be sustained. Things happen somewhere, somewhen, reliably, for everyone. Dreams are private and slippery. The waking world has addresses and dates.

Vasubandhu's reply: look again at the dream. Inside a dream you are in a definite place, at a definite hour, doing definite things, and none of it is anywhere at all. Determinate place and time can appear, fully convincing, with no external support whatsoever. So the reliability of waking spacetime does not prove a world out there. It proves that representation can carry order.

Do not misread this as saying nothing matters, it is all a dream. The Yogacarins kept karma, kept causation, kept the stream. Their point is subtler: the time you experience, its texture, its speed, its heaviness, is constructed by mind. And you already know this. An hour in pain and an hour absorbed in music are the same hour to the clock and not the same hour to you. Grief slows time. Flow deletes it. In deep meditation, seasoned practitioners report time going strange in exactly the ways these texts predict.

This is the practice payload of the whole chapter. If experienced time is constructed, it can be deconstructed, and that is trainable. The dream argument is not a skeptical trick. It is a tool. The mind that built the dream can notice the building, and the noticing loosens time's grip. Keep that in your pocket. When we reach Dogen and Dzogchen, the loosening becomes the point.

Chapter five. Nagarjuna: time under the knife.

Fourth answer: Nagarjuna, and this is the one the ultimate view people quote and, I will argue, half misunderstand. Chapter nineteen of the Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way is called the Examination of Time, and it is short enough to break your certainty in six verses. It opens like this, in my own close rendering from the Sanskrit. If the present and the future depend on the past, then the present and the future exist in the past. And if they are not there in the past, then how could they depend on it?

The vice is this. The three times lean on each other for their meaning. Present means between past and future. But if they lean on each other, they cannot stand on their own: if present and future exist in the past, they are the past; if they do not exist there, the dependence breaks. Try the other horn: maybe the times are independent, each a thing in itself. Then sequence dies. A past that is its own self sufficient thing cannot give way to a present. Either way, no self existent time survives analysis. The chapter closes by widening the knife: if time depended on any entity, where is time without an entity? And no independent entity is ever found. So where would time live?

Now the part the thin readings skip. Nagarjuna's target has a precise name: svabhava, inherent existence, existence from its own side. He is not attacking your calendar. He is attacking the idea that time could be found, under analysis, standing on its own. And his signature move is to turn emptiness into the engine of the world, not its grave. Because things are empty of fixed nature, they can change. Because time is empty, sequence is possible. A truly existent past would be frozen and unreachable, so nothing could ever become past. A truly existent future could never arrive. Ironically, it is the believer in real solid time who makes the world impossible. The empty world is the only one where anything can happen at all.

He even defends the convention he just dissolved. The Middle Way is famous for the two truths: conventionally, fire burns, seeds sprout, Wilt precedes Jordan; ultimately, none of it is findable under analysis. Denying the conventional truth is not depth. Nagarjuna names it plainly: it is nihilism, the extreme he argues against hardest. So when someone says, with a little smile, ultimately time does not exist, you now know the full sentence. Ultimately time is not findable as a thing, and conventionally it works perfectly, and the second half is not a consolation prize. It is half the teaching.

One more gift from the same book, because it makes the abstract concrete. In chapter two Nagarjuna examines motion itself: the path already gone over is not being gone over, the path not yet gone over is not being gone over, and a going separate from both is never found. Motion, like time, cannot be located as a thing. And yet, he would be the first to say, people arrive. Trains arrive. The hundredth point goes through the net.

Here is a way to feel it. Analysis is like zooming into a photograph. Zoom far enough and the picture dissolves into pixels, then into nothing pictorial at all. The failure to find the picture in the pixels is not a proof that there was no picture. It is a proof that picture was never a substance. It was an arrangement, a dependent pattern, real the way arrangements are real. Time is an arrangement. Which means it is empty, workable, and, as the next chapter will show, far stranger than empty.

Chapter six. Huayan: the ten times.

Fifth answer, and it is the one that sounds like poetry and works like logic. The Flower Ornament Sutra, the Avatamsaka, is the Himalaya of Mahayana vision, and it contains a teaching called the ten times. Listen to the shape of it. Take the three times. Each one contains its own three: the past of the past, the present of the past, the future of the past; the past, present, and future of the present; the past, present, and future of the future. That is nine. And the tenth is all nine at once, the total, one instant holding the whole.

This is not mystical inflation. It is a precise claim about what any moment actually is. The Huayan masters built their whole universe on dependent origination: every thing is what it is only in relation to everything else, so every thing carries everything else within it, the way each jewel in Indra's net mirrors every other jewel. Time gets the same treatment. Every moment is what it is only as the product of all moments before it and the condition of all moments after it. Therefore, in a real sense, each moment contains the total past and the total future, enfolded, not as miniature copies but as dependencies. The sutra says bodhisattvas explain all past, present, and futures this way.

The second Huayan patriarch, Zhiyan, drew the moral in his Ten Mysterious Gates. The ten time frames, he wrote, by virtue of dependent arising, mutually identify and interpenetrate, yet without losing succession and duration. His image: ten fingers make a fist without losing a finger. Read that twice. The interpenetration is total, and the sequence is intact. The whole is in each part, and the parts keep their order.

That sentence is exactly the resolution your intuition was reaching for. Wilt before Jordan is never violated; succession is never lost. And at the same time, this moment, this one, listening, is not a sliver cut off from the whole story. It is the whole story, present the way a chord is present in each of its notes. This is also how the tradition explains the Buddha's knowledge of past and future: not as time travel, but as the total present being legible to a fully unobstructed mind. The game with no film is gone. And the game with no film is right here, folded into everything it caused, which includes you, hearing this, now.

Chapter seven. Dogen: being time.

Now the master who said all of this better than anyone. Eihei Dogen, thirteenth century Japan, founder of the Soto Zen lineage, and his essay Uji, usually translated The Time Being, written on the first day of winter, 1240, at Kosho Horin monastery. Uji opens with a verse from the old master Yaoshan, who builds everything on one small phrase, for the time being. For the time being, standing on the highest peak. For the time being, walking the bottom of the deepest ocean. For the time being, three heads and eight arms. For the time being, the golden body of the Buddha. For the time being, a staff, a pillar, a lantern. For the time being, the earth and sky.

Dogen reads the little phrase for the time being like a safe cracker. In Japanese it is uji, aru toki, at a certain time. His reading, in the Tanahashi translation: time itself is being, and all being is time. Everything that exists is not in time. Everything that exists is time.

Follow him as he turns it. Mountains are time. Oceans are time. If they were not time, there would be no mountains or oceans. Time is not the stage on which mountains happen. The mountain is what time looks like from here. And then the line that dismantles the image everyone carries, in the Kapleau translation: do not regard time as merely flying away; do not think that flying away is its sole function. For time to fly away, there would have to be a separation between it and things. You picture time as a river carrying events, but there is no riverbank and no river, only the events, each complete, each itself time. As long as you imagine that time only passes, you never learn what being time means.

Then he does something no one else dared. Time passes, he says, from today to tomorrow, from today to yesterday, from yesterday to today, from today to today, from tomorrow to tomorrow. Not a one way conveyor. Each direction of passage is real as passage, each moment a complete world.

And here is where it stops being metaphysics and becomes your life. Because being is time, Dogen says, I am my being time. You do not have time. You are not running out of time, because running out would require you to be one thing and time another. You are time, taking this shape: sitting, listening, remembering, aging. The time I climbed the mountain, he writes, is the present moment of being time. What seems to belong to the past is of the present.

Apply it to Hershey. Your knowledge of the hundred point game is not a faded copy of a lost event, exiled to an unreal past. It is this moment's being time, carrying 1962 inside it, as real as anything you will touch today. And practice enlightenment, Dogen says, is time too. Which means awakening is not escape from time into some frozen eternity. It is time, fully inhabited, each moment complete, pine as time, bamboo as time. You do not defeat time. You stop imagining you are separate from it, and the imagined gap was the whole suffering.

Chapter eight. Kalachakra: the wheel of time.

Now zoom out to the biggest Buddhist machine for thinking about time, the Kalachakra, literally the wheel of time, the great tantra of cycles. Its teaching comes in three wheels. The outer wheel of time is the world: the procession of solar and lunar days, seasons, the vast cycles by which universes arise and dissolve. Time as cosmos, the clock written in the sky. The inner wheel of time is the body: the channels, the winds, the pulse of breath, the body's own cycles of birth, decay, and death. You carry a clock in your ribs, and it is counting.

The brilliance is in the claim that the two wheels mirror each other. The cosmos out there and the body in here run on matching gears. Your breath is a calendar. Your life is a season. This is why, in this system, ordinary beings are not merely in time. They are driven by it, wound by it, born and dying on schedule like clockwork toys.

The third wheel is the point. The other wheel of time is the path: the initiations and meditations that unwind the first two. The wheel keeps turning for the world and for the body, but the practitioner stops being turned by it. The goal, in this system's own language, is the state beyond time, the primordial buddha, the adibuddha, which is not a being who lives very long but the dimension that time never touched.

Notice the shift of register across our answers. Abhidharma analyzed time. Dharmakirti made it causal. Nagarjuna emptied it. Huayan enfolded it. Dogen identified you with it. And now tantra treats time as the machinery of samsara itself, something to be liberated from, not by destroying the wheel but by no longer mistaking yourself for a cog. Stay view level here; the techniques belong with a qualified teacher. The view alone is already enormous: time is not just a concept. It is the conveyor of birth and death, and there is a stance outside its pull.

Chapter nine. Dzogchen: the fourth time.

And then the highest teaching says the quietest and strangest thing of all. The Dzogchen tradition speaks of four times. Past, present, future, and a fourth. Longchenpa, the great fourteenth century systematizer, names it in his own voice: the three times and timeless time is Kuntu Zangpo time, the originally accomplished and changeless state. The fourth time is called no time, timeless time, Samantabhadra time. Not a fourth segment on the line. The openness in which the line appears.

First, the three times are put through the examination one final time, now as direct introduction rather than argument. Longchenpa's instruction on the mind: past, it has ceased. Future, it has not yet come. Present, it does not linger. Look for the mind anywhere in the three times and you find nothing graspable, neither within nor without, like the sky, immune to every proposition. And then the turn. It is not in the has been, nor on the side of the not yet. It is not in a now, but is a state naturally remaining identical with itself. Instead of seeking mind by mind, let be.

Two things about the fourth time must be said precisely, because this is where people drift into fog. First, timeless time is not a frozen eternity, not a permanent now stretched wide. The tradition insists the fourth time is as dynamic as the three. It is not the pause between moments. It is the nature of every moment, the way wetness is the nature of every wave. You never leave the three times to reach it. The three times were never anything other than it, playing.

Second, and this returns us to your question with everything gathered. Dzogchen does not deny sequence. Nothing in the recognition of the fourth time unhappens Hershey, unreorders Wilt and Jordan, or makes karma a fiction. What it dissolves is the searcher's last hope of finding time, or mind, or self, as a thing, anywhere, in any of the three times. The search fails. And the failure is the finding. What remains when seeking stops is not nothing. It is the open, knowing expanse in which games are played, tapes are kept, and dharma is heard, none of it outside the timeless, none of it reduced by it. One caveat, as always: this is view level, from published sources. The introduction itself comes from a living master, not from a podcast, this one included.

Chapter ten. The Western witnesses.

Now for the conversation your question already implied: what did the West find, walking the same road? First witness, the Cambridge philosopher J. M. E. McTaggart, 1908, a paper called The Unreality of Time, still the most argued about essay on time in English. McTaggart separates two ways events are ordered. The A series: past, present, future, where every event keeps changing its label, future becomes present becomes past. The B series: earlier and later, fixed forever. Wilt's game is earlier than Jordan's debut, and that never changes, that is B series. That the game was future, then present, then is now long past, that is A series.

McTaggart argues the A series is contradictory, every event would have to be all three, and that the B series alone is not really time, because without a moving now nothing truly changes. His verdict: time is unreal. Sound familiar? He walked, alone, to one edge of Nagarjuna's result. The Buddhist answer is complete where his is half. The A series is contradictory if you demand it be inherent, because it is standpoint dependent: past means before here, and here moves. But as convention, as dependent designation, it works flawlessly. And the B series, mere fixed sequence, turns out to be exactly the part that survives: causal order. McTaggart found time is not a substance and called it unreal. The Middle Way found the same and called it empty, workable, and liberating.

Second witness, Edmund Husserl, lecturing at Gottingen in 1905 on the consciousness of internal time, with one humble example that does all the work: a melody. At any instant only one note sounds. Yet you hear a tune, not disconnected beeps. If the present were truly a knife edge, music would be impossible. So the living present must have width. Husserl mapped it: the note sounding now arrives in primal impression; the note just gone is still held, not re imagined but held as just past, in what he calls retention, trailing off like a comet's tail; and the note to come is already leant toward, in protention. The present is not a point. It is a little wave with a wake and a bow.

Compare what we already have. Retention is almost exactly the Abhidharma's trace, observed from the inside: the past persisting precisely as present structure, not as a visited elsewhere. And the widened present is what any meditator verifies: watch closely and the razor thin now is a fiction; experience arrives with its just past still ringing. Husserl needed years of lectures to see it. Your own next song will show you in four minutes.

Third witness, Henri Bergson, who spent a career defending one word: duration, la duree, time as actually lived, qualitative, interpenetrating, uncountable, against the clock's time, which he said is space in disguise, moments laid side by side like beads. In April 1922 in Paris, Bergson met Einstein face to face. Bergson opened graciously, I came here to listen, and then pressed his case: the physicist's time, measured, relative, plural, is not the whole of time; there is also the single lived stream each of us is. The crowd thought Einstein won. History is still arguing.

The Buddhist position, assembled from our chapters, lands with eerie precision between them. With Bergson: lived time is real, primary, and irreducible, and no clock will ever capture it. Against Bergson, gently: even duration, examined closely, is a construction over moments, and its seamlessness is exactly the kind of illusion analysis dissolves. With Einstein's physics: no universal now exists anywhere. Against reducing everything to the physics: the question of what it is like to be a being in time is not answered by any equation. Each side held one truth. The two truths hold both.

Chapter eleven. Physics, honestly.

Now physics, and the rule for this chapter is honesty, because this is where dharma talks go off the rails. Newton gave the modern world its default picture in the Principia: absolute, true, and mathematical time, of itself and from its own nature, flows equably without relation to anything external. A universal river, everywhere the same. That is time as substance, precisely the thing every Buddhist school we studied says does not exist.

Twentieth century physics dismantled Newton's river from two directions. Relativity first: simultaneity is frame relative. Two events that are simultaneous for one observer happen in sequence for another moving observer, and no observer is wrong. There is no universal now spread across the cosmos. Take that seriously and the block universe follows for many interpreters: all events, laid out in four dimensional spacetime, with the flow of time as something like a local perspective rather than a cosmic process. Be careful though, and here honesty starts: the block universe is an interpretation of the mathematics, not an experiment anyone ran. Good physicists disagree about it to this day.

Second direction: the arrow. The fundamental laws work nearly as well backward as forward, yet eggs unscramble never, and you remember 1962 but not 2162. Where does the asymmetry live? The leading answer is thermodynamics: entropy rises, and the universe began, for reasons still debated, in an extraordinarily low entropy state. Every trace, every record, every memory, exists because of that gradient. Notice what that means, it is quietly astonishing. Memory is a thermodynamic phenomenon. The reason the past is knowable and the future is not is written in the statistics of heat. Dharmakirti's traces turn out to have a physics department.

Then quantum gravity, where time gets genuinely weird. When Wheeler and DeWitt quantized general relativity in the nineteen sixties, the resulting equation, the candidate law of the whole universe, contains no time variable at all. The universe, at that level, does not evolve. It simply is. Physicists call this the problem of time, and it is unsolved. Page and Wootters proposed in 1983 that time emerges from entanglement: clocks inside the universe read off correlations between subsystems; only the outside view, which nobody has, is timeless. And Carlo Rovelli has spent a career arguing, his essay is titled Forget time, that mechanics is fundamentally a theory of relations between variables, not of evolution in time, and that flow, the feeling of time passing, emerges thermodynamically, approximately, at the level of blurred description.

Now the honest reckoning, three points, no spin. One. Physics does not say time is an illusion. It says time is not a universal container: no absolute flow, no cosmic now, and at the deepest equations, possibly no time variable, only relations and sequence. Two. The resonance with Buddhist philosophy is real and worth saying out loud: no time substance, relations all the way down, sequence without a flowing river, the present as standpoint rather than stage. When a Sautrantika hears there is no container of time, only ordered events, she is on familiar ground. Three. The disanalogy matters just as much. Physics is the study of measurement. Buddhism is the study of experience and the end of suffering. No experiment can test Nagarjuna, and no meditation can fix a Hamiltonian. Anyone who tells you quantum mechanics proves the dharma, or the dharma predicts quantum mechanics, is selling something. The two traditions converge on the negative claim and part ways on what to do with your one wild and precious morning.

Conclusion. The game still counts.

Come back to Hershey one last time. March 2, 1962. No film, no video, only a photograph, a fourth quarter radio call sitting in the Library of Congress, a line in the record books, and, for a while longer, living memory. Every Buddhist answer we walked fits that game like a glove. The Abhidharma: the game was a stream of moments, each one real exactly once, each handing causation to the next. Sarvastivada's Vasumitra: Wilt before Jordan is a position in the order, as fixed as a rod in the tens place. Dharmakirti: the game does not exist now, and your knowledge of it is not defective for that, because traces are present effects of real causes, and that is all the past has ever been for anyone. Vasubandhu: your experienced time is constructed, which is why it can be transformed. Nagarjuna: time is not findable as a thing, and precisely because it is empty, the sequence works, and whoever says so time does not exist, as if that settled anything, has read half the book.

Huayan: the whole story is folded into this instant without disturbing the order of a single frame. Dogen: you are not in time and you do not have time; you are time, and your remembering of the game is 1962 present as now. Kalachakra: the wheels turn, cosmos and body, and there is a stance they cannot wind. Dzogchen: beneath, within, and as every one of these moments, the fourth time, timeless time, open and dynamic, never once interrupted by all this playing.

So here is the whole course in one breath. There is no time substance. Not in Abhidharma, not in Madhyamaka, not in Zen, not in the tantras, and, it turns out, not in the deepest physics either. And sequence is fully real: causal order, traces, karma, Wilt before Jordan, your whole biography, none of it threatened by the emptiness of time, all of it enabled by that emptiness. The error is never noticing one of the two truths. Reify time and you build a prison of clocks and dread. Erase time and you build a fog where nothing matters and no one is responsible. The Middle Way, as always, is the working path: hold the sequence, drop the substance.

Which is what be here now finally means, in its grown up form. Not only the present exists, so forget the rest. But: the present is where the entire sequence lives, as trace and as tendency; it is the only place causation happens, the only place practice happens; and its own nature, examined, is the timeless itself, never elsewhere, never later. The game with no film is gone. The game with no film is here. Both are true, and you are the proof, listening now, carrying everything that brought you. May every being discover the time that needs no clock. This has been the Open Treasury.
