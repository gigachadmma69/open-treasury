Jhana the Brasington Way
Pleasure, Steadiness, and the Question of Depth.

The instruction sounds almost too simple. Settle the mind. Find a pleasant sensation. Give your attention to its pleasantness, without trying to make it stronger. Let the conditions do their work.

That is the centre of Leigh Brasington's approach to entering the first jhana. The simplicity is real. So is the preparation. This is not a trick in which any pleasant tingle becomes an attainment because we give it a Pali name.

This episode is an original practical explanation of Brasington's approach, associated with his book Right Concentration and his teacher Ayya Khema. We will explain how to begin, how the first four states develop in his presentation, and why teachers disagree about whether these are the same jhanas described by Pa-Auk and the commentarial tradition.

Keep breathing naturally, do not push through distress or sleep disruption, and if you are following a retreat programme, agree changes of method with your teacher.

One. Settle before you switch.

Brasington begins with an alert, comfortable posture. A chair is fine. The point is to find a position that does not keep demanding adjustments, without making yourself so comfortable that you fall asleep. Pain is not an admission ticket.

Choose an object and stay with it. In his breath instructions, he uses the physical sensations near the nostrils. Find a place where the breathing is apparent and stop moving attention around looking for a better place. Notice the breathing that is already occurring. When distracted, recognize the distraction, relax, and return.

Counting can be a support in his access-concentration instructions. He suggests counting the gaps between breaths up to eight, then starting again. It is optional scaffolding, not a ritual that forces the next stage to happen. The eventual task is to stay with the object rather than with a running commentary about your performance.

His sign of readiness is sustained non-distraction. You are with the object. A thought may remain faintly in the background, but it does not carry you into a planning session or an argument. That is what he calls access concentration. We will keep his meaning attached to his name, because another tradition can use the same term with a different threshold.

Do not switch to pleasure the instant a little calm appears. His book excerpt stresses establishing and sustaining the concentration first. Equally, do not spend the sitting waiting for an official announcement from the mind. The practical question is whether you are steadily present or repeatedly being pulled away.

The breath can feel subtle as the mind settles. Brasington discusses this as one possible sign. It is not an instruction to make breathing shallow, stop breathing, or ignore an actual breathing problem. Our task is attention, not manipulating oxygen.

Two. Change the object, not the amount of force.

Once that steadiness is established, Brasington shifts attention to a pleasant sensation, preferably a physical one. Hands are a common example in his teaching. The faint pleasantness of a relaxed smile can work for some people. Warmth around the chest is another possibility, especially after loving-kindness practice.

The location is not sacred. What matters is that something is genuinely pleasant and that you can stay with it. This is not a long body search in which every sensation is inspected and rejected. If no workable pleasantness is apparent and the mind is getting busy, go back to settling rather than manufacture a success story.

Now make the shift cleanly. The breath was the concentration object; pleasantness becomes the concentration object. You are not trying to monitor both at once, score the experience, and operate an imaginary intensity dial.

What do you attend to? The felt quality of pleasantness. Not a theory of pleasure. Not the location as a set of coordinates. Not the question of whether it is stronger than thirty seconds ago. Rest with what makes the sensation pleasant.

That distinction is the heart of the method. A hand may tingle, but tingling is not necessarily pleasant. A sensation may be intense, but intensity is not the point. You are interested in the pleasant aspect, with enough steadiness to remain there and enough ease not to crush it.

Three. Let it gather, without standing over it.

Brasington describes the pleasantness developing into piti and sukha. In his teaching, piti has a prominent energetic or bodily quality, accompanied by mental exhilaration; sukha is the joy or happiness with it. These are teaching distinctions, not a claim that each Pali word has only one meaning in every text.

The experience may develop unevenly. There may be some increase, a plateau, then more. Your job is not to supply encouragement every time it changes. The moment you lean forward mentally and start asking whether this is finally it, attention has moved from enjoyment into evaluation and wanting.

Think of listening closely to music you like. You can let the music fill attention without continually asking how much enjoyment you have achieved. That is our analogy, not Brasington's quotation. The important feature is receptive steadiness, not passivity and not excitement about winning.

In his account, first jhana involves sustained, undistracted immersion in piti and sukha. A passing shiver or isolated burst of energy is not enough. His book excerpt explicitly distinguishes brief or minor forms of piti from the pervasive, sustained piti he identifies with this first state.

So do not confuse bootstrapping pleasantness with proving an attainment. Pleasantness can become a useful object without immediately turning into first jhana. And strong energy without stability or happiness does not automatically satisfy even Brasington's own description.

If it grows strong, stay interested in steadiness rather than maximum voltage. His method is not a contest to endure the most intense sensation. The direction of the later states is toward refinement, not endless escalation.

Four. From excitement to happiness to stillness.

Brasington's short outline describes the move to second jhana as a change of emphasis from the intense physical sensations toward the accompanying emotional happiness. The energetic side moves into the background; the joy or happiness becomes the focus.

For the third, that happiness becomes quieter, more like contentment or satisfaction. For the fourth, even the pleasure of that contentment is let go, leaving stillness and equanimity.

This is a map, not an instruction to imitate four moods in four minutes. The earlier state needs enough stability that a change of emphasis is something you can actually make, rather than a new story being told over a wandering mind.

There are also four immaterial attainments in the wider eight-state presentation: boundless space, boundless consciousness, nothingness, and neither perception nor non-perception. Brasington gives further instructions for these. We are naming them to complete the broad map, not compressing their training into a few sentences or making them a requirement for trying the first-state method.

The useful arc for this short episode is already clear. Establish attention. Attend to pleasantness. Allow sustained rapture and happiness. Refine toward quieter happiness, contentment, and equanimity. Afterward, the collected mind can support insight; the pleasant state is not presented as awakening itself.

Five. Are these jhanas legitimate?

There are several questions hiding inside that word. Does the method describe a serious, trainable meditative experience? Does that experience meet a particular teacher's threshold for jhana? Does it reproduce exactly what the earliest Buddhist texts intended? Those are different questions.

Brasington offers a coherent practice account with explicit criteria. Dismissing the whole thing as fake because it is more accessible than a stricter absorption standard does not answer that account. But the reverse leap also fails: usefulness does not prove that two standards describe the same state.

Look at the concrete difference. Brasington allows faint background thought that does not distract in his first-jhana account, and his entry method shifts toward pleasantness. In Pa-Auk's breathing instructions in Knowing and Seeing, absorption is described as uninterrupted knowing of the counterpart sign, followed by examining jhana factors and developing mastery. The Visuddhimagga distinguishes access from absorption by the strength of the factors and the stability of the concentrated process.

Those are different training descriptions and assessment criteria. A state meeting Brasington's description is not, on that basis alone, evidence that Pa-Auk would certify it as absorption. A state falling short of Pa-Auk's threshold is not, on that basis alone, worthless concentration.

The historical question is harder. Brasington argues for a distinction between more accessible sutta jhanas and deeper commentarial jhanas. That is his interpretation, not a settled scholarly verdict. Bhikkhu Analayo has published a detailed critique of attempts to establish a sharp break between early absorption and later exegesis, including the research on which such claims have relied.

The early formula itself describes seclusion from sensuality and unskillful states, rapture and pleasure, then progressively quieter concentration and equanimity. Interpreters disagree about how deeply withdrawn these states are and how to understand the mental operations named in the first jhana. Quoting the formula does not automatically settle those disputes.

The honest conclusion is neither all versions are identical nor only my preferred teacher has a real experience. Name the standard. Describe the experience. Keep practical benefit separate from historical certainty. Then the disagreement can teach us something instead of becoming a fight over a badge.

Six. A workable experiment.

For a session devoted to this method, begin with comfortable alertness and a single object. Return gently until the object is stable and distractions no longer take over. When that stability is established, shift to a real pleasant sensation and stay with its pleasant quality. Do not chase increases, narrate the process, or alternate restlessly between methods.

If the mind is not ready, settling is the practice. If pleasure is present but does not develop, staying steadily with it is still the task. Afterward, review what actually happened: how stable was attention, what changed, and what interrupted it? That is more useful than forcing a technical label onto the result.

And if another session is assigned to white kasina, let it be a white-kasina session. The objects in Brasington's pleasantness method and a kasina practice are not interchangeable simply because both may involve piti. Deliberately changing methods between sessions is different from mixing them moment by moment.

The most practical instruction is also the easiest one to miss. Build the conditions, then stop trying to supervise the payoff. The mind is learning to enjoy staying, not learning to demand a reward for staying. That is the Brasington doorway: steadiness first, pleasantness next, and less interference than you think.
