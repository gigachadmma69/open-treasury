Chapter 1. A raven at the table.

"Why is a raven like a writing-desk?"

You probably know the question. You may even know an answer. Hold it for a moment. We are interested first in what the question asks someone to do.

In Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, the Hatter puts it to Alice at the tea table. Alice is pleased. At last, perhaps, a game she understands: someone asks a riddle, someone else thinks, and there is an answer worth finding.

Eventually she gives up and asks for the solution.

"I haven't the slightest idea," says the Hatter.

"Nor I," says the March Hare.

The structure has collapsed. Alice has been working toward a destination her hosts cannot supply.

We will not settle every answer later readers have invented, or retell the riddle's entire publication history. In this scene, the answer is not given. That is enough to expose a question hiding inside every puzzle: what has the questioner promised the person who tries?

A riddle can be an invitation to notice something. It can also be a little arrangement of power. I know; you do not. I decide when you have failed. I may even change what counts as success after you begin.

But that is not the only possibility. A riddle can give an ordinary thing a voice so strange that recognizing it becomes a second encounter, not merely a correct label.

We are going to read a few examples from the Exeter Book through Benjamin Thorpe's 1842 edition and translation. We will keep the age and limits of that translation visible. Then we will return to Alice's unsatisfactory tea party and ask what makes uncertainty playful rather than cruel.

This is a small literary experiment. We will not solve every disputed Old English poem. We will not pretend that an unknown author has left us an answer sheet behind the curtain.

Our hypothesis is more interesting, I think, than the claim that riddles make us clever. A good riddle may let us stop being the person who already knows what a thing is.

For a few lines, an object speaks and we have to listen without its usual name doing all the work.

Then the answer arrives.

What if that is where the real reading begins?

Chapter 2. A book with questions still inside.

The Exeter Book is a surviving manuscript of Old English poetry, kept at Exeter Cathedral. The Cathedral dates its copying to about 970 and says Bishop Leofric gave it to the Cathedral at his death in 1072.

Those dates concern the manuscript's copying and custody. They do not give us an exact composition date for every poem it contains.

The University of Exeter describes the collection's riddles as virtually all those that survive in Old English. It also notes that some remain unsolved. We should hear both parts. The material is extraordinarily important, and not completely mastered.

A manuscript can be preserved without all its questions being closed.

That is a useful beginning for this course because age can make uncertainty embarrassing. We might expect that after so many centuries someone should have worked everything out. But surviving text is not the same as surviving context. A damaged line, an unfamiliar word, a lost association, an editor's division between poems: any of these can affect what we think we are reading.

The University and Cathedral now make the manuscript available digitally. That is an invitation to look, not a promise that looking will immediately make Old English transparent.

We are approaching selected poems through a historical English translation, which adds another layer. Thorpe's book prints Old English alongside his rendering. His preface is unusually candid. He calls his versions "translations, or rather attempts at translation."

He admits that some are almost, or even quite, as unintelligible as the originals seem to him. He hopes someone more competent will do better.

That confession is part of our source. We should not quote the confident-looking English column and quietly discard the uncertainty announced at the front.

Nor should we conclude that because a translation has limits, any interpretation is as good as any other. Words still constrain us. An image still has a shape. Scholarship can improve a reading. Humility is not permission to make the poem say whatever would suit our conclusion.

Our working agreement, then: say which edition we use, quote only what we have checked, distinguish a common solution from a certain original intention, and leave a difficult line difficult when we cannot responsibly repair it.

That sounds restrained. It also leaves room for surprise.

A question does not stop being alive because someone has cared for it carefully.

Chapter 3. A moth ate words.

Our first poem begins with a revelation rather than a concealment.

"A moth ate words."

Those are Thorpe's opening words for the poem numbered forty-eight in his edition. Riddle numbering varies, so the opening phrase is a useful way to identify it without pretending every edition agrees on the number.

The poem describes a creature consuming someone's speech in the material form that carries it. Near the end, Thorpe writes:

"The stealing guest was not
in aught the wiser,
who the words had swallow'd."

The body has taken in the words. Understanding has not followed.

This is often called the bookworm riddle. But simply naming the creature does not exhaust its joke, especially when the translation has already named a moth at the beginning.

The poem makes eating and reading approach each other, then refuses to let them become the same act.

There is a literal event in its imagined world: material bearing language can be consumed by a small creature. And there is a comparison waiting for a reader who has ever collected words without doing much with them.

That comparison is our reading. We do not need to claim the anonymous poet intended a criticism of our particular reading habits.

Still, it lands. I can have a sentence in my possession without knowing what it means. I can repeat it beautifully without noticing whether it fits the occasion. I can carry a library's worth of impressive language and remain unaltered in the place the language was meant to reach.

This does not make memorization worthless. Remembering can keep a line available until understanding catches up. It does not make pleasure in sound shallow either. A poem can matter before we can explain why.

The error would be to treat possession as proof of comprehension.

A moth has found a way into the book. The reader has to find a different way.

And the little creature protects the poem from becoming merely a schoolroom instruction. It is funny, slightly indignant, and materially specific. It does not say, be wiser, in a voice above the world. It gives us a thief so small that the stolen treasure has become dinner.

Once you know the answer, look again. The puzzle opens into another puzzle.

What would count as having received the words rather than merely having swallowed them?

Chapter 4. The book speaks back.

Now let the damaged book have a voice.

Another Exeter poem, numbered twenty-seven by Thorpe, begins with a speaker deprived of life by an enemy. It is wetted, placed in the sun, stripped of hair. Later it is marked and adorned, with gold among its ornaments.

The customary reading is a sacred book made from animal skin. A later historical anthology edited by Albert Cook and Chauncey Tinker labels the poem "The Bible-Codex." We are using that as an interpretation label, not pretending it is the anonymous poet's own title.

Thorpe's translation is difficult in parts. We will not smooth every obscure phrase into a confident reconstruction of a workshop. The opening transformation and the later ornament are enough for our question.

What happens when a book tells its history from inside its material?

A familiar object becomes unfamiliar. A finished page does not usually begin by announcing that something living was killed. Binding and decoration do not usually introduce themselves as events happening to a speaking body.

The poem's first-person voice changes where we stand.

In our reading, the object is no longer only what a reader uses. It has undergone a sequence before reaching the reader's hands. The riddle makes that sequence strange enough to notice.

We must be careful with the ethical conclusion. Giving a thing a voice in a poem does not prove that the thing literally feels as the poem speaks. Nor can we claim the poet shared every modern concern about animals, craft, consumption, or labor.

We can say what the literary device permits us to notice now.

The answer, book, should not silence the earlier voice. If anything, it makes the transformation more vivid. This was not an unrelated victim followed by a convenient object. The poem asks us to hold those perspectives together.

Beside the moth poem, it becomes a remarkable pair. One creature takes words into its body. Another body becomes something that can carry words.

That pairing is our synthesis, not proof that the manuscript's arranger planned this course. But it lets us hear material and meaning touching without becoming identical.

The page can preserve speech. The moth can destroy the page without acquiring the speech. The riddle can make us reconsider the page without settling every question about the speech.

A very small set of poems has opened a surprisingly large room.

Stay there before rushing to the next answer.

Chapter 5. Who owns the solution?.

We now need to distinguish three things that can look like the same thing on a printed page.

The words of the poem. The translator's choices. The editor's proposed answer or title.

All may be useful. They are not identical kinds of evidence.

A modern-looking heading can make an interpretation feel as though it has always been there. A smooth translation can conceal an uncertain word. A familiar answer can make a stubborn line disappear from our attention.

The problem is not editing. Without editors and translators many readers, including the listener imagined by this course, would have much less access to these works. The problem is forgetting which help we have received.

Thorpe's preface gives us a good model of saying what remains unfinished. He does not make the uncertainty disappear by printing a column of English beside the original.

We can carry that discipline into our own reading. If someone proposes a solution, ask which details it explains. Then ask which details remain awkward.

That second question need not be hostile. A strong interpretation can admit a residue. It may be the best available account without being a perfect key.

And a clever alternative is not established merely because it surprises people. Surprise is a pleasure, not a proof.

There are also different kinds of openness. Sometimes two answers are both well supported. Sometimes a difficult passage has one likely answer and several entertaining guesses. Sometimes the evidence is too thin to rank them responsibly. We do not honor ambiguity by calling all three situations the same.

The phrase I do not know can mean the investigation is unfinished. It need not mean that there is nothing to investigate.

This matters because uncertainty can be used in two opposing ways. A gatekeeper can pretend certainty in order to exclude other readers. A performer can pretend endless uncertainty in order to avoid any check on a dazzling claim.

Neither is the openness we want.

A generous riddle has resistance. It lets a proposed answer meet the actual words. It does not become whatever the most confident voice in the room announces.

So who owns the solution?

In our practice, no reader gets ownership strong enough to stop the next reader looking. Scholarship deserves credit. Evidence deserves attention. Neither requires a promise that no one will ever ask again.

The answer can be shared without becoming a locked door.

Chapter 6. The Hatter's contract.

Return to the tea table.

Alice hears a question shaped like a solvable riddle. She begins to work on it. The Hatter later admits he has no idea of the answer.

Why is she annoyed?

Not simply because she dislikes uncertainty. Earlier she is pleased at the prospect of the puzzle. The difficulty looked like part of an activity whose terms she recognized.

My reading is that the hosts have failed to share the terms of the game. Alice thought she was being asked to discover something. The questioner was not in a position to tell her whether she had found it.

An unanswered riddle can still be delightful if everyone knows that is the invitation. Invent as many answers as you can. Find the strangest resemblance. We are making something, not recovering the one solution I possess.

But that is a different agreement from I have a puzzle for you, see if you can solve it.

The difference lies partly in what happens to failure. In a cooperative invention, an unlikely answer can become new material. In a test with a known answer, it can be wrong in an informative way. In an unannounced impossible test, the participant may spend effort trying to satisfy an authority whose standard does not exist.

Carroll's scene makes that absurd. We can enjoy the absurdity without copying the hospitality.

The tea table is already difficult before the riddle. Alice is told there is no room despite the table's size. Wine is offered when there is none. A personal remark about her hair arrives instead of a welcome.

The riddle belongs to a whole atmosphere in which language keeps withdrawing the ground it seemed to offer.

That does not make every joke a broken promise. Friends can enjoy nonsense, tease each other, and invent impossible questions when they share the pleasure. The test is not whether the words obey ordinary expectations. It is whether the people understand what they are being invited into and can stop participating.

Alice does not owe the table infinite patience simply because the table is amusing to us.

A reader can delight in a fictional situation while hoping a real child in it would be treated better.

That double vision is part of reading too.

The Hatter has given us a question he cannot answer. We can take the question away without taking his manners with it.

Chapter 7. The objection: ignorance in a fancy hat.

Now the objection.

Is this simply an elaborate defense of not knowing? We have uncertainty, ambiguous solutions, the value of another look. At some point, shouldn't somebody get the answer right?

Yes. When there is a good answer, getting it matters. When a factual question has consequences, a riddle's openness is not a substitute for checking.

A medicine dose is not improved by leaving its meaning poetically unresolved. A meeting place should not be treated as a creative ambiguity someone else must solve. Consent is not a hidden answer to be extracted from an unwilling person.

These are ordinary distinctions we bring to the course. Literary play does not suspend them.

The value of uncertainty depends on what is uncertain and what hangs on it. A safe puzzle can let us practice living briefly without a name. A practical obligation may require us to stop playing and ask a direct question.

And riddles can be used cruelly. A person can hoard a solution to enjoy another person's frustration. A host can make not knowing into public humiliation. A teacher can use a tricky wording to measure who already knows the trick rather than who understands the subject.

We need not pretend those are harmless because they involve words.

A fair invitation allows someone to say, I would like a hint. Or, tell me. Or, I am done. No declaration of personal inadequacy should be required.

There is also a subtler objection to our material. The moth comparison could become an insult to people who learn differently, need repetition, or do not show understanding in a form someone else expects.

We should not use it that way. A reader's speed, memory, education, language background, or manner of answering is not a measure of their worth. The poem offers an image for our own reflection; it is not permission to call another learner a worm.

Likewise, the speaking book does not give us a complete ethical system about every material object. It changes a perspective. We still have to reason about consequences and responsibilities.

A riddle can loosen an assumption. It cannot do all our thinking afterward.

That is the answer to the objection. We are not giving ignorance a fancy hat and calling it wisdom. We are making room for a precise kind of not-yet: attention held open long enough for a thing to be seen differently.

Then we owe it the work of looking.

Chapter 8. After the click.

There is a recognizable pleasure when a solution arrives. The scattered details suddenly belong together. What looked contradictory becomes a set of clues.

We do not need a theory of the brain to enjoy that change. We can simply notice what a good answer does to the lines already there.

Before the solution, each description can feel like a separate demand. Afterward, the same description points somewhere. We read backward as well as forward.

But the click can become a stopping signal. Got it. Next.

That is the habit this course wants to interrupt.

Try reading the moth poem again after you have named the bookworm. The object is no longer a mystery. Yet the gap between consumption and understanding has not become less interesting. Knowing the answer has made the image available for thought.

Try the book poem after you have accepted the customary solution. The language of injury does not become irrelevant packaging. It now describes the cost and transformation hidden inside an object you can hold.

The answer does not replace the poem. It changes your route through it.

This is our interpretive proposal. Some riddles are mostly enjoyed for the solution, and there is nothing wrong with a quick joke. Not every puzzle owes us a lifetime of meditation.

But a poem that survives the answer offers another kind of pleasure: recognition that does not use up attention.

You can even discover that the answer you first accepted leaves something unexplained. That is not always a disappointment. It can be the point at which reading resumes.

There is a risk here too. We should not keep a weak claim alive merely because surrendering it would end the fun. Open attention is not a duty to preserve every speculation.

Sometimes looking again confirms the solution. Sometimes it improves it. Sometimes it sends it back to the workshop.

All three can be satisfying if the purpose is to meet the work rather than merely to win against another reader.

The best question after I know what it is may be, what have I still not noticed?

That question is not limited to ancient texts. A familiar object, a word used every day, a task dismissed as simple: any of them may become less obvious if we let its usual label stop speaking for a moment.

The answer can return afterward. It need not return alone.

Chapter 9. Make a small speaking thing.

Our optional exercise is to write a riddle, not to put someone else through one.

Choose an ordinary object near you. Nothing private, dangerous, or attached to a person who has not agreed to be part of the game. A cup, a key, a shoe, a folded cloth. No special object is required.

Write three short sentences in its voice without naming it.

In the first, describe what it does in a way that sounds like something else. In the second, describe something done to it. In the third, describe a relation it has with people.

These are our invented instructions, not a reconstruction of Old English poetic practice. You do not need alliteration, a medieval voice, or a claim of authenticity.

Then write the answer separately.

Read the three sentences with the answer in mind. Do they still tell you anything worth noticing? If not, there is no failure. You may have made a perfectly good quick puzzle. If you want the longer pleasure, revise one sentence so it makes the familiar object newly specific rather than merely harder to guess.

Here is a deliberately simple example, written for this course. I hold what you cannot keep in your hands. I am lifted when you want what I carry. You notice my absence most when you spill.

A cup is a plausible answer. A bowl may fit too. That is useful information about the riddle, not a reason to blame someone for an alternative.

If you want only cup, add a detail that distinguishes it. If you enjoy both, say the game has more than one answer. Do not pretend the participant should have read the answer you wrote down in private.

And if you share the riddle, explain the terms. Do you know a solution? Are you inviting invention? Are hints welcome? Is the other person interested at all?

The purpose is shared attention, not a verdict on anyone's intelligence.

Finally, put the answer back beside the object. Notice whether the object has changed for you, even slightly. You may see a curve, a worn place, a history of hands. Or you may simply see a cup and want tea.

That is allowed.

A game that offers another look should not punish someone for being ready to stop looking.

Chapter 10. Leave the words uneaten.

We began at a table where a question promised an answer and the hosts could not provide one.

We found another kind of question in an old manuscript: a thing speaking from a position its ordinary name does not quite prepare us to hear.

The moth consumes words without becoming wiser. The animal skin becomes a book that speaks of what happened to it. A translator admits that some passages defeat him. A reader proposes a solution, then returns to the lines to see what the solution has changed.

These are not all the same lesson. They are several ways to distinguish having an answer from having finished paying attention.

Alice is right to want to know what game she has entered. Thorpe is right to show where his rendering is uncertain. A later reader is right to ask whether an old solution explains the words. And a tired participant is right to say, tell me, or let us do something else.

We can love a riddle without making uncertainty compulsory.

That may be its most generous use: a little interval in which not knowing is permitted, bounded by the knowledge that no one's dignity depends on producing the answer.

The reading is selective. Alice's tea party is in chapter seven of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland. The Exeter poems discussed here are identified by Thorpe's edition numbers and opening words in the companion ledger. The Cathedral and University of Exeter offer context and access to the surviving manuscript. Many other readings and translations exist; we have not settled them all.

Keep that limitation with the pleasure. It leaves room for another reader without pretending the existing work did not happen.

And when you next meet a sentence you want to collect, try the moth's question on yourself. What would it mean to receive this rather than merely possess it?

Perhaps you would explain it in your own words. Perhaps you would find a case where it does not apply. Perhaps you would let it change one small action. Perhaps you would discover that you do not understand it yet, and keep it available without performing certainty.

None of those responses needs an audience.

The moth ate words. We can do something else with them.

Let the answer arrive. Enjoy the click. Then give the thing one more look.

The answer is not the end of the game. It may be the first moment you can really see what you have been playing with.
