# The Morning After

Ten chapters, 25:11.27. Narrated by Tara using AI voice Aoede.

## Scope and sources

An original literary argument about temporary festive worlds, pleasure, work, power and what remains after the party. This reads particular English-language works, not a universal history of festivals. The retained brand is a literary metaphor, not instructions to handle or save hot coals. The critical description of Distaff sport is not a reenactment or endorsement. Fictional speakers are not automatically their authors' moral positions.

The primary-text sources are Robert Herrick's selected seasonal poems in Hesperides (nos. 892-894 and 1026), Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol (Staves II and V), and William Shakespeare's Twelfth Night (II.iii and V.i).

## Quote and claim ledger

### Chapter 1: warehouse transformation

Dickens, Stave II, approximately text lines 1440-1486. Exact Fezziwig fragment: 'No more work to-night.' Apprentices put up shutters; clear the room; sweep floor; trim lamps; heap fuel; fiddler occupies desk. Script's observation that the festival begins with work is original interpretation of those narrated actions. Fiction is expressly labeled fiction; no claim that this depicts every historical business.

### Chapter 2: ending the pie

Herrick No.894, UPON CANDLEMAS DAY, approximately lines 14931-14935:
'End now the white loaf and the pie, / And let all sports with Christmas die.' Entire two-line poem, exact words. Seasonal timing is contextualized through English Heritage, not used as a universal calendar. Herrick's No.1026 offers a different literary closing point after Twelfth Day; script flags that tension instead of harmonizing every poem into one fixed timetable.

### Chapter 3: succession of decorations

Herrick No.892, CEREMONIES FOR CANDLEMAS EVE, approximately lines 14887-14914. Quotes exact couplet 'Instead of holly, now up-raise / The greener box, for show' and ending 'Thus times do shift; each thing his turn does hold: / New things succeed, as former things grow old.' Paraphrased order: holly replaced by box, then yew, birch/flowers, rushes/oak. This is the poem's arrangement, not a claim that every English household followed it. 'A mood receives time and then yields it' is our reading, identified as such.

### Chapter 4: saved brand

Herrick No.893, THE CEREMONIES FOR CANDLEMAS DAY, approximately lines 14919-14929. Exact: 'Part must be kept wherewith to teend / The Christmas log next year.' Surrounding poem says burn until sunset, quench, lay up until next Christmas; promises protection from a fiend. Teend gloss 'kindle' appears in the nearby editorial gloss. Script explicitly does not endorse supernatural protection or recommend handling/storage of hot embers. 'Choose the ember' is our metaphor for selecting a beneficial consequence, not Herrick's ethical teaching.

### Chapter 5: Fezziwig's power

Dickens Stave II, approximately lines 1480-1595. Guests include employees and family/acquaintances; party ends eleven; hosts say goodbye individually; apprentices sleep under counter in back shop. Exact Scrooge quotation: 'He has the power to render us happy or unhappy; to make our service light or burdensome; a pleasure or a toil.' Attribution level: fictional character speech about Fezziwig, not Dickens speaking directly as a social scientist. Morgan manuscript corroborates the passage, with punctuation differences. Final spoken words follow Gutenberg consistently.

Interpretation: the employer remains an employer; generosity need not be dismissed but does not replace pay or fair institutions. This analysis is ours. The script does not invent historical wage conditions or a documented exploitation claim absent from the scene.

### Chapter 6: Saint Distaff

Herrick No.1026, SAINT DISTAFF'S DAY, OR THE MORROW AFTER TWELFTH DAY, approximately lines 16226-16247. Exact opening: 'Partly work and partly play / Ye must on S. Distaff's day.' The fire/flax/tow and water retaliation are paraphrases of the poem, not instructions. The closing sends people to their vocations the following day. Script labels the poem's gendered rough sport and does not assume consent, safety or universal historical practice. Fire reenactment is explicitly excluded. No claim is made that Saint Distaff was a canonized historical person.

### Chapter 7: cakes, ale, injury

Shakespeare, Twelfth Night II.iii, approximately lines 1480-1487. Exact Toby speech: 'Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?' Dramatic voice, not Shakespeare's unqualified personal doctrine.

V.i approximately lines 4450-4482: Malvolio says 'I'll be revenged on the whole pack of you.' Olivia says 'He hath been most notoriously abus'd.' ASCII apostrophe is typography normalization of source. The plot paraphrase is limited to deception/humiliation and Malvolio's departure. The course does not diagnose him or turn the entire play into a single moral thesis. Consent argument is our ethical interpretation.

### Chapter 8: practical follow-through

Dickens Stave V, approximately lines 3825-3870. Bob is promised a salary increase and help for his family. Exact Scrooge speech: 'Make up the fires, and buy another coal-scuttle before you dot another i, Bob Cratchit!' Narrator then says he was better than his word. 'Salary cannot be paid in sentiment' is original commentary. Personal generosity is expressly not a substitute for institutional rights or reliable compensation. No inference that every real employer will change after an emotional experience.

### Chapter 9: optional exercise

No autonomous invitations, spending, recordings or commitments are authorized. No therapeutic or social outcome guaranteed. Real fire excluded.

### Chapter 10: rain and end

Shakespeare V.i, final clown song, approximately lines 4495-4522. Exact refrain fragment: 'For the rain it raineth every day.' Final verse's play-ending/promise paraphrased, not quoted from a modern musical setting. The reading of weather as ordinary difficulty beyond performance is explicitly ours.
