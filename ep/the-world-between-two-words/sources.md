# The World Between Two Words

Vairotsana and the Courage to Translate

Source companion | The Open Treasury | 19 November 2026

An original 24-chapter course about translation as answerability: to a source, to a receiving community, and to the people whose understanding our words will shape. Vairotsana's remembered journeys and exile are placed beside collaborative translation, variant credits, revision, the funeral narrative in the Testimony of Ba tradition, and the ethics of receiving another person's words.

## How to read this course

The Tibetan translator Vairotsana must be distinguished from the Buddha Vairocana. English spellings vary. The course uses a broad imperial setting rather than asserting a precise birth or death date. Later sacred lives, layered historical narratives, canonical credits and modern scholarship are different kinds of evidence. A story can have religious and literary value without becoming an independently verified event.

This course is not a complete biography or a translation from Tibetan. It does not claim direct collation of Tibetan canonical witnesses, complete reading of the Great Image biography, or complete reading of the Tibetan corpus. The language and communication examples are original illustrations, not historical dialogue or reconstructed teachings by Vairotsana. Ethical applications are the course author's arguments.

No translated prayer or other source text is recited. There are no advanced tantric, breath, subtle-energy, visualization or initiation instructions. Chapter 23 offers only an ordinary reflection on the difference between another person's words and one's own inference. It is not a Great Perfection practice or a promise of realization.

## Claims and boundaries

### A remembered imperial translator, not a certified chronology

Traditional accounts locate Vairotsana in the Buddhist world of Trisong Detsen, Samye and the early translators. The course does not settle competing exact dates or harmonize all episodes of his sacred lives. One modern article gives a birth date that does not fit comfortably with the broader placement used in other sources. That number is not adopted. Miracles, night teaching, secret writing, exile and return are introduced as traditional narrative, not as facts independently established by contemporary records.

### Translation is collaborative

Raine's discussion supplies the common pattern of an Indian scholar and a Tibetan translator, sometimes with an additional reviser. It is not a universal staffing chart. The course does not invent a particular conversation, office or daily routine for Vairotsana. Organized vocabulary and revision belong to a wider institutional history and are not credited wholesale to one hero. Broad older claims that all translators were saints, all originals can be recovered, or all work ended completely at one political turning point are not repeated.

### A precise example of credit and revision

The introductions to 84000's The Prayer of Good Conduct and The Dwellings of Bodhisattvas discuss the credits attached to the larger Buddhavatamsaka collection. Both describe the Dege catalogue as naming Yeshe De, Jinamitra and Surendrabodhi, with Vairotsana as editor. Their descriptions of some other canonical colophons differ in whether they speak of translation attribution or editing. The course preserves that difference and uses the narrower shared point. It does not present a modern introduction as equivalent to independently examining each Tibetan witness.

### The funeral contest is not the Samye debate

The Account of Providing Food for the Dead is an appendix within a layered manuscript history. Its portrayal of Vairotsana's participation in a royal funeral dispute is the concrete case examined by Schaeffer. It is not the better-known Buddhist debate between gradual and sudden approaches to awakening. Its accusations against opponents are not treated as neutral descriptions of all Bon traditions. Nor does the course claim access to every historical participant's private motives.

### Composition is not the same as copying

Doney's analysis and the selected pages of Willis and Gonkatsang distinguish layers, additions, different endings and later recensions. An old story can be copied late; a later addition can become continuous running text. Dates proposed for the surviving manuscript do not automatically date every passage. Doney's discussion of an emperor regretting untranslated Chinese teachings concerns a literary coda, not a secure record of final words. His analysis of the blame for Padmasambhava's dismissal concerns changing portrayals across versions.

### A woman teaching the hero is not proof of general equality

The short Chudunma passage is an English translation of a later literary witness. Her identity and conversation are not independently reconstructed from the extract. The scene gives a woman religious authority and makes Vairotsana a listener. It does not, by itself, establish equal opportunities for all women in an entire historical society. Textual variants in the modern translator's notes affect ethically important wording. The course does not suppress those variants or turn nonduality into permission to harm.

## Sources and reading route

### 1. Kurtis R. Schaeffer, Cultural Brokers and Other Historiographic Metaphors

Full title: Cultural Brokers and Other Historiographic Metaphors: Perspectives from Indo-Tibetan Cultural History. Medieval Worlds 20, 2024, 109-125. DOI 10.1553/medievalworlds_no20_2024s109. Official journal PDF.

https://medievalworlds.net/0xc1aa5572_0x003f3182.pdf

Read: abstract and complete argument, printed 109-122, with acknowledgments 123. The bibliography was consulted selectively, not read as a separate body of research. Main uses: a limited and careful account of cultural brokers; Vairotsana in the funeral narrative; the difference between a rich literary portrayal and an unqualified report of historical events. The course's questions about power, dependency and ethical accountability are its own extensions, not direct quotations.
