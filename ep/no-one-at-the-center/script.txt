No One at the Center. Vasubandhu and the World That Mind Makes.

Chapter one. A world in four words.

A message arrives on your phone. Four words: "We need to talk."

Before you have asked a question, a world is already here. The room becomes smaller. Something tightens in the chest. You remember a conversation from yesterday. You hear an accusation that has not been made. Perhaps you begin preparing a defense against someone who only wanted to ask about dinner.

There are words on a screen. There is a body responding. There is a history. And there is the extraordinary speed with which all of this becomes: something is happening to me.

The Indian Buddhist philosopher Vasubandhu is interested in that speed. But if we reduce him to a teacher who says our thoughts affect our feelings, we miss almost everything difficult and wonderful about him. He asks what a person is. How actions leave consequences. How memory works without an unchanging owner. What we are entitled to claim about a world beyond experience. And what would have to change for suffering to stop reproducing itself.

His famous answer is associated with Yogacara, often called mind-only. That name has launched a thousand bad explanations. You create the universe. Other people are your projections. Nothing matters because everything is a dream.

Those are not conclusions we get to smuggle past him.

Vasubandhu argues about other minds because other beings matter. He analyzes conditioning because actions have consequences. His world includes rebirth, karma, meditation and the bodhisattva's commitment to awakening for the sake of others. We can question those commitments. We cannot quietly remove them and announce that he was doing modern psychology all along.

This course is an encounter with that larger world. We will enter the Treasury of Abhidharma before entering mind-only. We will hear the objections before enjoying the answers. And we will let an ancient argument remain unfinished where it really is unfinished.

Our question is simple enough to keep in a pocket. If there is no permanent owner behind experience, how can there still be a life to care for?

Not a trick question. The whole course turns on whether we can answer it without throwing away either half.

Chapter two. The person behind the legend.

We meet Vasubandhu through texts and through people remembering him. Those are different kinds of evidence.

He probably lived in the fourth or fifth century of the common era. His life is associated with northwestern South Asia, especially Gandhara, and with places farther east, including Ayodhya. Traditional biographies disagree about dates, teachers, journeys, the circumstances of his turn toward Mahayana, even where he died.

There has also been a scholarly debate about whether works bearing his name require more than one Vasubandhu. We do not need a dramatic verdict to begin. A single, perfectly documented career is not what survives. Nor does uncertainty make the surviving arguments disappear.

In the stories, he is brilliant, sharp, sometimes proud. His older brother Asanga is associated with Mahayana and Yogacara. Vasubandhu initially rejects that world, then becomes one of its great interpreters. In one conversion story, shame at his earlier criticism makes him want to damage the tongue that spoke it. His brother redirects the impulse: use the same capacity to teach.

That is a traditional story, not a recorded private conversation. Its interest is not an invitation to self-punishment. Its interest is the possibility that intelligence can change its allegiance without being destroyed. The instrument that made the mistake can become part of the repair.

Jonathan Gold's account of Vasubandhu in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats those disagreements as real constraints on what a biography can claim. Later communities remember him as a second Buddha. They tell stories of astonishing learning, immense numbers of works and overwhelming victories in debate. Such stories show the scale of his authority for those communities. They do not give us a license to treat every detail as contemporary history.

We will take a more demanding form of respect. We will neither flatten him into an infallible saint nor explain him away as a collection of legends. We will follow a body of work that moves among analysis, argument, interpretation and the path to freedom.

And we will resist a very tidy picture: first he believed one thing, then he advanced to the next thing, then he finally arrived where we already agree with him. Real thinkers are not escalators. His earlier work has its own depth. His later work does not make it disposable.

Chapter three. The intellectual city.

Imagine entering a city by its debating halls rather than by its monuments.

One argument concerns time. Another concerns whether a person is something beyond the changing body and mind. In another room, someone asks how a vow can still matter after the words have stopped. Someone else asks whether a visible object is directly perceived or known through a mental appearance.

These are some of the disputes Vasubandhu inherits. They are not all disputes between Buddhism and something else. Buddhists disagree with Buddhists, sometimes at tremendous length.

Sarvastivada is associated with the striking claim that dharmas exist across the three times: past, present and future. The position is more careful than the idea that everything happens at once. It is part of an attempt to explain causation, knowledge and the efficacy of past actions. Kashmiri Vaibhashika is a major scholastic tradition within this world.

Sautrantika positions, often voiced in Vasubandhu's discussions, press against parts of that system. They appeal to the discourses and challenge what they regard as unnecessary theoretical furniture. But these labels are not modern membership cards. The boundaries and historical identity of such groups can be difficult to reconstruct. We should follow the arguments rather than assign every sentence to a party headquarters.

Beyond Buddhist debates stand Brahmanical traditions with their own sophisticated accounts of selves, knowledge, matter and causation. Nyaya and Vaisheshika do not exist to be easily defeated by a Buddhist hero. Samkhya has its own account of experience and liberation. A course that lets only Vasubandhu sound intelligent would not explain his world. It would stage a victory parade.

There are also monasteries, patrons, teachers, translators and students. Philosophy lives through institutions and relationships. Manuscripts have to be copied. Students have to eat. A teaching has to be made intelligible in another language. The transmission of an argument is itself a dependent event.

Keep this intellectual city in view. Yogacara will not arrive as a mysterious revelation from nowhere. It develops amid existing problems, inherited practices and arguments about what Buddhism is trying to accomplish.

The city is crowded because the question matters: what kind of account of experience actually helps beings become free?

Chapter four. The Treasury is a tool.

Vasubandhu's Treasury of Abhidharma, the Abhidharmakosha, condenses an immense intellectual world into verse. Its prose commentary opens that world into debate. The commentary does not simply applaud every position the verses summarize.

Abhidharma can sound like the administrative department of enlightenment. Categories, subcategories, lists of factors. But the project has an urgent purpose. If suffering is conditioned, we need to distinguish the conditions. A tangled experience becomes more workable when its elements are no longer mistaken for one solid fate.

Take anger. In ordinary speech, I am angry. That may be useful and honest. But it can also hide a great deal. There is a perception of insult. An unpleasant feeling. Attention returning to a particular detail. An intention to answer. A remembered humiliation. A bodily surge. These are related; they are not interchangeable.

The analysis is not meant to make you ashamed of using the word "I." It asks what that convenient word is bundling together.

The Treasury treats far more than individual emotions. It addresses the elements of experience, the faculties, worlds and rebirth, action, afflictions, the path, knowledge and meditative attainment. It is a map of a Buddhist universe, not a pocket book of calming techniques.

Within the scholastic account it presents, seventy-five dharmas are classified. We are not going to memorize seventy-five entries and pretend that this is understanding. The important question is what work the classifications do. Which distinctions clarify causation? Which preserve a doctrine? Which create fresh difficulties?

Vasubandhu is unusually alive to those difficulties. He can state a position with enough care that its defenders recognize it, then turn and ask whether the explanation has earned the extra entity it introduces.

That gives us a way to read the Treasury without treating it as either dead taxonomy or infallible science. It is an instrument. Instruments can reveal what an unaided glance misses. They can also import assumptions about what there must be to find.

Later, Yogacara will press the analysis further. What if the very objects our analysis identifies are not as independent of cognition as the analysis assumes? But we should feel the power of the first instrument before watching it questioned.

Chapter five. A life without a proprietor.

Suppose you say: if there is no self, who remembers?

It is a serious objection. You remember something that happened years ago. The present memory seems connected to a past experience. Surely there must be someone who persisted from then to now.

Vasubandhu challenges the extra step. Connection does not automatically establish an unchanging connector.

Buddhist analysis groups the person under five aggregates: form, feeling, recognition or perception, formations, and consciousness. Different translations divide the English territory differently. The point is not that a human being is five lumps. The point is that what we call a person can be investigated as conditioned processes rather than explained by a separate, enduring owner.

A past event can change a causal sequence. A later event can arise because of that change. Memory need not be an eternal witness reaching into a private cabinet. It can be an effect of earlier experience within a continuing stream.

But be careful. Saying "a stream" does not solve everything. It names the continuity we still have to explain. Why this memory rather than another? What connects particular stages? What makes responsibility intelligible? A metaphor is a beginning, not a proof.

Vasubandhu also argues against Buddhist Personalists who affirm a person in a special relation to the aggregates. They are not merely careless people who forgot the Buddha's teaching. They are trying to protect moral continuity and liberation from what looks like disintegration. Their concern is one we should feel, even if we accept his answer.

Here is an ordinary distinction that helps. A friendship can be real without being an additional object hiding behind all the conversations, commitments, memories and acts of care that constitute it. Explaining it relationally need not cancel its value. This is our illustration, not Vasubandhu's proof. But it loosens the assumption that only an independently existing core can make something matter.

No permanent self does not mean no pain. It does not mean the person you hurt was imaginary. It does not mean an apology should begin with a lecture on aggregates.

If an account of no-self makes it harder to acknowledge another person's injury, the account is being used to protect precisely the self-concern it was meant to question.

Chapter six. The moment that cannot stay.

Now the problem becomes harder.

Vasubandhu accepts a doctrine of momentariness: conditioned things do not endure as stable substances underneath changing qualities. What appears continuous is explained through a succession of causally connected events.

Why believe something this severe?

One line of argument concerns the relation between a thing and its nature. If a thing has a fixed identity, how can that very thing become something with a different identity? Another concerns destruction. If destruction is simply the absence of a thing, can it be a new positive product made by a destructive cause? Vasubandhu argues that conditioned things perish through their own nature rather than waiting for destruction to be manufactured from outside.

This is not a laboratory discovery. It is a philosophical argument with premises about identity, causation and absence. We can examine those premises. We do not need to decorate them with quantum physics.

A candle flame may help us picture continuity through renewal. Yet the flame does not establish the doctrine. Examples make a proposal imaginable; arguments must do the additional work of making it credible.

The price of the doctrine is immediately visible. If everything is momentary, how does anything persist long enough to learn, promise, remember or become free? If consciousness stops in a deep meditative attainment, what allows it to begin again? How can an action bear fruit much later, even in another life?

These are not marginal embarrassments. They are central engines of Buddhist theory.

Notice the rhythm of the inquiry. We remove a permanent self because it seems unnecessary or incoherent. Then we discover how much explanatory work that self was being asked to do. We must now perform that work differently. Simply repeating "there is no self" is not an answer.

This is one reason Vasubandhu remains interesting. He does not only remove the familiar support. He keeps asking what can carry the load instead.

For us, the modest lesson is already demanding. You are not required to be an unchanging substance for your habits to be persistent. Nor does persistence prove that a habit is your essence. A conditioned pattern can be difficult to alter and still be alterable.

Between "this is what I am forever" and "none of this is real," there is a much more exacting territory: this arises through conditions. Which conditions?

Chapter seven. The vow after the ceremony.

You make a promise in the morning. By afternoon you are thinking about something else. Has the promise ceased to matter?

For Buddhist philosophers, vows create a puzzle. Their ethical significance continues when the person is not actively reciting them. A disciplined person asleep is not in the same position as someone who has never undertaken the discipline. How should the difference be explained?

One Vaibhashika account invokes a kind of unperceived physicality associated with ethically significant action and restraint. The term is avijnapti-rupa. It is part of a technical explanation, not a quaint name for a visible aura.

Vasubandhu challenges the need for that explanatory item. A vow can alter a person's dispositions. It changes what is remembered, what is avoided, how the stream is prepared to respond. Its continuing force need not be a separate invisible substance carrying the promise around.

The same wider debate asks how intention, performance and the successful completion of an action differ karmically. Intending harm, trying to inflict it and accomplishing it are not simply one identical event. The moral world must be more exact than that.

We should not turn an ancient account of karmic consequences into a complete modern theory of justice. Our legal and ethical reasons for attending to actual harm extend beyond an agent's experience of it. But the philosophical pressure is illuminating: what additional work is done when an intention becomes an act? How does an act reshape the person who performs it?

Here, karma is not a cosmic customer-service system distributing fair outcomes on demand. In Vasubandhu's world, it is a much larger doctrine of intentional action and its consequences, including across lives. We can explain that doctrine without claiming to verify it.

And we need an ethical boundary. Another person's suffering is not evidence that we know their past actions. A doctrine of karma does not entitle us to blame the sick, the poor or the abused. The next action available to us is care, not a fantasy of omniscient accounting.

Return to the promise. The useful question is not whether you have produced a permanently good identity. It is whether the promise changes the conditions under which you act.

A vow that never reaches attention, environment or conduct has been left outside the machinery of life. A vow that enters those conditions can keep working when it is no longer being announced.

Chapter eight. No single author of everything.

Vasubandhu also argues against a sovereign creator as the single cause of the world.

His concern is causation. The world is various. Events arise at different times under different conditions. How can one unchanged cause, entirely sufficient in itself, account for this changing multiplicity?

If the creator's nature is always to produce the world, why does everything not arise all at once? If different desires explain different creations, we have introduced distinctions that themselves demand explanation. If additional materials and conditions determine what can be created when, then the account depends on multiple causes rather than the creator alone.

The argument also touches suffering. What would it mean to praise a creator for a world in which beings undergo such misery?

These are serious objections. They are not a refutation, in advance, of every account of God anyone has ever held. A thoughtful theist may reject the description of divine causality, dispute the premises or distinguish kinds of explanation. We do not improve Vasubandhu by pretending that his opponents have no replies.

What matters for our journey is the consistency of his pressure. He is suspicious of an explanatory sovereign who is said to make events happen while the specific causal work is carried by something else.

Earlier the questioned sovereign was the permanent self inside a person. Here it is a single author behind the universe. The cases are not identical. But in both, he asks whether the explanation clarifies dependence or conceals it beneath a powerful noun.

This can become a useful discipline without becoming a universal answer. When we say "my personality made me do it," have we explained the event? Or have we renamed a pattern? When we say "that's just how the world is," have we found a cause or stopped looking?

There are times when a name helps us act. There are also times when it ends inquiry too soon.

Vasubandhu's alternative is not that nothing causes anything. It is the opposite: do not let the grandeur of one supposed cause erase the actual plurality of conditions.

That plurality will matter as we turn toward Yogacara. Mind-only will be easy to misunderstand if we imagine that he removes God and the self only to install a giant Mind as their replacement.

Chapter nine. The brother and the wider vow.

We can now return to Asanga without turning the story into a before-and-after advertisement.

Asanga is one of the central figures associated with early Yogacara. The tradition also invokes Maitreya, with different ways of understanding the figure's role and the authorship of associated works. A traditional account may speak of teachings received from the bodhisattva Maitreya. A historical account asks how texts, authors and communities developed. Those questions do not have to be forced into one evidential category.

Yogacara means something like the practice or discipline of yoga. Its textual world includes a huge and layered body known as the Yogacarabhumi, the Discourse Unraveling the Intent, Asanga's Compendium of the Great Vehicle and works associated with Maitreya. These are not chapters written at one moment by one hand.

Nor does every early Yogacara text teach an identical doctrine of mind-only. A tradition can acquire a famous philosophical position without every layer of its literature having started there.

Mahayana widens the horizon of the path through the bodhisattva commitment to awakening for the benefit of beings. It also presses the critique of inherent identity beyond the person. The elements into which we analyzed the person are themselves to be questioned. We cannot simply remove the solid self and leave a universe of equally solid little replacements.

That is part of what makes Yogacara more than a new theory about what stuff exists. It is an account of bondage and transformation. How does experience take a divided, graspable form? How do habits make that division feel inevitable? What kind of cultivation changes the conditions from which it arises?

We can appreciate the wider commitment without adopting a contemptuous picture of earlier Buddhist paths as small or selfish. Polemical rankings belong to the historical materials. They need not become our voice.

And we can hear a generous meaning in the conversion stories. The brilliant critic does not have to become less intelligent. The scope of his concern can change. An ability trained on defeating a position can be retrained on understanding the suffering that position is trying to answer.

The mind that makes a fortress can also learn how to open a door. But we still need to explain what kind of mind this is.

Chapter ten. The missing interval.

Picture a lamp being switched off. Later it comes on again. What connected the two occasions?

For a Buddhist account of momentary consciousness, the question is sharper than it looks. If one moment of consciousness conditions another, what happens when the familiar kinds of consciousness are absent?

The tradition recognizes six: seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, bodily sensing and mental consciousness. That sixth includes thinking and awareness of mental objects. But some meditative attainments are described as suspending the ordinary active consciousnesses. There are also questions about deep sleep, unconsciousness and the continuity between lives.

We are describing the tradition's theoretical problems, not declaring that a modern sleep laboratory has established its categories.

The store or foundational consciousness, alaya-vijnana, helps answer these continuity problems. It supplies an account of ongoing, subtle conditioning beneath the familiar episodes of awareness. It also helps explain the maturation of action and the continuity of embodied life.

The name is dangerous. "Storehouse" suggests a building full of tiny memories and karmic objects. "Foundation" suggests a permanent floor. Neither image should be allowed to harden into the theory.

Alaya is conditioned. It changes. It is not an immortal witness or a secret owner. In the Thirty Verses, it is treated as a flowing process with seeds, not a soul that happens to have a Buddhist name.

This is an attempt to explain how continuity can occur without the kind of enduring self the analysis rejects. Whether it succeeds depends on whether its causal role is intelligible and whether it avoids rebuilding that self in subtler language.

That suspicion is fair. If someone says, "I have discovered an invisible process that explains every gap," we should ask how the process is characterized and what constraints govern the explanation. Reverence does not remove that obligation.

But the need being addressed is real. A person is more than the thought currently in the spotlight. What we have practiced can influence what happens next without appearing as a sentence in consciousness.

The Yogacara proposal gives that fact an ambitious religious and philosophical architecture. We are now ready to walk through the architecture, keeping the exits visible.

Chapter eleven. Eight ways a world happens.

The eight-consciousness account is easier to understand if we do not picture eight people sharing a skull.

There are the five sensory consciousnesses: visual, auditory, smell, taste and bodily awareness. There is mental consciousness, which thinks, distinguishes and takes mental objects. These give us six.

The seventh is afflicted mentation, often called manas. Its distinctive trouble is appropriation. It takes the ongoing flow associated with the foundational consciousness as a self. A process is being mistaken for a proprietor.

The eighth is alaya, the foundational consciousness we have just met. It carries the account of seeds, maturation and continuity. The numbering is a teaching aid. The functions and their relations are the important part.

Return to the message on the phone. There is a visible pattern. There is understanding of words. There are feelings and intentions. Then there is the almost invisible act of taking the entire event personally: this threatens me, this confirms what always happens to me, this must be defended against.

That is our modern illustration of appropriation. It is not a claim that we can point to a single brain region and label it manas.

The Thirty Verses, which we have read in the complete publisher-issued translation by David Karma Choephel, associates afflicted mentation with forms of self-delusion, self-view, self-conceit and self-attachment. This need not look grandiose. The quiet assumption that every event belongs in the continuing case for or against me can be enough.

And consciousness never does all the work alone. Yogacara analyzes mental factors that accompany it. Contact, attention, feeling, recognition and volition are among the basic ones. Other factors can be helpful or harmful: care, generosity and non-hostility on one side; greed, anger, confusion and their elaborations on another.

This is not eight separate computers feeding a central executive. A central executive is exactly what we should be wary of sneaking back into the picture. It is an account of related functions within a conditioned stream.

Nor is alaya the enlightened part and the others merely dirty. The foundational process belongs to the explanation of ordinary bondage. Liberation requires a transformation of the basis, not permanent loyalty to a hidden inner warehouse.

The architecture makes a practical claim about our lives. The sentence you consciously endorse is only one part of what shapes your response. Beneath the declaration "I know better" may remain well-practiced habits of grasping. Knowing the address of a door is not the same as being able to walk through it.

Chapter twelve. Seeds without a warehouse.

A seed is an excellent metaphor until we begin looking for a tiny object.

In Yogacara, seeds help describe causal capacities. Past acts and experiences condition what can arise later. Current experience can reinforce or alter the pattern. The process is reciprocal: what arises expresses conditioning and becomes part of further conditioning.

Think of learning a language. At first you labor over sounds and rules. Later a word is understood before you deliberately recall a rule. A history of practice is active in the present without being laid out in front of you. This is a modern analogy for dispositional learning, not proof of the Buddhist account of karmic seeds.

The distinction matters because alaya has a far larger job than explaining ordinary habit. It is implicated in rebirth and the formation of the worlds beings experience. If we keep only language learning, we have selected one accessible comparison, not translated the whole doctrine into contemporary science.

William Waldron's scholarship is especially useful here. It asks us to notice that the conditioning of experience is not merely private. Language and shared patterns of activity help shape the world that becomes intelligible to us. We do not first become fully finished, isolated minds and then add society as optional decoration.

The words that make an insult recognizable, the roles that make a gesture respectful or offensive, the categories through which a community notices things: these have histories between people. What feels most immediately mine can depend on something learned with others.

But a shared world is not a single cosmic mind. Similar conditioning and interaction do not establish one universal subject wearing many masks. That is not a conclusion we can insert into Vasubandhu's work simply because it sounds nondual.

There is another boundary. The doctrine of seeds must not become a device for total self-blame. If the world I experience depends on conditioning, it does not follow that I deliberately chose everything that happened to me. Conditions include what other people do. They include situations I did not design and cannot individually control.

A better practical question is local and answerable: what am I rehearsing now?

Not, "Did I manifest this injury?" But, "What response might reduce harm?" Not, "Why am I still a bad person?" But, "Which pattern becomes more likely when I repeat this act?"

The seed metaphor becomes humane when it opens the future. It becomes cruel when it is used to prosecute the past with knowledge we do not possess.

Chapter thirteen. The case against the obvious.

The Twenty Verses puts a bold claim on trial. The world of experience is appearance-only or representation-only, vijnapti-matra. How far that claim reaches is disputed. But Vasubandhu is plainly doing more than recommending a positive attitude.

His opponent has good questions.

If there are no independent external objects doing the explanatory work, why do experiences happen in particular places and at particular times? Why do different people encounter what seems to be the same thing? And why does a real meal satisfy hunger while an imagined meal does not?

Vasubandhu begins by challenging the alleged impossibility. Dreams can have definite places and times. Within a dream, a room is over there rather than everywhere; an event happens now rather than at every moment. So spatial and temporal order alone do not prove an independently existing object.

For shared experience he appeals to Buddhist cosmological examples. Hungry ghosts are described as jointly encountering a terrible world shaped by similar karma. Beings in hell encounter tormentors within a shared predicament.

We need to be honest about the force of this reply. It works within religious assumptions accepted by the imagined debate partners. A listener who does not accept hungry ghosts has not been given independent empirical evidence that shared worlds can occur without external objects. We should not replace the ghosts with social media and claim the ancient argument has now been experimentally proved. Shared interpretation is not automatically the same thing as the absence of a material world.

For causal effects, Vasubandhu gives a deliberately bodily example: a sexual dream can produce an actual physical response. Merely experienced appearances are not therefore causally inert.

Again, be exact. Showing that some dream experiences have effects does not show that every waking experience is a dream. The initial replies loosen the opponent's claim that appearance-only is impossible. More is needed to establish it as true.

That is an important distinction in any argument. You may discover that an alternative can explain something you thought only your view could explain. You have lost an exclusive advantage. You have not necessarily lost the whole case.

Vasubandhu knows he needs more. The next move is to examine the rival account of external objects itself.

Before we follow him, give the opponent a little credit. The person asking about the meal has not failed to understand spirituality. They are asking whether our theory can survive contact with hunger. A worthwhile philosophy should welcome the question.

Chapter fourteen. The atom with a side.

Now we reach an argument small enough to fit between two atoms, and large enough to unsettle a world.

Suppose a visible object is made of ultimate, partless atoms. How do they combine?

If one atom meets a neighbor on one side and another neighbor on another side, it seems to have distinguishable sides. But then it has parts. If it has no distinct sides at all, how can combining many such atoms produce the extension we perceive?

Vasubandhu also challenges the alternative of a whole existing independently over and above its parts. What exactly is perceived in addition to the parts? How can an absolutely unitary, partless object have the spatial differentiation that perception seems to require?

These are arguments about parts and wholes, extension and perception. The technical word is mereology. Their targets include forms of ancient Buddhist atomism and non-Buddhist accounts of wholes.

They are not a prediction of quantum mechanics. Modern uses of "particle," "field" and "atom" are not interchangeable with the entities under dispute. We do not honor an ancient philosopher by pretending that he settled a scientific question before the relevant science existed.

What he is trying to do is clear. The realist must give a coherent account of the object that supposedly causes and grounds perception. It is not enough to point at the apparent object and assume that its metaphysical structure explains itself.

How decisive is the argument? That depends on the available alternatives and the assumptions we accept. A realist might reject the proposed account of ultimate parts, offer a different theory of composition or dispute the move from limits of perception to nonexistence. Defeating a particular atomism would not, on its own, defeat every possible realism.

Vasubandhu's larger strategy combines the pressure on physical explanations with the claim that appearance-only can explain the relevant features of experience. Some interpreters emphasize inference to the best explanation; others analyze the structure differently. We should not mistake our ability to summarize the strategy for the disappearance of every objection.

Still, something valuable has happened. The ordinary world has not been destroyed. Its obviousness has stopped counting as a complete theory.

A cup can remain perfectly available for drinking while the account of what ultimately constitutes a cup becomes difficult. Philosophy begins when we can keep those levels distinct without using either one to silence the other.

Chapter fifteen. The three natures of a quarrel.

Let us return to our four-word message. You assumed an accusation. Then you learned the sender wanted to discuss dinner.

A useful introduction to the three natures can begin here, provided we do not confuse the illustration with the full doctrine.

The imagined or constructed nature is the way experience is taken under a false division and attribution. In our example, an independently hostile person confronts an independently threatened me. My reading feels built into the message itself.

The dependent nature concerns the conditions through which the experience arises. There are words, memories, habits, expectations and a present situation. In Yogacara's fuller account, the process is explained through conditioned consciousness and its dispositions, not merely through a modern list of psychological influences.

The perfected nature is the absence of the imagined nature in that dependent process. The supposed intrinsic structure was never there in the way we grasped it. Seeing that absence is not adding a third magical substance behind the first two.

This is an original explanation of the relation, not a translation of the verses. Another route appears in the Three Natures Exposition attributed to Vasubandhu. A magician makes an elephant seem present. There is the elephant the audience takes to be there; there is the conditioned appearance through which that mistake occurs; and there is the absence of an actual elephant in that appearance. The absence is not another elephant hiding backstage. Likewise, the perfected nature is not an additional object discovered underneath the world.

These are not simply three floors of a building: illusion downstairs, a little reality in the middle, absolute bliss on the roof. They are connected ways of understanding the same situation.

Nor is the imagined nature just an occasional false opinion. The analysis reaches the structure of subject and object: an independent grasper confronting independently established things to be grasped. That division is far more intimate than one mistaken text-message interpretation.

Here the tradition becomes subtle. It does not merely say that the object is unreal while the subject survives untouched as the ultimate owner. Both sides of the grasping relation must be examined. If the world has been moved inside a private mental room and the self is still sitting on the throne, the inquiry has stopped too soon.

Different interpreters disagree about the status of the dependent process and about how to describe what is ultimately real. We will not hide that disagreement inside a beautiful metaphor. But the central relation is clear enough to use: dependence does not require the intrinsic separation we imagine.

Now add an ethical complication. Perhaps the message really was hostile. Understanding construction does not require calling every danger a misunderstanding. We still distinguish accurate recognition, uncertainty and projection. We still protect people and set boundaries.

The invitation is not to dissolve judgment. It is to stop making our first construction of a situation the unquestionable law of the universe.

An experience can be conditioned without being arbitrary. A response can become wiser without pretending that nothing happened.

Chapter sixteen. Do other people survive?.

If everything is mind-only, what about your mind?

This is the point where an attractive slogan can become a moral disaster. If other people are merely my projections, perhaps their suffering has no claim on me.

Vasubandhu does not accept that escape. The Twenty Verses acknowledges other streams of consciousness and their influence on one another. It also invokes the Buddha's knowledge of other minds. His account is not the ordinary claim that only my personal mind exists.

That does not make the philosophical difficulty vanish. If appearances are insufficient to establish external objects, why are they enough to establish other minds? If explanatory economy is a virtue, why not make the account even smaller and retain only one stream?

Those are genuine pressures. They should remain audible.

Later Buddhist epistemologists take them up. Dharmakirti argues, in effect, that the realist does not directly perceive another person's inner life either. Both positions use an inference. I encounter apparently intentional activity that does not originate in my own present volition; another stream of intention helps explain it. The argument works with experienced signs, so idealism is not automatically deprived of all access to it.

That is a later development, not a speech we should put into Vasubandhu's mouth. And it has limits. An inference can be plausible without delivering the direct, complete knowledge of another mind that it does not possess.

Later still, Ratnakirti pushes the problem in a more radical direction. Interpretations of his position differ, including how ultimate and conventional levels are being distinguished. It would be careless to turn his arguments into Vasubandhu's secret final teaching.

For our purposes, two things must stand together. Vasubandhu intends to preserve other beings and moral consequences. His intention alone is not a complete solution to every problem his theory raises.

We owe him both kinds of honesty.

And there is a practical test. Does a reading make us more capable of listening to another person whose experience differs from ours? Or does it give us a reason to overwrite them?

If someone says they are hurt, "you are only appearing in my mind" is not advanced philosophy. It is a refusal of relationship.

The bodhisattva's world cannot be populated only by opportunities for the bodhisattva to feel wise. Other beings are not decorative figures in the drama of my awakening.

Chapter seventeen. What does mind-only mean?.

We can now enter the scholarly disagreement without using it as fog.

One important reading takes Yogacara as a form of metaphysical idealism: the independent material world is denied, while consciousness and its transformations do the explanatory work. There are strong reasons for this reading, including the explicit arguments against external objects. It cannot be dismissed as a mistake made only by people who failed to meditate.

Another family of readings emphasizes what can be known, or the structure of experience. The target is the claim that objects exist in the independent way they appear to us. On such readings, the teaching need not amount to the simple proposition that mental stuff is the only stuff in the universe.

Nondual interpretations go further in questioning the picture of an already finished inner mind facing an already finished outer world. Subject and object are themselves dependent constructions within experience. Matthew MacKenzie develops a careful comparison between internalist and nondual readings along these lines.

These approaches overlap, but they are not identical. Some may illuminate one text better than another. Yogacara is a long, varied tradition, not a single sentence waiting for its correct English label.

There is a tempting way to escape the disagreement. We can say: whatever Vasubandhu meant, everyone agrees experience is conditioned, so let us keep only that. This gives us a useful contemporary reflection. It does not give us the whole historical Vasubandhu.

There is an opposite temptation: select the most extreme metaphysical reading, present it as certain, then treat every doubt as spiritual immaturity. That replaces study with a loyalty test.

A better stance has several levels. We can be confident that Vasubandhu challenges naive confidence in independently given objects. We can be confident that he analyzes the construction of a subject as well as its objects. We can acknowledge that he offers arguments more radical than ordinary cognitive bias theory. And we can remain exact about disagreement over the scope and ultimate commitments of those arguments.

None of this is validated merely because a modern scientist says perception involves processing. A theory in which physical brains construct representations is not automatically a theory in which physical objects are denied. Similar vocabulary can conceal different explanations.

If you need one portable sentence, make it this: mind-only is an invitation to examine the status of both the world we grasp and the grasper we assume. It is not permission to believe anything we like.

Chapter eighteen. Reading against the letter.

Vasubandhu has a problem that every inheritor of a large tradition eventually meets. The authoritative texts do not always seem to say the same thing.

Some teachings speak in the ordinary language of persons and objects. Others undo the apparent solidity of both. How can both belong to a coherent path?

His answer involves interpretation. A teaching may have an intention suited to its hearers without being the final literal description of reality. Words that help one person can mislead another if lifted out of their situation.

In the Twenty Verses, the opponent can object that the Buddha taught sense organs and their objects. Vasubandhu cannot simply ignore the objection. He explains why such a teaching might be given and how its purpose fits a deeper account.

He also wrote a work concerned with the proper exposition of scripture, the Vyakhyayukti. Interpretation is not an accidental repair carried out when philosophy becomes inconvenient. It is part of his intellectual practice.

This can be generous. It allows a tradition to teach people where they are rather than require everyone to begin with the most difficult formulation. It recognizes that language has effects and that a technically correct statement can be harmful if misunderstood.

But the danger is equally clear. "The teacher had a special intention" can become a way to make any favored position immune from evidence. An interpreter can always claim that the inconvenient passage secretly means the opposite of what it says.

We need to keep the discipline that distinguishes interpretation from special pleading. What textual reasons support the reading? What contradiction does it resolve? What pedagogical purpose does it explain? Does the explanation fit the larger work, or only protect the interpreter's preference?

And there is an ethical limit of our own that should be explicit. A theory of skillful teaching is not a general permission for teachers to deceive students, conceal harm or demand obedience. Historical explanation and present-day authorization are different matters.

The value of Vasubandhu's example is not that we should hand our judgment to the person who claims the deepest interpretation. It is that words belong to situations, purposes and causal effects.

Even the phrase "no self" can help or wound depending on what it is being used to do. Spoken to loosen possessiveness, it may open something. Spoken to dismiss someone's grief, it may close the very capacity for care the path requires.

Chapter nineteen. Changing the basis.

The Thirty Verses has a different movement from the Twenty. Rather than staging objection after objection, it gives a compact account of consciousness, its transformations and the path beyond grasping.

One of its most important cautions comes late. Thinking that everything is mere representation is still a thought held in a subject-object structure. You can make mind-only into a treasured object. You can become the person who possesses the correct view of non-possession.

The theory then becomes another piece of furniture in the room it was supposed to help you examine.

The culmination is associated with transformation of the basis, ashraya-paravritti. The path does not merely replace an unpleasant opinion with a pleasant one. It concerns a change in the conditions of experience and the patterns of grasping and affliction.

This belongs to a demanding Buddhist path of ethical discipline, learning, contemplation and meditative cultivation. We cannot deliver its claimed realization through a few minutes of audio. Nor should a temporary feeling of spaciousness be announced as proof that the entire structure has been transformed.

What we can offer is a small, original reflection inspired by the inquiry. It is not a traditional initiation or a substitute for sustained training.

Choose an ordinary, mild irritation. Not your worst memory, not an overwhelming situation. If the exercise becomes unsettling, stop and return to your surroundings.

First notice what can actually be observed. Perhaps a few words were spoken. Perhaps there is tension in the face. Perhaps a reply is forming.

Then notice what has been added. A conclusion about the other person's entire character. A prediction that this always happens. A verdict about what the event proves about you.

You do not have to defeat any of these thoughts. Just distinguish an occurrence from the claims gathering around it.

Now ask about conditions. Were you tired? Was something unclear? Is there a real boundary that needs to be stated? Is an old expectation narrowing the available meanings?

Finally, choose one act that would reduce confusion or harm. Ask a question. Take time before answering. State the boundary without adding a sentence designed to wound. Or seek help if the situation requires it.

The point is not to manufacture a blank mind. It is to experience the possibility that the first world your reaction builds is not the only world in which you can act.

No fireworks are required. A slightly less automatic response can be a serious event.

Chapter twenty. Does awakening lose the world?.

There is a question we have not yet allowed to become vivid. Suppose dualistic grasping ends. What happens to the colors, sounds and forms of experience?

Does wisdom mean that all appearance must disappear? Or can appearance remain without being divided into an independent observer and an independently existing observed thing?

Later Yogacara debates about cognitive aspects turn on questions of this kind. The Sanskrit word akara can mean an aspect, form or image in cognition. Sakara and nirakara become labels for positions concerning whether cognition has such aspects and what status they possess.

The labels shift with context. A position called imageless in a debate about perception is not automatically identical to every later Yogacara position bearing a similar name. Nor should every later distinction be projected back into Vasubandhu's lifetime.

Within the later Yogacara controversy, one important tendency treats the appearing forms as false accretions. What is ultimately free is not stained by them. Another tendency argues that manifest forms need not themselves be the problem. A cognition can be free of the imagined division into grasper and grasped while still having appearance.

This is a simplified doorway into a difficult debate. It is not a chart assigning every philosopher to an unchanging team. Yuichi Kajiyama's study of the controversy shows both the importance of later materials and the difficulty of aligning the different sources.

But feel what is at stake. Is freedom imagined as getting rid of the world, or as a change in how experience occurs? Does the fact that something appears already make it deceptive? Or is the deception the status we attribute to what appears?

These questions help explain why Buddhist traditions can sound so different when describing wisdom. One description emphasizes the ending of fabrication. Another emphasizes appearance without fixation. We should neither declare them identical nor assume that their vocabularies can never meet.

And we should resist a shortcut especially attractive in audio courses: induce a quiet state, announce that the absence of vivid content proves the ultimate, and leave the listener to defend the experience afterward. Calm, blankness, insight and liberation are not interchangeable names.

A useful question is less theatrical. After the quiet, how do we meet what returns? A sound. A demand. Someone else's need. If the only way to preserve our supposed freedom is to keep the rest of life away, the inquiry is not over.

Chapter twenty-one. The world after him.

Vasubandhu's work does not end in a single line of agreement.

Commentators develop different accounts. Sthiramati and Dharmapala become associated with distinct interpretive trajectories. Dignaga and Dharmakirti help reshape Buddhist work on knowledge and argument. Later thinkers debate whether and how the forms appearing in consciousness are ultimately real.

Transmission into Chinese produces further developments. Paramartha's translations and interpretations matter. Xuanzang's seventh-century work, and the interpretive project associated with his student Kuiji, help shape the tradition often called Faxiang in China and Hosso in Japan. Xuanzang's Demonstration of Consciousness-Only draws on a commentary tradition around the Thirty Verses; it is not simply another short book written by Vasubandhu.

Other works attributed to Vasubandhu matter for traditions of the bodhisattva path, Buddha-nature and Pure Land devotion. Attribution can be contested. A text's influence in a community does not settle the identity of its original author.

In Tibet, the Treasury remains a major field of study. Yogacara is debated, classified, criticized and combined with other approaches. Shantarakshita is important to later syntheses of Yogacara reasoning and Madhyamaka. These histories are not adequately described as one school losing a tournament and vanishing.

The disagreement with Madhyamaka is especially easy to caricature. One side allegedly believes mind is real; the other allegedly believes nothing exists. Neither slogan is enough. The disputes concern how emptiness should be understood, whether a foundation is being retained, and whether the supposed foundation has itself been examined. Different texts and interpreters draw the lines differently.

We have followed a route through this large world, not read every surviving work. The Treasury alone could sustain years of study. The source companion names the materials that supported our route, and distinguishes complete short texts from selections and scholarly accounts.

So return to the beginning. Four words arrive on a screen. A world appears. There is a history behind its appearing, a living process within it, and another person who may not be who your fear has made them.

You do not need to solve every metaphysical dispute before asking what happens next. But those disputes can loosen the certainty with which a passing construction becomes an unquestionable self and an unquestionable world.

The absence of a permanent owner does not cancel the need for care. It makes care a matter of conditions, acts and relationships rather than the defense of a hidden essence.

Memory can matter without an eternal witness. A promise can matter after its words have ended. A person can be more than a passing thought without being an unchanging thing.

And an insight is not finished when it gives us an impressive account of experience. It has to reach the next moment of experience.

Perhaps the message really is difficult. Perhaps a defense is needed. Perhaps an apology is. Perhaps dinner is waiting.

Before the old world closes around the event, there may be room for one honest question.

That room is not outside your life. It is something your life can learn to make.
