Most maps of progress are drawn by what they add. You climb, you gather, you arrive somewhere richer than where you started. This discourse draws its map the other way around. Nine times, the Buddha points to something the mind does, and says: there is a place where that stops. The path here is not measured by how much experience you can collect. It is measured by what you can set down. That is this episode's reading of the shape of the text, and everything that follows stays with it: a map that advances by subtraction.

Welcome to The Buddha's Nine Progressive Attainments: what falls away, and what remains. Our source is a short discourse from the Aṅguttara Nikāya, the collection of discourses arranged by number. In the Book of the Nines it is number 33, the Anupubbavihārasamāpatti Sutta, the discourse on the successive abidings and attainments. It is set at Sāvatthī, in Jeta's Grove, and in it the Buddha speaks to the monks. A few honest words about how this episode is made. Everything you will hear as teaching from the discourse is a new working rendering, made for this episode directly from the Pali. These are not scholarly translations. They are not the historical Buddha's recorded voice, and they are not a substitute for a teacher, or for the published translations, which we compared as we worked and will credit at the end. What you will hear is explanation: our reading of a textual map. Other Buddhist systems sort these states in their own ways, and this episode claims no more than that this discourse sorts them this way. The shape of the discourse is a repeated question. For each of nine attainments, the Buddha first names the thing that ceases there, then stages a doubter who asks where it ceases and who could possibly live that way, then gives the answer. The nine, in brief, are these: four jhānas, the deep absorptions; four immaterial attainments, where even the perception of form has fallen away; and a ninth state, the cessation of perception and feeling. Here is how the teaching opens, in our rendering: 'Monks, I will teach you the nine successive abidings and attainments. Listen to this. Attend carefully. I will speak. And what are the nine successive abidings and attainments?'

The first round begins with the coarsest item on the list. Here is the pattern, given in full this once: 'Where sensual pleasures cease, and of those who abide having made sensual pleasures cease, again and again, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' Then the staged objection: 'But suppose someone says: Where do sensual pleasures cease? And who are these who abide having made them cease, again and again? I do not know this. I do not see this. Whoever speaks like that should be answered like this: Here, friend, a monk, quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unskillful qualities, enters and dwells in the first jhāna, which has directed thought and sustained thought, and rapture and pleasure born of seclusion. It is here that sensual pleasures cease. And these are the ones who abide having made them cease, again and again.' And after every answer, the discourse adds this: 'Truly, monks, one who is honest and without deceit would delight in that answer and approve of it, saying: Well said. And having delighted in it and approved of it, he would honor the one who spoke, and attend on him with palms joined.' Hold on to two things. First, the Pali repeats its own key phrase: having made them cease, having made them cease. The text describes repeatedly bringing this factor to cessation and dwelling in that attainment, not permanent eradication of desire. Second, the mind of the first jhāna is not blank. Directed thought and sustained thought are still at work. What has ceased is not thinking. What has ceased is sensuality, the pull to live inside sense pleasure. And notice the order of the Buddha's priorities. The first jhāna could have been introduced by its rapture, its pleasure. It is introduced instead by an absence. Here, sensual pleasures cease.

Second round. 'Where directed thought and sustained thought cease, and of those who abide having made them cease, again and again, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' Where is that? The discourse abbreviates the formula here, as it does for the next two rounds, so we give it in its full standard form, the same formula spelled out elsewhere in the early discourses: 'Here, friend, a monk, with the settling down of directed thought and sustained thought, enters and dwells in the second jhāna, which has inner serenity and unification of mind, which is free of directed thought and sustained thought, and which has rapture and pleasure born of concentration. It is here that directed thought and sustained thought cease. And these are the ones who abide having made them cease, again and again.' A word about those two factors, because translators have rendered them differently for a long time, and this episode will not pretend the debate is settled. Vitakka, as we render it, is directed thought: the mind turning toward its object and taking hold. Vicāra is sustained thought: the mind staying with the object, moving around it, examining it. Some translators hear these two as ordinary verbal thinking, the inner sentences of the everyday mind. Others hear something quieter, a movement of attention below the level of words. The discourse itself is structural, and on either reading the structure is the same. In the first jhāna these movements were present, and the state was defined by what else had stopped. In the second, the movements themselves grow still, and rapture and pleasure remain, now born of concentration rather than of seclusion.

Third round. 'Where rapture ceases, and of those who abide having made rapture cease, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' The answer, in the full standard form: 'Here, friend, a monk, with the fading away of rapture, dwells equanimous, mindful and clearly aware, and he feels pleasure with the body. He enters and dwells in the third jhāna, the one of which the noble ones say: equanimous and mindful, he dwells in pleasure. It is here that rapture ceases. And these are the ones who abide having made it cease.' Be careful with this round, because it is easy to mishear. The discourse does not say that pleasure ends here. Rapture ends: pīti, the bright, rising joy of the first two jhānas. What remains is quieter: sukha, pleasure, felt with the body itself, under the steady gaze of equanimity. The ladder is not a ladder out of feeling good. Through the first three jhānas, pleasure remains as rapture falls away; in the fourth, even pleasure is relinquished. Rapture is real. The text honors it across two whole attainments. It is simply not the summit, and by the third jhāna the mind has set it down.

Fourth round. 'Where the pleasure of equanimity ceases, and of those who abide having made it cease, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' The answer, in the full standard form: 'Here, friend, a monk, with the giving up of pleasure and the giving up of pain, and with the earlier disappearance of elation and grief, enters and dwells in the fourth jhāna, which is neither painful nor pleasant, and which has purity of mindfulness brought about by equanimity. It is here that the pleasure of equanimity ceases. And these are the ones who abide having made it cease.' This is the finest distinction in the first four, and the Pali compound carries it: upekkhāsukha, the pleasure of equanimity. That is what ceases at the fourth jhāna: the fine pleasure that accompanied equanimity through the third. Equanimity itself does not cease. It remains, and it has work to do: the fourth jhāna's mindfulness is purified by it. Set the two formulas side by side and the exchange is exact. In the third jhāna, equanimity and pleasure stand together. In the fourth, the pleasure is gone and the equanimity stands clearer. Even the subtlest comfort is set down, and what the comfort was resting in stays in place.

With the fifth round the terrain changes. The first four attainments refine a mind still living in a world of form. The next four begin by going beyond perceptions of form. Fifth round. 'Where perceptions of form cease, and of those who abide having made them cease, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' The answer, which from here on the discourse gives in full: 'Here, friend, a monk, having wholly passed beyond perceptions of form, with the disappearance of perceptions of resistance, and with no attention paid to perceptions of diversity, abides with the perception 'space is endless'. He enters and dwells in the dimension of infinite space. It is here that perceptions of form cease. And these are the ones who abide having made them cease, again and again.' Three things go quiet together: perceptions of form, of resistance, of diversity. Of shaped things with edges. Of a world that pushes back. Of one thing picked out as different from another. What opens in their place is not a bigger thing. It is a field without edges, and the attainment is named after the field. Hear this as a description of perception, not as a claim that physical space is the absolute nature of the universe. The discourse is mapping the inside of meditation, not issuing a cosmology. Sixth round. 'Where the perception of the dimension of infinite space ceases, and of those who abide having made it cease, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' The answer: 'Here, friend, a monk, having wholly passed beyond the dimension of infinite space, abides with the perception 'consciousness is endless'. He enters and dwells in the dimension of infinite consciousness. It is here that the perception of the dimension of infinite space ceases. And these are the ones who abide having made it cease.' Notice the rhythm the map has settled into. Each attainment turns its predecessor into the next thing to cease. Space itself, which a moment ago felt like final openness, becomes one more perception to pass beyond. The next attainment is named infinite consciousness; the text does not explain that transition here.

Seventh round. 'Where the perception of the dimension of infinite consciousness ceases, and of those who abide having made it cease, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' The answer: 'Here, friend, a monk, having wholly passed beyond the dimension of infinite consciousness, abides with the perception 'there is nothing'. He enters and dwells in the dimension of nothingness. It is here that the perception of the dimension of infinite consciousness ceases. And these are the ones who abide having made it cease.' Nothingness here is the name of an attainment, the perception a meditator rests in: there is nothing. It is not the Buddha's teaching that nothing exists. It is not nihilism, and it is not despair. There is a difference, and this discourse stands inside it, between attending to nothing at all and believing that nothing is real. Eighth round. 'Where the perception of the dimension of nothingness ceases, and of those who abide having made it cease, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' The answer: 'Here, friend, a monk, having wholly passed beyond the dimension of nothingness, enters and dwells in the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception. It is here that the perception of the dimension of nothingness ceases. And these are the ones who abide having made it cease.' There is almost nothing left to describe here, and that is the point. Perception has thinned to the point where the discourse will not call it perception and will not call it non-perception either. It is not unconsciousness. It is not a blackout. It is the finest perception the map allows, defined by the refusal of both labels. One rung remains, and on that rung perception itself is what ceases.

Ninth round. 'Where the perception of the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception ceases, and of those who abide having made it cease, I say: truly, in that respect, those venerable ones are free of craving, cooled, crossed over, and gone to the far shore.' The answer: 'Here, friend, a monk, having wholly passed beyond the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception, enters and dwells in the cessation of perception and feeling. It is here that the perception of the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception ceases. And these are the ones who abide having made it cease.' And the discourse closes with a plain summary: 'These are the nine successive abidings and attainments.' Handle this ninth state carefully, because language strains here and the discourse knows it. It calls the state saññāvedayitanirodha, the cessation of perception and feeling. It does not call it sleep, though nothing is perceived. It does not call it death, though feeling has stopped. It does not call it dissociation or a blank, and it does not call it final liberation either. It gives no technique for reaching it, no instructions, no account of how long it lasts, no story of anyone entering it or coming back. The pattern the discourse has followed nine times simply completes itself. Each rung was defined by the ceasing of the rung before, and at the top, the last and finest perception ceases, and feeling with it. This episode follows the text's example: this is a state described on a map, not a state to be induced by following instructions from a recording.

Now listen again to the phrase the discourse would not let go of. After every declaration, after the words about being free of craving, cooled, crossed over, gone to the far shore, the Buddha adds a small qualifier: tadaṅgena. In that respect. With respect to that factor. That qualifier is the lock on the whole map. When the first jhāna makes sensual pleasures cease, the one who dwells there is crossed over in that respect, with respect to sensuality. The grand words are spoken, and the qualifier keeps them honest. And the qualifier is never withdrawn. There is something else this discourse never does. Other discourses, having told of the jhānas, go on to tell of the taints, the deepest defilements, being destroyed through knowledge, and of a mind released once and for all. The Sāmaññaphala Sutta, the great discourse on the fruits of the contemplative life, ends its meditation account exactly there. This discourse does not. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, in a note on this sutta, draws attention to that absence, and the absence carries weight. This map does not declare that everyone who completes the nine attainments is thereby fully liberated, and it does not declare that liberation requires all nine. What it says, nine times, is narrower and, to our ears, more trustworthy: here is exactly what ceases, and here is the freedom that belongs to that ceasing, in that respect. A map can be profound and still be a map. It describes the country. It does not certify that any of us has arrived.

So the map is complete, and it is a strange kind of treasure map, because every landmark on it is a disappearance. Sensuality goes. Thinking goes. Rapture goes. The finest pleasure goes. Perceptions of form are left behind; then the perceptions associated with infinite space, infinite consciousness, and nothingness. At the last, perception and feeling themselves cease. Each time, what the discourse calls freedom is found on the far side of a ceasing, and each time the same honest qualifier is attached: in that respect. If there is one movement to take from this text, and we offer it plainly as our interpretation, not as a further quotation, it is this. The ordinary way to honor an experience is to hold it tighter: to make it last, to collect it, to build a self out of having had it. This discourse honors experience differently. It asks what the experience is made of, watches one component after another be set down, and finds the mind lighter on the other side of each setting down. The progress is not in what you can gather. It is in what you can stop needing. Credits and sources.
