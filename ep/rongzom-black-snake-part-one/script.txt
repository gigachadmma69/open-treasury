The Snake Nobody Needed to Kill. Reading Rongzom, part one.

Imagine five people looking into the same patch of water. The first sees a snake and reaches for a weapon. The second says it is only a reflection, but reaches for a remedy anyway. The third knows it cannot bite and still cannot put a hand near it. The fourth stamps on it to prove there is nothing to fear. The fifth does not need a victory over it.

One image. Five theories of liberation. And a question that can turn against every theory, including the one that calls itself the highest: what, exactly, are you still trying to defeat?

This is the opening installment of a reading of the accessible writings of Rongzom Chokyi Zangpo, an early Tibetan thinker associated with the Nyingma tradition. We begin with The Black Snake Discourse, then read Establishing Appearances as Divine. These are full readings of the two short works, with explanations where the argument needs them. We are not claiming to have translated or covered his entire surviving corpus. That larger project includes works not yet checked and ritual material excluded from this public series.

The translation in this installment is by David Higgins, published on Lotsawa House in 2023. It is used under the Creative Commons Attribution NonCommercial 4.0 license. The explanations are new editorial commentary, not words by Rongzom or Higgins. Footnote numbers and typographic features are not spoken. The written source links preserve the translator's notes. This is a study reading, not a critical edition, an empowerment, or a claim to transmit a lineage.

One more agreement before we start. A polemical map is not a neutral survey. Rongzom is arranging rival Buddhist approaches so that Great Perfection comes out on top. We will hear what that arrangement lets him see, and also ask what it makes his rivals look like. You do not have to surrender your judgment in order to take the argument seriously.

Chapter one. The dispute begins after the appearance.

Rongzom's text.

The categories of views and practices belonging to the higher and lower vehicles may be understood, in summary form, as follows. The various views are posited based upon the appearing of embodiments, locations, and objects, which are encompassed by body, speech, and mind. But as for the question of whether things appear or not: for any people who uphold the various scriptural traditions, from beginners up to tenth level bodhisattvas, this is not a matter for debate. Why is that? Because directly perceived appearance is indisputable. For this reason, debates concerning such appearance arise over the ways in which it is characterized.

To summarize, the ways of characterizing appearance are of five kinds. In this regard, we shall first cite an example. The example is the appearance of the reflection of a black snake in water:

Commentary. The opening is more careful than a quick reading might suggest. Rongzom does not say that whatever appears is true. He says the occurrence of appearance is not what these systems dispute. Their disagreement begins when they say what the appearance is. Is it a real thing? An illusion with real consequences? A pure display? Something requiring removal?

Keep that distinction available. An appearance can be undeniable as an occurrence while our account of it remains mistaken. A nightmare is experienced. The monster it presents need not be standing beside the bed. That is an explanatory analogy, not an additional example from Rongzom's text.

The Buddhist vocabulary can sound remote. Here, a vehicle is an approach to liberation. A bodhisattva is someone oriented toward awakening for the benefit of beings. The tenth level is an advanced stage of that path. Rongzom's opening range, from beginners to tenth-level bodhisattvas, gives his argument a deliberately wide scope. Now comes the image that will do most of the work.

Chapter 2. Five responses to one reflection.

Rongzom's text.

1. Some people, believing it is an actual snake, are afraid, and try to get rid of it.

2. Some know it to be a reflection of a snake. Although they thus recognize that it is not a real snake, they see that even a reflection has the capacity to produce harmful effects, so they make efforts to rely on ways to counteract it through skillful means.

3. Some know it to be a reflection and because it is therefore not grounded in the major elements, they know it has no capacity to produce effects. Still, due to the power of having previously been afraid of snakes and the like, they are unable to touch or trample the reflection.

4. Some understand that it has no capacity to produce effects because it is [only] a reflection, and to quickly dispel this paranoid state of mind, they touch it and trample it by means of ascetic disciplines.

5. Some know it to be a reflection, so they have no thought of rejecting or accepting it and do not make efforts in any way at all. In a similar vein, the philosophical tenets of the vehicles accord with the meaning of these examples.

Commentary. The list is simple enough to hear without interruption. Its difficulty comes afterward. The person who stamps on the reflection has understood more than the person who mistakes it for an animal. Yet the stamping still gives the reflection a job to do. It must become the occasion for a demonstration, a victory, a completed cure.

That is the distinctive pressure of the fifth response. It does not merely replace a bad opinion with a better opinion. It questions the extra transaction in which an appearance must be defeated before freedom can begin.

But the parable has a boundary. There is still water here. A reflection cannot bite, but water can drown someone. Reading an account of illusion does not cancel the practical consequences of bodies, conduct, or neglect. Rongzom's image is an argument about views and fixation. Turning it into an instruction to ignore danger would be our mistake, not an achievement of the text.

He now assigns the five responses to five approaches. Listen for two changes together: what the approach says exists, and what response that claim seems to demand.

Chapter 3. The first response. Treating the threat as a thing.

Rongzom's text.

1. The first of these is the way of the Śrāvakas. In this case, they accept that these phenomena of suffering and sources of suffering exist conventionally and exist ultimately, and also that they are substantially existent. Hence, by the power of these beliefs, they see things as real and engage in accepting and rejecting them. This is similar to seeing the snake's reflection as something real and then trying to get rid of it. Among what are known as "the four modes of existence," in this system we have three: 1) ultimate existence, 2) conventional existence, and 3) the substantial existence of both.

Commentary. Shravaka means a hearer or disciple. In this passage, Rongzom presents this approach as treating suffering and its sources as substantially real, and therefore as objects to accept or abandon.

It would be careless to turn this paragraph into a verdict on every early Buddhist text or every living Theravada tradition. We are hearing Rongzom's compressed characterization within his own ascending scheme. A defender of the approach could object that identifying suffering and abandoning its causes need not entail the crude realism suggested by the snake image. The present text does not stage that full exchange.

There is nevertheless a clear question here. When liberation is pictured as getting rid of a thing, does the remedy inherit the original belief in the thing? That question will become sharper with each step.

Chapter 4. The second response. An illusion that can still hurt.

Rongzom's text.

2. The second example corresponds to the Middle Way (Madhyamaka) system of the Great Vehicle (Mahāyāna). In this case, these appearances are like magical illusions because they are not substantially established as something real. However, just as an illusory poison has the capacity to produce effects, so the illusion-like polluting factors of existence produce harmful effects if they are not dealt with by skillful means, but they produce beneficial effects if they are dealt with by such means. Therefore, because such phenomena are substantially existent on the conventional level, it is claimed that they should be accepted or rejected. This is similar to claiming that although it is just a reflection of a snake, it has substantial existence in producing effects. In this system, among the four kinds of existence, the so-called ultimate existence is refuted, but so-called conventional existence and nominal existence are retained.

Commentary. Madhyamaka means Middle Way. The important words in this paragraph are conventional and ultimate. They distinguish ways of speaking about how phenomena function and whether those phenomena withstand analysis as independently established realities. Rongzom's objection is that the distinction can leave a remainder: the appearance is not ultimately real, but it still seems sufficiently real, conventionally, to demand a metaphysical response.

His illusory poison is meant to make that remainder visible. The supposed object has been demoted from ultimate substance to effective appearance. But has the structure of fear and correction really changed?

A Madhyamaka reader need not accept his account of their view. That is important. We should not congratulate Rongzom for refuting a position without checking whether the position's defenders actually hold it in the form he needs. For now, distinguish his diagnosis from a neutral definition of the Middle Way. The argument asks whether emptiness has been fully understood, or whether it has become a second explanation laid over a world that still feels solid underneath.

Chapter 5. The third response. Help from the other side.

Rongzom's text.

3. The third example corresponds to the Kriyā and Yoga outer tantras of asceticism. Here, phenomenal appearances are without any substantial existence because they are like illusions. Even though these things are without such a fault, the adepts cannot bring themselves to take action due to the power of previous fear. But they are able to summon an external divine hero to act on their behalf. This is similar to knowing that the reflection of a snake is harmless, but still not being able to touch it oneself. Among the four modes of existence, in this system the so-called ultimate existence as well as substantial existence on the conventional level are refuted, but nominal existence on the conventional level is retained. In addition to accepting only this system of two truths in common [with the Madhyamaka], they recognize that there is no substantial existence on the conventional level. It is here that the view of equality which realizes the inseparability of the ultimate and conventional is first attained, albeit to a lesser degree.

Commentary. Kriya and Yoga are categories of tantra. Here Rongzom makes the shift from an account of what things are to a relationship with a divine agent. The imagined practitioner understands the reflection is harmless but still needs someone else to handle it.

The striking feature is that he credits this approach with a first, lesser realization of the inseparability of the two truths. In this text, even an approach called outer tantra stands above the Madhyamaka position he has just described. That is his hierarchy. It is not a universal Buddhist league table.

An external divine hero sounds almost comic in the snake scene. The comedy is philosophical. A person may be able to repeat the correct account of reality while still locating freedom somewhere else, in someone else's power. Rongzom lets the image expose the distance between knowing a sentence and no longer needing protection from its supposed object.

Chapter 6. The fourth response. A victory that keeps the enemy employed.

Rongzom's text.

4. The fourth example corresponds to the view of the inner Mahāyoga. Here, being increasingly aware that all polluting factors of existence are like an illusion, adepts undertake miraculous forms of tantric conduct in order to swiftly make possible the view of equality, in a manner akin to trampling [on the illusory snake] by means of ascetic practice so as to quickly dispel even the mere fear of the snake's reflection. In this system, when belief in the nominal existence of conventional reality is mostly extinguished so that awareness is largely freed of grasping for and believing in the view of truth as twofold, the thought of the inseparability of the two truths is attained to a medium degree.

Commentary. Mahayoga is the inner tantric approach named here. Rongzom's image is deliberately physical: trampling the reflection to undo the remaining fear. We are reading a characterization of a religious approach, not instructions for tantric conduct. Nothing in this public reading authorizes anyone to imitate transgressive practices or dispense with their conditions.

The text's philosophical point can be understood without those practices. A corrective action may loosen a fixation and still preserve a subtle relation to what it corrects. The reflection remains the thing around which the action is organized.

Notice that the hierarchy is graded, not simply yes or no. The inseparability of the two truths has now been realized to a medium degree. Rongzom is describing increasing freedom from an arrangement in which conventional truth and ultimate truth stand apart as two established compartments. The last movement will question whether even the remaining arrangement needs to be held.

Chapter 7. The fifth response. No further conquest.

Rongzom's text.

5. The fifth example corresponds to the view of Great Perfection ( rdzogs chen ). In this case, there is the realization that, if the snake is illusory, then all rejection, fear, and actual trampling derive from a view based on belief in real entities. Realizing in this way that what is illusory provides no basis for making efforts, adepts neither reject anything nor try to achieve anything. In this system, the understanding of the illusion-like nature of phenomena reaches its full extent. Realizing that appearances have no defining characteristics, adepts are freed of even the most subtle beliefs in ultimate and conventional reality and are thus liberated from all metaphysical views. This is conventionally termed "the purport of basic equality," "the view of the inseparability of the ultimate and conventional".

Commentary. Great Perfection translates Dzogchen. The danger in hearing this paragraph is to replace its argument with a slogan: do nothing. The paragraph is more specific. It says that if the snake is illusory, rejection, fear, and trampling still arise from belief in real entities. The object of the criticism is that belief and the activity it compels.

Doing nothing because one is exhausted, indifferent, or avoiding responsibility is not what the argument has established. Nor does the paragraph give us a test by which we can declare ourselves realized. It describes the standpoint Rongzom places at the summit of his comparison.

There is a second trap. We can make Great Perfection into the most impressive object on the list, a treasure possessed by the cleverest person in the room. The paragraph says liberation from metaphysical views. If we turn that liberation into one more fixed metaphysical view, we reproduce the problem under a grander name. Rongzom will return to this difficulty at the end.

Chapter 8. What remains when a mistake loosens.

Rongzom's text.

Because directly perceived appearance has arisen due to the power of latent tendencies, it does not quickly disappear. Because habituation to such appearance is produced by adventitious erroneous notions, it can quickly disappear. This habituation, moreover, arises from belief in characteristics. That in turn arises from the view of real entities. But if these three conceptions cease, then even if appearance based on the essentialist conception has not ceased, there will still not arise the metaphysical view of truth as dual.

Commentary. This is one of the most useful distinctions in the work. The appearance may persist even when a fixation on it has changed. Rongzom distinguishes the lingering appearance, conditioned by latent tendencies, from the mistaken habits and views through which we invest it with defining reality.

The text does not say that recognition instantly erases everything experienced. It explicitly allows appearance to continue. That matters because otherwise we might imagine awakening as the destruction of the visible world, or treat any remaining appearance as proof that understanding has failed.

At the same time, do not turn this paragraph into a timetable for psychological recovery. Its contrast between what disappears quickly and what does not belongs to this philosophical account. It is not a promise that trauma, illness, or entrenched habits vanish on command.

The argument is moving away from the question, why is there still an image, toward the question, must the image still be taken as evidence for the view that once governed our response to it?

Chapter 9. The two truths and the furniture of the mind.

Rongzom's text.

Here, someone may wonder why Madhyamaka scriptures do not distinguish truth into two categories on the ultimate level and why Guhyamantra scriptures do not reject appearances. This requires explanation.

A person in whose mind defining characteristics of the two truths are intellectually posited as truly established and are thus determined to be objects of knowledge will never be able to relinquish dualistic mind. For when that person has determined that "the two truths are inseparable," with this deeply held belief, he also has not let go of holding that the conventional exists as mere illusion. That being so, even when he establishes the nondual nature of reality, he harbors thoughts associated with dualism.

When determining that "conventionally, things are illusion-like," they are described as "illusion-like" because the conceptual elaborations, i.e., the imputations of ultimate existence made by Śrāvaka and Yogācāra followers have been quelled. However, the Mādhyamika does not say "illusion-like" on account of things being empty of causally efficacious substances on the conventional level. Here, even at the time of making this determination, having apprehended the defining characteristics of what is substantially established on the conventional level as being real, it is then claimed that "these are not actually established as real entities." In this instance, therefore, the mind has not let go of those two modes of being, [i.e., the ultimate and conventional]. This being so, it follows that when appearance, viz., subject ( chos can ) , is posited as a defined instance ( mtshan gzhi ), so long as it dawns in our mind to remove the predicates ( chos ) of this subject or conceptual elaborations regarding this defined instance, and to thus regard appearance as merely illusory, the mind that clings to and believes in the defining characteristics of appearance has not ceased. Such a person is not deemed to be someone having the view of great equality.

Commentary. These are the densest paragraphs so far. Hear their central accusation in plain language. A thinker establishes conventional reality over here, ultimate reality over there, then announces that the two are inseparable. But if the two have already been installed as fixed objects of knowledge, does the announcement really undo the separation?

The logical vocabulary sharpens the complaint. A subject is what a claim is about. A predicate is what is claimed of it. A defined instance is the example to which a definition applies. Rongzom worries that the structure of our analysis can preserve a thing-like appearance as its subject even while stripping reality from the predicates attached to it.

Here is a modern explanatory comparison. Imagine putting two boxes on a table and writing inseparable across both lids. The word alone does not remove the boxes. That is not a Tibetan analogy or a quotation. It is a way of hearing his charge that an intellectual determination can retain the very division it says it has overcome.

Whether this charge succeeds against a particular Madhyamaka author requires reading that author. The present work gives us Rongzom's challenge, not the last word in every Buddhist debate about the two truths.

Chapter 10. A medicine need not become a monument.

Rongzom's text.

In this regard, determining objects of knowledge by fixating the mind on the subdivision of the two truths was declared to be a remedy for people who cling excessively to real entities. However, the essence of phenomena is without dualistic defining characteristics. Indeed, one in whom the belief in defining characteristics has ceased is free from such clinging and therefore no longer craves or wishes for anything that appears. In that instance, the term "view of great equality" is employed.

Commentary. This paragraph keeps the polemic from becoming a simple rejection of analysis. The division into two truths has a remedial use. It can work against excessive attachment to real entities. The problem begins when the remedy itself becomes an enduring object of fixation.

That gives us a useful way to reread the entire sequence. The earlier approaches are not necessarily useless. They are being evaluated by the traces of fixation Rongzom thinks they retain. The hierarchy is a claim about the completion of a remedy, not simply a demand that nobody ever use one.

This also prevents a lazy appeal to the highest view. Refusing every distinction before it has done any work is not automatically freedom from distinction. Sometimes it is merely a way of protecting the confusion a distinction might have exposed. Rongzom's own decision to argue at length is evidence that explanation still has a place in this text.

Chapter 11. The child who was never there.

Rongzom's text.

An additional question: "Isn't mere appearance the conventional reality?" [Reply:] This is what was pointed out above with respect to any person who believes appearance to be conventional and believes, in the back of his mind, that freedom from conceptual elaborations regarding that is the ultimate. Still, for a mind that does not believe in the reality of the two truths, to ask whether they are one or two is like asking whether the son of a barren woman is blue or white, as is cited in scripture.

Commentary. The son of a barren woman is a traditional impossible example. Asking whether he is blue or white grants the very subject whose existence the example excludes. Rongzom uses the image to question a demand that the two truths must finally be either one or two.

Do not hear this as a trick for escaping any difficult question. It works only if the question does smuggle in a false premise. The burden is to show that the premise is mistaken. Otherwise a colorful refusal would replace an argument.

His point here is that a mind free from belief in two established realities cannot be adequately described by choosing how those two established realities are related. The demand for a final arrangement may itself be the fixation under discussion.

Chapter 12. The ladder refuses a throne.

Rongzom's text.

A further question: "What, then, does your tradition establish?" We only repudiate your inferior metaphysical views without establishing any superior point of our own. In customary usage, this is called "the view of great equality" even though there is no belief whatsoever in any metaphysical view.

Commentary. This ending is sharper than a comforting declaration that everything is one. Rongzom has not replaced many entities with one supreme entity. He denies establishing a superior metaphysical point of his own. Great equality is a customary designation, not a newly discovered substance.

Does that resolve every tension in the text? No. There is an obvious difficulty in mounting a hierarchy of views whose summit disavows any metaphysical view. We should leave the difficulty visible. One possible reading is that the hierarchy is itself a teaching arrangement whose work ends when its organizing fixation ends. That is our interpretive proposal. It is not a sentence secretly added to Rongzom's conclusion.

The final exchange also changes the opening image. At first, we may have wanted to become the fifth person, the one with the best response to the reflection. By the end, even that identity can look like another thing to defend. The discourse does not hand us a medal for belonging to the winning school. It questions the need to secure freedom by establishing something to stand on.

We have now read the complete Black Snake Discourse in Higgins's translation. We have not read every footnote aloud, and we have not settled its textual or philosophical disputes. The written source gives the translator's apparatus and bibliographical references.

Our next work takes up an apparent reversal. If there is no superior metaphysical position to establish, how can Rongzom write a treatise called Establishing Appearances as Divine? If the reflection needs no conquest, why speak of a world as a mandala? Does divine mean another reality behind this one, an idealized picture placed over it, or a different way of understanding the very appearances we already encounter?

The next reading matters because easy answers to those questions would undo the precision of this one. Calling everything illusion can become a way of dismissing what hurts. Calling everything divine can become a way of decorating what we have not understood. Rongzom's second text will not let either formula rest for long.
