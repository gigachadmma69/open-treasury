# The Woman Who Kept the Future

## Yeshe Tsogyal, Wisdom, and the Courage to Ask

The Open Treasury | Narrated by Tara | Source companion | 19 November 2026

This original 24-chapter course studies Yeshe Tsogyal as questioner, receiver, transmitter, teacher and wisdom figure. Its argument is that receiving can be an active, intelligent, future-making form of authority. It also asks why honoring a feminine religious principle does not automatically protect actual women.

The course is a sourced introduction and critical reflection, not an exhaustive account of the Tibetan corpus, a full reading of the commercial biographies, an initiation, or a manual for advanced practice. It includes a non-graphic discussion of sexual violence and coercion. Listeners can skip chapter 12. No listener is asked to imitate suffering, forgive an assailant, accept sexual contact, or remain in an unsafe relationship.

## Course route

1. A Question Changes the Room. An original imagined scene, not ancient reported dialogue.
2. The Person, the Lives, and the Living Presence. Historical limits, literary lives, religious presence.
3. The Life She Was Supposed to Want. Marriage, renunciation and differing narrative routes.
4. Receiving Is an Activity. Dialogue and intelligent discipleship.
5. The Price of Being Allowed to Learn. Humility, inferiority rhetoric and access.
6. A Teaching for an Actual Life. Labor, age and the conditions of learning.
7. Could This Go Wrong? Error, nihilism and attachment to a view.
8. The Memory That Belongs to the Future. Traditional perfect recall and responsible preservation.
9. Devotion Without Disappearing. Trust, discrimination and coercion.
10. What the Word Consort Conceals. Specific histories rather than one universal verdict.
11. The Authority to Refuse. A comparison with the later woman Sera Khandro.
12. A Story Survivors Do Not Have to Imitate. Non-graphic bandit narrative and explicit safeguards.
13. Austerity Is Not a Competition. Hardship is not a credential.
14. When Two Women Meet. Yeshe Tsogyal and Mandarava, with textual witnesses distinguished.
15. After the Master Leaves. Teacher and transmitter in her own right.
16. A Secret Is Not the Same as Enforced Silence. Privacy, preparation and accountability.
17. Someone Who Has Not Been Born Yet. Treasure tradition and concern for future recipients.
18. Whose Voice Are We Hearing? Attributed speech, mediation and interpretation.
19. Compassion Beyond the Pleasing Story. Care without erasing harm or practical help.
20. Mother Without a Compulsory Life. Maternal imagery without a compulsory social role.
21. The Sky-Goer Is Not Our Projection. The range of dakini and khandroma meanings.
22. How Would We Recognize Wisdom? Spiritual claims and ordinary accountability.
23. The Women Who Keep the Pages. Reception, a nun's journey and women's editorial work.
24. Keep the Future Open. Return to the question and the work of attention.

## Claims and boundaries

History and sacred biography are different inquiries. No precise lifespan is certified. An absence of secure contemporary evidence does not prove that no historical woman existed. Later narratives are not treated as imperial eyewitness reports. Different versions are not silently combined into one story.

The source base is strongest for literary history, traditional biographical motifs, question-and-answer literature, and the tension between theological female authority and women's social conditions. Miracles, perfect recall, visions, journeys to hell, treasure concealment and superhuman accomplishments are labeled as claims of traditional accounts.

Everyday scenes and ethical judgments are original applications. They are not passed off as translations, direct sayings of Yeshe Tsogyal, a universal historical Buddhist position, or a replacement for lineage-specific teaching. Neither sacred status nor a reported realization establishes consent, exempts a person from accountability, or requires a survivor to provide care to an assailant.

No breath, gaze, posture, subtle-energy, sexual-yoga, initiation, mantra, deity-visualization or advanced retreat instructions are given. Descriptive study of religious literature is not practical authorization.

## Sources and reading route

### 1. Jue Liang, Conceiving the Mother of Tibet

The Life, Lives, and Afterlife of the Buddhist Saint Yeshe Tsogyel. University of Virginia doctoral dissertation, 2020. This is the main scholarly source. The complete official PDF was obtained, but only the following sections were read for this course: title, abstract and contents; printed pp.47-70; 122-130; 162-189; 241-256; and 284-293. Downloading the full dissertation is not a claim of reading every page. Earlier source descriptions may call the last range an epilogue opening; the epilogue was subsequently read through its end.

These sections support the development and variation of the lives, early historical references and absences, dialogues as scripture and performance, female-inferiority rhetoric and access, ideal consortship, the Mandarava encounter, the range and religious interpretation of khandroma, and later reception at Larung Gar. Chapter 23 uses Liang's reported encounter with a nun and her description of the fifty-three-volume editorial project. It is a report in research completed in 2020, not a claim about current conditions or a claim that we read the whole collection. Most of the dissertation, Tibetan primary editions and appendices were not independently studied.

https://libraetd.lib.virginia.edu/public_view/k3569480f

### 2. Janet Gyatso, Yeshe Tsogyal

Encyclopedia of Religion, 2005, hosted by Encyclopedia.com. Entire entry and bibliography read. Supports historical cautions, the development of the full lives, the later Taksham narrative and its bandit episode. The entry's precise traditional dates are not adopted as verified chronology. Its bibliography's older date for the genealogy article is not used; more recent sources cite that article as 2006. The genealogy article itself was not independently obtained and read for this course.

https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ye-shes-mtsho-rgyal-yeshe-tsogyal

### 3. Jamgon Kongtrul, The Tambura's Yearning Song of Devotion

A Prayer Summarizing Khandro Yeshe Tsogyal's Life and Liberation. Complete English prayer, colophon and notes read. Samye Translations, 2017; translated by Stefan Mang with reference to the Padmakara biography and Richard Barron's translation of the prayer; edited by Libby Hogg. Version 1.3, 9 March 2023. The colophon identifies its basis in Samten Lingpa's treasure biography and its composition in 1893.

A principal traditional witness for study, perfect recall, Nepal, austerities, teaching, treasure, compassionate activity, the later Mandarava exchange and departure. It does not narrate the bandit violence in the same detail as the later life described by Gyatso. The prayer is not evidence independently verifying its events. Neither the full source biography nor Barron's whole book is claimed read. No practice sequence or translated recitation is included.

https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/jamgon-kongtrul/tamburas-yearning-song-of-devotion

### 4. Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro, The Delightful Play of Sarasvati

In Praise of Yeshe Tsogyal, Foremost of Dakinis, Queen of Space. Complete English text and colophon read. Translated by Adam Pearcey, 2022, with Khyentse Foundation and Terton Sogyal Trust support. Version 1.1, 17 September 2024. Supports the praise tradition's theological register, idealized feminine imagery and representation of teacher and transmitter. Not an independent historical witness.

https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/jamyang-khyentse-chokyi-lodro/praise-to-yeshe-tsogyal

### 5. Do Dasal Wangmo, Tsogyal's Liberational Life in Brief

Complete English text, colophon and note read. Translated by Adam Pearcey, 2023. Version 1.0, 11 March 2023. A female author's devotional representation of realized teacher, guide and three-kaya figure. The Tibetan edition cited by the translator was not independently translated. A short devotional life is not the full Tibetan biographical corpus.

https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/do-dasal-wangmo/brief-yeshe-tsogyal-life

### 6. The Golden Garland of Nectar: Questions and Answers

From the Khandro Nyingtik. Translated by Adam Pearcey with Tsadra Foundation support, 2026. Version 1.1, 6 November 2026. Read the complete translator introduction and notes; the opening through essence, nature, compassion and view; the first meditation discussion; and the final revelation frame, colophon, bibliography and notes. Middle sections were not read in full and are not represented as such.

Used for the literary question about error, the warning against nihilistic disregard for conduct, and attachment to one's own view. The translator notes a near-identical text with different interlocutors in Tsongkhapa/Lhodrak Namkha Gyaltsen collections, uncertain subdivisions and provisional translation. We do not decide the direction of borrowing or certify eighth-century authorship. No advanced instruction, consort criteria or prophecy is taught.

https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/pema-ledrel-tsal/golden-garland-questions-answers

### 7. Holly Gayley, Revisiting the Secret Consort

Revisiting the Secret Consort (gsang yum) in Tibetan Buddhism. Religions 9(6), 179, 2018. Read abstract, introduction, sections 1-3, and section 4 up to the fetched truncation during discussion of Dzongsar Khyentse's response. The ending and complete note apparatus were not read. Used for historical variation, public consort roles, partnerships, women's experience, secrecy and accountability. Its dated reporting is not presented as current news. Neither a universal defense nor universal condemnation of every relationship follows from it.

https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/9/6/179

### 8. Sarah H. Jacoby, She Said No

She Said No: Toward a Survivor-Centered History of Vajrayana Buddhist Sexuality. Journal of the American Academy of Religion 92(1), 2024. Read abstract and opening; Silences, Stereotypes, and Critiques; Sera Khandro as a Subject of Feminist Historiography; Buddhist Technologies of Sexuality; the opening of Winter Flowers through the conversation with Dorje Zungma; and the captured Not On Your Life section through its analysis of the proposed longevity liaison and the difficulty of having a refusal heard. That capture ends mid-analysis. The remaining article was not read in full.

Supports the particular point that Sera Khandro could value certain tantric relationships, refuse others, and criticize religious sexual misconduct. It is not evidence that every woman had the same experience. Sera Khandro is explicitly a later historical comparison, not Yeshe Tsogyal speaking. No technical practice descriptions are reproduced.

https://academic.oup.com/jaar/article/92/1/110/7762837
