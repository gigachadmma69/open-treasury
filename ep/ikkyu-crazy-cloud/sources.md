# The Monk Who Wouldn't Behave
## Ikkyu, Crazy Cloud, and the difference between freedom and an alibi
14 November 2026

An original nine-chapter critical course about Ikkyu Sojun, the Zen monk, poet and calligrapher. Its question is the difference between freedom and self-excuse. It considers serious training, criticism of religious appearance, literary paradox, love, grief, war and responsibility for a damaged institution.

This is not a complete reading or translation of the Crazy Cloud Anthology, a definitive biography, or a practice transmission. Selected passages and commentaries were read for this course, not Ikkyu's whole corpus. No restricted practice is taught. Irregular conduct is not presented as a method of awakening or an exemption from consent, care or accountability.

## How to hear the claims
The course distinguishes surviving objects, historical accounts, traditional biography, poems and the narrator's interpretation. The story of imperial parentage is marked uncertain. The crow awakening is described as a traditional account, not an independently observable event. Later folk tales are not silently treated as contemporary biography. The voice of a poem is not a complete record of the poet's conduct, and the poems do not give us other people's private thoughts or consent.

The organizing argument about freedom and an alibi is our reading. The analogies about the sword, musical training, ladders, appearances and responsibility are original commentary, not newly discovered Ikkyu sayings. The closing definition of freedom is the narrator's formulation.

## Sources and editions

### Surviving artworks and institutional records
- Nara National Museum, *Portrait of the Priest Ikkyu Sojun*, accession 505-0, dated 1447. Ikkyu's own inscription; red-lacquered sheath with wooden/bamboo sword. The museum's interpretation of criticism directed at outward appearances supplies the opening image. https://www.narahaku.go.jp/english/collection/v-505-0.html
- Tokyo National Museum, ColBase, *Transmission Outside the Teachings, Unattainable through Words*, B-2944, fifteenth-century calligraphy. The museum separates later Edo childhood stories from the historical monk and explains the words on this surviving object. https://colbase.nich.go.jp/collection_items/tnm/B-2944?locale=en
- Metropolitan Museum of Art, *Discussion of Buddhist Principles by Tetto Giko*, transcribed by Ikkyu, late fifteenth century, object 2023.583.6. The catalog's English translation is credited to Tim T. Zhang. The course describes, but does not recite, this text on renunciation, consequences and sustained effort. https://www.metmuseum.org/en/art/collection/search/897102
- Cleveland Museum of Art, *Calligraphy with Willow and Swallows*, 1400s, accession 2015.463. The museum describes variation of script, ink and reduced strokes; these are artistic observations, not proof of spiritual attainment. https://www.clevelandart.org/print/art/2015.463
- MOA Museum of Art, *Calligraphy*, fifteenth century. Corroborates Ikkyu's abbacy, criticism of fame-seeking authorities and the Onin War's 1467–1477 dates. https://www.moaart.or.jp/en/collections/106/

### Biography and context
- James Hugh Sanford, “Ikkyu Sojun,” *Encyclopedia of Religion*, 1987, with revised bibliography, hosted by Encyclopedia.com. The signed entry is the main biographical source: early literary/scriptural education; Ken-o from 1410 and death in 1414; Kaso near Lake Biwa; traditional 1420 awakening; criticism of Yoso; Mori; appointment at Daitokuji in 1474 and rebuilding with Sakai support. Sanford's full 1981 book was not read for this course. https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ikkyu-sojun
- JAANUS, “Ikkyu,” hosted by Columbia University. Art-historical reference for the literary/art circle, crow account, and growth of later folk material. The page contains date inconsistencies about Ken-o and the Onin War; those dates were not used. Conflicting accounts about certification and doubtful attributions of Noh plays were excluded rather than resolved by guesswork. https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/record/ikkyu
- Sonja Arntzen, 2022 lecture announcement, Glorisun Global Network and UBC Asian Studies. The abstract explicitly discusses Ikkyu's late-war poems and refugee experience. The announcement was read; the lecture was not watched. https://glorisunglobalnetwork.org/guest-lecture-sonja-arntzen/
- *Having Once Paused: Poems of Zen Master Ikkyu (1394–1481)*, University of Michigan Press, 2015, publisher's summary and table of contents through Project MUSE. Contextual corroboration only. The restricted full text was not accessed and no translated poem was taken from this edition. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/40959

### Poetry and close critical reading
Sonja Elaine Arntzen, *The Poetry of the Kyounshu “Crazy Cloud Anthology” of Ikkyu Sojun*, University of British Columbia doctoral dissertation, 1979. The university's own open collection was used for noncommercial research and education. Selected introduction passages and poem commentaries were consulted, not the entire dissertation. The dissertation translates one hundred poems, not the complete anthology.

University record: https://open.library.ubc.ca/soa/cIRcle/collections/ubctheses/831/items/1.0100255

DOI: https://doi.org/10.14288/1.0100255

Relevant printed-page locators, which may differ from the PDF viewer's page count:
- Pages 1–5: opening prose/poem and analysis of ethics, nonduality and the final drunken-song reversal. The course's only direct modern translated quotation is the six-word line, “Students who ignore karma are sunk,” credited aloud to Arntzen. Its complicating final turn is discussed rather than suppressed.
- Pages 12–20: anticlerical poems, Yoso, literary drinking and desire, Mori, and the illness poem's self-questioning about cause and effect. Illness is not presented as proof of wrongdoing.
- Pages 21–28: institutions, literary formation, audience, Chinese allusion and poetic innovation.
- Pages 255–258, anthology poems 388–390: the response to a book-burning monk, remaining inner attachment, fire and regrowing grass. Discussed in original critical prose, not read as translated poems.
- Pages 259–260, poem 394: soldiers killed in war.
- Pages 263–268, poems 493/494 and notes: bamboo cultivation, the Linji pine-planting allusion, and the shaded retreat.
- Pages 271–272, poem 544 and notes: Mori's nap and the butterfly/Zhuangzi allusion.
- Pages 281–285, poems 604/605 and notes: flight and temporary refuges in 1469; Du Fu references.
- Pages 287–288, poems 639–641: famine in 1461 and prewar calamity.
- Pages 289–291, poem 647 and notes: soldiers, peace and war, the “no mind” reversal, and the discrepancy between a dated poem introduction and the biographical chronicle. No prophecy claim is made.
- Pages 295–296, poem 715 and prose introduction: the sparrow, grief, funeral observances, formal name and Buddha parinirvana imagery. This is the basis for chapter 6; no translated verse is copied.
