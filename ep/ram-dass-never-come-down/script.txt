# The Man Who Wanted Never to Come Down
## Ram Dass, Neem Karoli Baba, and the Work of Coming Back

### 01. The man who wanted never to come down

Imagine discovering that the life you have spent years building might be a costume. Your profession, your impressive opinions, the name on your door. For a few hours, all of it falls away. You feel a freedom you didn't know was possible. And then the ordinary mind returns. Someone wants the rent. Your body wants breakfast. You are annoyed with exactly the same person you were annoyed with yesterday.

What do you do next?

That question joins two very different lives. Richard Alpert was an American psychologist, a Harvard faculty member, and a participant in the psychedelic upheaval of the nineteen sixties. Neem Karoli Baba was an Indian Hindu teacher, remembered by his devotees as Maharajji. The American would become famous as Ram Dass. The Indian teacher would become known to many Western readers through him. But Maharajji was not a supporting actor waiting in India for an American to arrive. He had a devotional world of his own, with Indian devotees, temples, relationships, and a religious language that cannot be reduced to a Western search for an extraordinary experience.

This course follows their meeting and the difficult question it opened. What if the point of awakening is not to stay above the world? What if the point is to return without closing your heart?

We will move between history and interpretation. Some things are documented public events: a dismissal from Harvard, a book published in nineteen seventy-one, an organization founded to serve people, a stroke. Other things come to us as remembered encounters, devotional stories, and claims about miracles. Those are different kinds of evidence. We will not pretend that a story becomes a controlled experiment because its teller once taught psychology. And we will not pretend that a story has nothing to teach unless it passes as a controlled experiment.

The distinction matters most when the story is beautiful. Beauty can help us listen. It can also make us lower our guard.

There are three movements in what follows. First, the search: why success, drugs, and even dramatic insight did not settle Alpert's problem. Second, the encounter: Maharajji, Hanuman, devotion, and the astonishing possibility of being loved without first becoming presentable. Third, the return: fame, service, mistakes, disability, and death. We will come back to each strand through the others. The guru's blanket will meet the professor's credentials. The idea of boundless love will meet a hospital, an ethical boundary, and an ordinary task that somebody still has to do.

You do not have to accept a guru, use a divine name, believe a miracle, or decide what happens after death to follow this course. You do have to consider a less comfortable possibility: that an experience of unity might leave the work of becoming trustworthy almost entirely unfinished.

Ram Dass could make a room laugh at the elaborate business of being somebody. Neem Karoli Baba, in the stories his devotees preserve, could undo that business with a glance, a joke, or a demand that people be fed. The temptation is to make either man into an escape hatch. Our task will be harder and more interesting. We will ask what their lives reveal when escape stops being the goal.

### 02. A very successful costume

Before Ram Dass there was Richard Alpert, born in Massachusetts in nineteen thirty-one, into a prosperous Jewish family. He studied at Tufts, Wesleyan, and Stanford, and built a career in psychology. By the early nineteen sixties, he was teaching at Harvard. This was not someone shut out of the respectable world. He had gained entry to one of its most convincing rooms.

The later Ram Dass repeatedly described a gap between achievement and the feeling of being at home in his life. He had status, professional skill, possessions, and appetites. What he did not have, in the story he came to tell, was a satisfying answer to what any of it was for.

We should be precise about this. A person's later account of his younger self is already an interpretation. Once a life has acquired a dramatic turning point, the years before it tend to look like a long preparation. Everything begins pointing toward the guru. Actual lives are less tidy. Alpert's academic work was real work, and his dissatisfaction does not prove that psychology is empty. It tells us something about the person who was doing it.

His problem is recognizable without a Harvard appointment. You can be excellent at a role and uncertain about the being who performs it. You can become very good at earning approval without finding a place where you can stop earning it.

That distinction helps explain why the later message of being here could reach people far outside the counterculture. The target was not employment, ambition, or thought itself. It was the unnoticed bargain beneath them: once I have secured the right description of myself, I will finally be allowed to exist without anxiety.

The trouble is that descriptions need defending. The intelligent person must avoid appearing foolish. The generous person must never feel selfish. The spiritual person must appear unusually calm. The costume becomes a small security operation.

Ram Dass would later make that operation visible through humor. His own vanity, lust, and fear became material. This was part of his appeal. He could discuss spiritual life without sounding as though he had been born sitting serenely on a mountain. But self-disclosure has limits. Telling a funny story about a mistake is not the same as repairing its effects. We will need that distinction when the professor becomes a teacher himself.

For now, notice the first connection with Maharajji. In Ram Dass's remembered experience, the guru's importance was not simply that he supplied a better identity. It was that he seemed to love the person whose identities kept changing. The professor could be successful or disgraced, skeptical or devoted, and the relationship did not appear to depend on winning the next round.

But Alpert did not reach that relationship by deciding it would be healthy to feel accepted. He reached it through a more hazardous route. The self he had learned to inhabit became, under psychedelics, something he could watch dissolve.

That discovery felt like an answer. It was really the beginning of a larger question. If the costume can disappear for an afternoon, why does it fit so tightly again the next morning?

### 03. The laboratory and its shadow

At Harvard, Alpert worked with Timothy Leary on the exploration of psychedelic substances and human consciousness. The project included psilocybin, the psychoactive substance found in certain mushrooms. Their interest belonged partly to psychology and partly to an increasingly expansive hope: perhaps changing consciousness could change human life at its roots.

The excitement is easy to understand. If someone's sense of self, perception, or emotional world can change dramatically, our everyday picture of the mind is incomplete. But an exciting question does not excuse weak methods or broken boundaries.

Harvard's own history describes serious concerns about the rigor and safety of the work. Researchers sometimes participated while under the influence themselves. Colleagues questioned controls and subject selection. There was an agreement not to involve undergraduates. Alpert was dismissed in nineteen sixty-three after giving psilocybin to an undergraduate off campus.

This is a place where the legend often edits too quickly. In the heroic version, a frightened establishment expels a visionary for discovering too much. There may be real institutional resistance in the history of unconventional research. But that cannot erase the specific obligations of a teacher and researcher. Access to an intense experience does not cancel duties toward the person receiving it.

Nor should we make the opposite mistake and assume that a broken research program proves every question it raised was worthless. Scientific value and ethical conduct need separate examination. Both matter.

A newer historical reflection from Harvard's Center for the Study of World Religions helps widen the frame. It draws attention to Lisa Bieberman, a Radcliffe graduate who did substantial organizing and information work in the psychedelic community. Her account challenges a history populated almost entirely by charismatic men. She also pressed a moral question: what did the experience contribute to a person's conduct and community?

That question runs directly toward the later Ram Dass. A transformed state is not yet a transformed life.

After Harvard, Alpert and Leary continued their explorations, including at Millbrook in New York. In Alpert's own retrospective accounts, repeated attempts to remain in an altered state kept ending in the same frustration. He came down. He could experience an opening, but he could not simply install it as his permanent address.

We do not need to repeat the quantities in his dramatic drug stories to understand the problem. And those stories are not instructions. This course does not recommend taking psychedelics, trying to imitate historical experiments, or treating a spiritual anecdote as medical guidance.

The philosophical problem is more durable than the chemical details. A state has a beginning and an end. If freedom means preserving one particular state forever, then the wish for freedom can become another form of grasping. You become dependent on the feeling of not being dependent.

That is the trap the title of this course points toward: the man who wanted never to come down. The search began with the possibility that consciousness could open. It matured only when opening had to be connected with responsibility, relationship, and the life that remained after the extraordinary moment.

Before that maturation, there was India. There was grief. And there was an old man who, in Alpert's telling, knew where the grief lived.

### 04. A meeting told through grief

In nineteen sixty-seven, Alpert traveled to India. Through the seeker Bhagavan Das, he came into the circle of Neem Karoli Baba. The encounter would become one of the central stories in modern American spiritual culture. It needs to be heard both sympathetically and with its source clearly in view.

What follows is Ram Dass's account, preserved in the archive of his teachings. It is not an independently verified reconstruction of every exchange.

He remembered himself as exhausted, skeptical, and ready to go home. His mother had died. During the journey toward the mountains, he spent time outside beneath the stars thinking about her. He said he did not tell the others. The next day he met Maharajji, who was seated with people around him, wrapped in a blanket.

The first exchanges, as Ram Dass told them, did not meet his expectations of spiritual refinement. Maharajji asked about the car and money. Alpert felt suspicious, defensive, and exposed. He was not arriving as an eager believer who immediately recognized his master. His remembered reaction included the fear of being hustled.

Then, according to the story, Maharajji referred to his being outside the night before, to his thoughts about his mother, and to the illness associated with her death. Alpert experienced this as knowledge that the guru could not ordinarily have possessed. His defenses broke. He cried. What remained central in his later telling was an immense feeling of being known and loved.

There are at least three questions here, and they should not be forced into one.

Did Maharajji have paranormal knowledge? The story itself cannot settle that for us. It is a remembered account, translated across language, told by a deeply affected participant, and repeated in a devotional setting.

Did Alpert experience the encounter as life-changing? His later life, teaching, and repeated testimony give strong grounds for saying that he did.

What can the story mean to someone who does not accept its supernatural interpretation? It can still reveal the difference between being analyzed and feeling received. The academic expert arrived prepared to observe. He remembered finding himself unable to remain only the observer.

A skeptical listener may say that grief made him unusually susceptible. A devotee may say that grace met him exactly where he was vulnerable. Those are not interchangeable explanations. We should leave their disagreement visible rather than producing a comforting fog in which everybody is secretly saying the same thing.

What is clear is the reorientation. Alpert did not merely add an interesting teacher to his collection of experiences. Maharajji became the center of a relationship that continued after travel, distance, and eventually death.

This is why the story cannot be reduced to a spiritual magic trick. If its whole value were that the guru knew a private fact, the question would be settled by confirming or disproving that fact. For Ram Dass, the knowledge opened a much larger possibility: perhaps the parts of himself he concealed did not make him unlovable.

But to understand what he entered, we now have to stop following only the American. Who was this teacher, before becoming the answer to an American's problem?

### 05. The teacher outside the American story

Neem Karoli Baba is also known as Neeb Karori Baba. Devotees commonly call him Maharajji, a respectful honorific. The name Neem Karoli connects him with a place, not with a doctrine he invented for Western visitors.

The record available to an English-language listener is uneven. It contains recollections, devotional biographies, temple histories, photographs, and later interpretations. It does not give us a complete, independently documented account of every year of his early life. A responsible course should not manufacture that completeness.

We can still identify the world in which his presence mattered. It was a Hindu devotional world, especially associated with Hanuman, with temples and ashrams in northern India and a community that existed beyond its foreign visitors. Kainchi and Vrindavan recur in accounts of that community. People came for many reasons: devotion, consolation, guidance, blessing, food, and the hope of relief from trouble.

One story explains the teacher's name through a train that would not move after he had been put off it. In the account preserved by the Taos ashram, railway officials finally invite him back, and the train resumes its journey. The story gives the holy man a power worldly officials cannot command. It also insists that someone dismissed as socially insignificant must be treated with respect.

That is an interpretation of a devotional story, not a claim that we have verified a railway miracle. The distinction lets us attend to what the story is doing without pretending to know more than our evidence allows.

Maharajji is often remembered as elusive, humorous, abrupt, affectionate, and difficult to contain in a formal teaching system. Ram Dass's account from a nineteen seventy-three lecture describes a teacher who moved between places and disrupted the devotees' attempts to hold him in a predictable role. Even in that loving portrait, the relationship was not uninterrupted sweetness. Ram Dass remembered frustration and bewilderment alongside bliss.

Notice the risk. Once unpredictability becomes a mark of holiness, it can be used to excuse anything. We must not conclude that confusing behavior proves wisdom. The historical fact is that devotees interpreted Maharajji's behavior within a relationship of devotion. Whether another person's behavior deserves the same interpretation is a separate question, requiring evidence and ordinary safeguards.

The teachings most commonly associated with Maharajji are strikingly concrete: love, service, truthfulness, remembrance of God. They can look almost too simple beside elaborate spiritual philosophies. But simplicity in wording is not simplicity in practice. It is possible to discuss boundless awareness for an hour and avoid a hungry person for the entire hour.

A guru remembered through feeding people asks something awkward of the seeker. How much of your hunger for enlightenment is a wish never again to be bothered by the needs of others?

The next part of the course turns to the figure who helps explain this combination of devotion and action. If Maharajji is the living center of the story for Ram Dass, Hanuman is one of its deepest symbolic roots.

### 06. Hanuman, or strength that does not belong to itself

Hanuman is a central figure in the devotional world surrounding Neem Karoli Baba. In the Ramayana tradition, he is the devoted servant and ally of Rama. He is remembered for strength, courage, intelligence, and extraordinary action. The point is not that devotion makes him helpless. It gives his powers a direction.

This matters because modern spiritual language can make surrender sound like becoming vague and passive. Hanuman offers a very different image. Capacity is not abolished. It is put at the service of something beyond self-display.

The Victoria and Albert Museum's account of Hanuman emphasizes both his abilities and his devotion to Rama. Sculptures can show power and humility in the same figure. To a contemporary eye, that combination is almost a challenge. We are accustomed to imagining humility as weakness, or power as proof that humility has been outgrown.

For our course, Hanuman supplies a bridge between Maharajji's devotional world and the later service work associated with Ram Dass. If every spiritual opening ends by improving the private magnificence of the person who had it, something essential has been missed. Strength becomes more interesting when it is not constantly asking to be admired.

The name Ram Dass is usually translated as servant of God. It can also be heard more specifically through devotion to Ram, or Rama. A name like this points away from being the owner of a spiritual achievement. It does not certify that its bearer always lives up to it. It names a direction of practice.

Here the course needs a cultural correction. Hanuman is not an archetype invented to help Western people overcome ego problems. He is worshipped within living religious traditions. People pray, sing, make offerings, visit temples, and participate in inherited forms of devotion. Psychological interpretation can be useful, but it is not a replacement for those forms or for the people who sustain them.

A listener who does not share Hindu belief can still learn from this without claiming to have extracted the real meaning from an outdated shell. We can say: this tradition understands devotion in ways that challenge my assumption that freedom must mean needing nobody. That is a respectful encounter. It leaves room for the tradition to mean more than what I can immediately use.

Maharajji's devotees have often associated him very closely with Hanuman. We should describe that as their devotional understanding, not as a historical identification that a biography can prove. Again, the boundary protects both honesty and the seriousness of belief.

Return now to the American who wanted a permanent opening of consciousness. The Hanuman model changes the question. Instead of asking how I can remain in the highest state, I might ask what my abilities are for when the state changes. Instead of asking whether I am enlightened, I might ask whether I can carry what needs carrying without turning the whole scene into a referendum on my importance.

That is not a smaller ambition. It is an ambition with the spotlight moved.

And it prepares us for bhakti, the path in which love is not a decorative emotion added to knowledge, but a way of knowing and living in its own right.

### 07. Why love embarrassed the expert

Bhakti is often translated as devotion. In the world we are exploring, it includes a loving orientation toward the divine, expressed through remembrance, worship, relationship, and service. It is not merely having a tender feeling. Nor is it identical with obedience to a human authority.

The distinction is important. A person can feel powerful devotion and still need to ask whether an institution is honest, whether a teacher respects boundaries, and whether someone has been harmed.

Why might this path unsettle an intellectual? Because it does not let the knowing self remain safely outside the material. You can explain love with great sophistication and still be unwilling to receive it. You can analyze surrender while preserving a firm private contract that you will only surrender after you have guaranteed the outcome.

For Ram Dass, Maharajji repeatedly became the place where that contract failed. In his accounts, the relationship did not function primarily as a series of propositions he could master. He remembered a quality of presence and affection that changed his relation to himself.

But love here should not be confused with endless affirmation. A relationship can be warm and still frustrate the person's preferred self-image. Ram Dass described moments when his bliss was interrupted by practical questions. The interruption is revealing. An experience of boundlessness may be beautiful, but someone still has a body and a bill and a responsibility.

This gives us a useful way to distinguish devotion from intoxication. Intoxication, in the broader sense, wants more of the feeling and less of whatever interrupts it. Devotion can learn to include the interruption in the relationship. The meal has to be prepared. The visitor needs attention. You are still capable of vanity while thinking very elevated thoughts.

In the tradition around Maharajji, remembrance is one way of returning. A divine name can gather attention when the mind disperses. It can be spoken, sung, or held inwardly. Kirtan, the singing of sacred names, is often practiced in call and response. Krishna Das, another American devotee, describes learning that practice in northern India and later bringing his own musical expression of it to Western audiences.

We are discussing that practice, not borrowing a commercial recording or pretending to transmit a secret formula. You can understand its shape without performing it. Attention wanders, the name returns. Emotion changes, the rhythm continues. Many voices participate, and the center does not have to be a lecture by the cleverest person in the room.

There is an intellectual modesty in that. Thought is welcome, but it no longer holds every office.

There is also a danger if devotion becomes a way to avoid difficult feelings or independent judgment. Repeating a sacred name does not make a financial demand reasonable. Feeling love for a teacher does not make a complaint disloyal. These are not cynical additions from outside spiritual life. They are requirements for keeping love from becoming a tool of control.

Soon, the problem becomes sharper. Ram Dass told a story about Maharajji and LSD that seems almost designed to overwhelm ordinary judgment. We need to see why it mattered to him and why it cannot carry the weight of proof.

### 08. The beloved with more than one face

One of the most revealing pieces in the Ram Dass archive concerns Maharajji speaking about Jesus. It comes through Ram Dass's recollection in a nineteen eighty-nine teaching, not through a transcript made at the original conversation. That distance is worth knowing before we interpret it.

In the account, Maharajji presents Jesus as absorbed in love, serving others, and perceiving the divine in them. He brings Christ and Hanuman into a shared devotional field. The effect is to interrupt a narrow idea that love must belong to one religious community alone.

We need to be careful about what kind of statement this is. It is Maharajji as remembered by a Hindu devotee, speaking about Jesus through a devotional understanding. It is not a neutral historical account of Christianity. Christians themselves hold diverse and specific views about the identity of Jesus, salvation, and the meaning of the crucifixion. Those differences do not disappear because a Hindu teacher offers a generous interpretation.

But the generosity is still important. Within this remembered teaching, Maharajji does not need the foreign sacred figure to be an enemy. He recognizes a pattern of self-giving love. That recognition helps explain why Ram Dass's later teaching could feel hospitable to people with different religious backgrounds.

There is a temptation to simplify this into a slogan: all religions are the same. That is not what our evidence allows us to conclude. A more careful possibility is that a person can recognize holiness across a boundary without denying the boundary or mastering the other's theology.

This lets us distinguish religious hospitality from spiritual shopping. Hospitality listens to the guest and accepts that something remains outside the host's categories. Shopping takes the pleasing elements and leaves the obligations at the door.

For a listener coming from a Christian background, the story may make Hindu devotion feel less remote. For a Hindu listener, it may show the confidence of a devotional world able to encounter another sacred figure without surrendering its own. For a secular listener, it may raise the question of how love can widen without becoming abstract.

The difficulty is always the same: everyone is much easier to love in general than in particular. An imagined humanity never interrupts, misunderstands, or needs the bathroom at an inconvenient time. Actual people do.

Here the remembered Maharajji returns us to service. If the divine is encountered in beings, devotion cannot remain only an affection for a photograph of the teacher. The disagreeable visitor, the tired caregiver, and the person who does not share your language of God all become a test of what you meant.

Again, this does not mean making yourself available to mistreatment. The divine image is not a waiver of consent. It means refusing to reduce another person to the use they have for your preferred spiritual mood.

This chapter gives us a less spectacular Maharajji than the train or the LSD story. He is not demonstrating an exemption from ordinary reality. He is changing what counts as an encounter with the sacred. The person before you becomes harder to dismiss.

That may be the most demanding kind of miracle story: the one whose ending requires something from the listener.

### 09. The miracle that must not become a medical claim

Among the most famous Ram Dass stories is his account of giving LSD to Maharajji. In the version published in his teaching archive, he describes an initial encounter in nineteen sixty-seven and a later return to the subject. He says Maharajji appeared unaffected by amounts he expected to produce a powerful experience.

The story reverses the hierarchy Alpert had brought from the West. The American arrives with the substance that has overturned his understanding of reality. The Indian teacher seems to treat it as something familiar and, in this instance, unable to disturb him. The expert finds his measure of expertise turned inside out.

That is the narrative structure. It is compelling. It is also not a pharmacological finding.

We do not have a controlled study here. We cannot independently establish the contents and condition of the substances, the exact administration, the full observations, or the teacher's internal experience. The account itself includes Ram Dass's uncertainty about the first occasion and his interpretation of the second as resolving it. Reporting that interpretation faithfully is not the same as adopting it as a demonstrated fact.

Most importantly, the story gives nobody a basis for assuming that devotion, enlightenment, meditation, or confidence makes a person immune to a drug. It is not a safety test. It is not something to imitate. Spiritual status is not a substitute for medical knowledge.

Why spend time on a story we cannot verify? Because it shows what was at stake in Ram Dass's transition. He had been pursuing a state. He came to understand Maharajji as embodying a freedom that did not depend on producing that state chemically. Whether we accept the miraculous interpretation or not, we can see how the story reorganized his values.

The event is also a lesson in the limits of stories themselves. Devotional communities preserve accounts because they carry a relationship. Skeptics may read the same accounts as evidence of suggestion, selective memory, or exaggeration. Sometimes neither side has the evidence needed to settle the disputed mechanism. Honesty means allowing that uncertainty to remain.

We can then ask a different question without pretending it settles the first one. What follows ethically from believing the story? If it leads someone to dismiss medical risk or to treat a charismatic person as incapable of error, that is dangerous. If it loosens the belief that every spiritual possibility must fit a familiar personal framework, it may open useful inquiry. The consequences still need examination.

Maharajji's remembered message repeatedly leads back toward love and service. The miracle story attracts attention. The unglamorous instruction tests what happens to the attention afterward.

This will become one of our recurring measures. Does a spiritual account increase curiosity and care, or does it create an exemption from scrutiny? Does it make the teller more responsible, or simply more interesting?

The difference will matter when Ram Dass returns to America. He is about to become the narrator of a transformation so captivating that readers may want the story to guarantee the reliability of the person telling it.

### 10. A book becomes a doorway

Be Here Now appeared in nineteen seventy-one. It brought Ram Dass's transformation to a wide audience through an unusual mixture of personal narrative, illustrated spiritual teaching, and practical material. It did not resemble a conventional academic argument. Its form helped make the encounter feel immediate.

The title is almost suspiciously easy to understand. Be here now. Three short words, and apparently no application form. Yet the words become difficult the moment we ask what they exclude.

Do they mean never plan? Obviously a book does not print itself, a retreat does not organize itself, and a meal does not appear because everyone has stopped thinking about Tuesday. Do they mean ignore history? Then the same teaching could be used to avoid apologizing. Do they mean stay pleasantly relaxed? That would exclude grief, pain, and most traffic.

The useful interpretation is more demanding. The present is where memory happens, planning happens, and action happens. You can think about tomorrow while knowing that you are thinking, rather than being entirely carried away by the imagined tomorrow. You can remember harm without pretending that the memory is the whole of the current moment. Being here is a change in relationship to experience, not a ban on mental activity.

For Ram Dass, this was connected with the witness: the capacity to notice thoughts, feelings, and roles rather than being completely identified with them. We will examine that more closely soon. For now, notice how the message changes as it crosses a culture.

A Hindu devotional relationship, an American psychological vocabulary, psychedelic experience, and material drawn from several religious traditions meet in one popular work. That mixture opened doors. It also encouraged some readers to treat different traditions as if they were different packaging for exactly the same thing.

We should resist that shortcut. Traditions disagree about self, God, liberation, practice, and authority. A Buddhist account of not-self is not automatically the same claim as a devotional account of an enduring soul. A productive comparison makes those differences visible. It does not dissolve them in the hope of sounding generous.

Ram Dass's gift was often translation in the broad sense: helping people hear a possibility they could not yet hear in another vocabulary. His limitation, and the limitation of any such project, is that translation can become extraction. What gets lost when a religious world becomes a set of portable insights for the individual consumer?

One answer is community. Another is the ordinary labor behind the luminous scene. Food, cleaning, organization, translation, care. We have already encountered Lisa Bieberman in the psychedelic history. We will encounter Siddhi Ma in Maharajji's community. If the story makes only the charismatic men visible, it is incomplete.

Still, the book's invitation was powerful. You could question your inherited identity without necessarily replacing it with another institution. You could admit confusion. You could laugh at your own spiritual ambition. You could begin.

But a doorway is not the house. A striking book can open the question of how to live. It cannot do the living for you. And a teacher who becomes famous for a doorway still has to find his way through it every day.

### 11. The witness is not a security camera

Suppose anger arrives. Usually the experience has a very persuasive grammar. I am angry. I am right. The other person is the problem. The sentences arrive together, already wearing uniforms.

The witness introduces a little space. There is anger. There is heat in the body. There is a story about what the other person meant. There is an urge to send a message that will look less wise tomorrow morning.

Nothing mystical has to be claimed at this stage. It is a shift from total involvement toward noticing. Ram Dass taught versions of this shift, often as a way of loosening identification with the personality. The person and the situation are not erased. They stop occupying the entire field.

But the witness can acquire a costume of its own. Now I am the advanced person who observes emotions instead of having embarrassing ordinary ones. I watch myself being angry with a faint spiritual smirk. The old identity has moved upstairs and installed better lighting.

That is why witnessing needs to meet love. Otherwise it can become a cold distance from life, or an excuse to remain uninvolved. If someone tells you that you have hurt them, observing your discomfort is useful. Observing so serenely that you never answer them is less useful.

In Ram Dass's later language, awareness increasingly comes with tenderness. The movement is not simply away from the personality but toward a less defended relation to the whole person. The ashamed part can be noticed without becoming the enemy. The frightened part can be included without being handed control of the car.

This links directly back to the encounter with Maharajji. The guru, in Ram Dass's experience, seemed to see what he was hiding without withdrawing love. The witness could begin to take on that quality: clear seeing without immediate rejection.

We should not turn this into a claim that self-acceptance fixes every psychological difficulty. It does not. Contemplative practice can be helpful, neutral, or unsettling depending on the person and circumstances. If attending inward becomes overwhelming or detached in a frightening way, there is no spiritual prize for forcing it. Ordinary support and appropriate professional care remain valid.

As an optional reflection, notice something entirely simple: the contact of your feet with the ground, or the sound of this voice. Then notice whether a thought is also present. You do not have to remove it. For a moment, the thought and the sound can both be part of what is noticed. If that exercise does not suit you, leave it. Listening to the distinction is enough.

The point is modest but important. You can have a thought without obeying it, and a feeling without being reduced to it. What you do next remains your responsibility.

The witness opens a space. Devotion gives that space a warmth. Service asks what you will do with it. These are not necessarily three separate stages. In the story we are following, they keep correcting one another.

A person who only witnesses may withdraw. A person who only loves may lose boundaries. A person who only serves may burn out or become controlling. The interesting life begins when none of those tendencies is allowed to run the whole show.

### 12. When the guru dies

Neem Karoli Baba died in Vrindavan on September eleventh, nineteen seventy-three. His devotees often speak of the event as mahasamadhi, understanding a saint's death through their religious account of realization and the departure from the body. A historical statement can give the date and place. It cannot independently certify the spiritual meaning devotees give the event.

For a relationship centered on presence, death creates an obvious crisis. The person whose glance, voice, or touch carried so much meaning is no longer available in the ordinary way. What remains?

In Ram Dass's case, the relationship with Maharajji did not end. His teaching repeatedly described an ongoing inward connection. Devotees may understand this as the continued activity of the guru. A psychological interpretation might emphasize internalized relationship, memory, and the way a loved person's influence persists. Those interpretations differ. We do not need to pretend to settle the metaphysics in order to recognize the importance of the continuing bond.

But there is an ethical question hidden inside that continuity. If I believe my teacher is guiding me inwardly, how do I distinguish guidance from my own wishes? The teacher is no longer available to contradict my report. An inward relationship can deepen responsibility. It can also become an authority that no one else is allowed to question.

That is why the world outside the feeling remains important. Can I explain my decision? Can people disagree with me without being treated as spiritually deficient? Am I willing to discover that what I called guidance was desire?

Maharajji's community also continued through people doing visible work. Sri Siddhi Ma was a close devotee and a guiding presence in the ashrams after his death. The Taos ashram's memorial account describes her decades of care for the community and its sacred places. It also records that devotees revered her as a spiritual figure in her own right.

This changes the picture. A tradition survives through more than the memory of one man's extraordinary presence. It survives through people who maintain a place where others can arrive, eat, worship, grieve, and be received. The practical and the sacred are not competing explanations. In an ashram, the clean floor and the remembered blessing occupy the same room.

Krishna Das offers another form of continuity. In his account, devotional singing became a way of sustaining his connection with Maharajji after the teacher's death. Kirtan traveled, changed musical expression, and gathered new communities. That is a real cultural transmission, and it deserves credit to its Indian sources as well as to its later interpreters.

So the story after nineteen seventy-three is not simply that a guru disappeared and an American kept talking about him. It is a network of relationships, institutions, songs, stories, care, and contested interpretations.

And for Ram Dass it included vulnerability to error. Losing the physical teacher did not make his judgment infallible. The next chapter concerns a mistake he acknowledged, and why acknowledging it is more instructive than pretending that spiritual maturity eliminates the possibility of being fooled.

### 13. The beard with egg on it

Ram Dass spoke publicly about having become caught in a spiritual situation that he later recognized as mistaken. In the nineteen seventies he became involved with a teacher known as Joya. Independent reporting describes his eventual break with that relationship. In a later talk preserved in his own archive, he described the episode as involving his desires for power and enlightenment, and acknowledged drawing other people into it.

He wrote about the experience under the title Egg on My Beard. The title is funny. The underlying point is serious: someone capable of moving others through spiritual teaching can still misjudge a teacher and influence followers in the wrong direction.

We do not need to turn this course into a trial of every participant to learn from that admission. Nor should we use his willingness to admit error as a way of instantly erasing it. Honesty after a mistake matters. So do consequences, repair, and the safeguards that might prevent repetition.

There is a common fantasy that wisdom provides a permanent immunity from projection. Once you have seen deeply, you can recognize the genuine article at a glance. Ram Dass's account undermines that fantasy. Wanting something intensely can affect the interpretation of evidence, including when the thing wanted is enlightenment.

This is where the stories about Maharajji need their hardest companion. If the first meeting opened Ram Dass through a feeling of being known, that does not establish a general rule that anyone who makes you feel known is spiritually reliable. Strong feelings identify strong feelings. They do not, by themselves, establish another person's competence or character.

So what might responsible devotion look like? Here we are making an ethical argument of our own, rather than claiming to reproduce a checklist from Maharajji.

A teacher should not need secrecy about harm to preserve the teaching. A community should have ways to hear concerns beyond the teacher's immediate circle. Spiritual progress should not depend on sexual access, financial pressure, or surrendering the right to leave. Independent relationships and medical care should not be treated as enemies. A demand that feels coercive does not become safe because it comes with sacred vocabulary.

None of this proves that devotion is a mistake. It says that love and accountability must coexist. The alternative is an arrangement in which the teacher gets the benefit of transcendence while the student carries all the consequences.

Ram Dass's later reflections on decision-making return to the need to listen again and admit error. This does not mean changing your mind whenever you feel uncomfortable. It means refusing to make your previous decision more important than what you can now see.

The witness we discussed earlier has work to do here. Can I notice my need to have chosen correctly? Devotion has work to do too. Can I let truth matter more than the identity of being a good disciple?

And service supplies the final test. Are the people around this teaching becoming safer, freer, and better able to care for one another? If the answer is no, a beautiful atmosphere is not enough.

A spiritual life that can survive correction is more trustworthy than one that requires an uninterrupted reputation for wisdom.

### 14. The person you are helping is not your spiritual project

If awakening remains a private experience, it can become an unusually elegant form of self-absorption. Ram Dass's later life repeatedly moved toward the question of service. That movement is one of the strongest links between his American teaching and Maharajji's remembered emphasis on care.

Seva Foundation identifies Ram Dass as a co-founder and dates its founding to nineteen seventy-eight. Its account connects both him and Larry Brilliant with Maharajji's influence. Seva's work has included preventing blindness and restoring sight. Ram Dass was one participant in a much larger effort involving local communities, clinicians, organizers, donors, and many others.

That last sentence matters. A familiar celebrity history makes the visible teacher responsible for an enormous collective achievement. The more honest story distributes credit. Good intentions do not operate on eyes. Skilled people do, with systems and resources that somebody has to maintain.

Service also became an explicit subject of Ram Dass's books and collaborations. How Can I Help?, written with Paul Gorman, and Compassion in Action, written with Mirabai Bush, point toward the complexity of helping. We are not claiming to summarize every argument in books we have not read in full for this course. Their titles and publication history mark the sustained place of service in his work.

The basic problem can be examined directly. Helping somebody can contain several motives at once. I may genuinely care. I may also want relief from the discomfort of seeing suffering. I may want to be needed. I may want gratitude. I may want evidence that I am one of the good people.

Noticing mixed motives does not mean doing nothing until I become pure. That would be a very convenient spirituality for anyone who prefers not to wash the dishes. It means helping while remaining willing to learn what is actually useful.

Imagine bringing food to someone who cannot eat it, then feeling offended that they have spoiled your generous moment. The problem is not the original wish to help. It is the way your identity has taken precedence over their need.

Service asks you to become specific. What is wanted? Who knows the situation? What skills are missing? What would increase the other person's agency rather than dependence on you? And can you accept that the best contribution might be quiet, technical, or performed by someone else?

Here Hanuman returns. Strength has a purpose beyond displaying strength. The professor's brilliance, the speaker's charisma, the devotee's tenderness, and the organizer's patience all become capacities that can be offered rather than advertised.

This also corrects a misunderstanding of spiritual acceptance. Accepting that suffering is present is not the same as approving of preventable suffering. Seeing a situation clearly can be the beginning of effective action. Refusing to see it because you want to remain peaceful is something else.

The person receiving help is not a prop in your awakening. If the encounter really loosens the boundary of self-importance, it should make their actual life more visible, not less.

But there is another side to service, and Ram Dass would meet it with unusual force. What happens when the person who helps has to become the person who needs help?

### 15. Love does not require agreement

Before we reach the stroke, we need to consider the political objection. A teaching that asks us to love everyone can sound evasive in the presence of injustice. Someone is being harmed, and the spiritual person wants to discuss the unity of consciousness. It is not hard to see why that can feel insulting.

Independent reporting on Ram Dass records criticism of his distance from political anger and social causes. His later service work complicates any simple picture of withdrawal, but it does not make the objection disappear.

In the late-life profile published by GQ, images on his altar included political figures whose views he did not share. The practice, as he described it, was a challenge to keep his heart open. That is an inward discipline. It does not tell us whether a particular policy is just or what action should be taken against it.

We have to keep those levels distinct. Loving a person need not mean agreeing with them, trusting them, giving them power, or leaving others exposed to them. An open heart can coexist with a closed door. Compassion can support a firm boundary. It can also support organized opposition.

That distinction is our ethical interpretation, not a guarantee that Ram Dass always applied it successfully. A course should make room to learn from a teacher without turning every weakness into a hidden strength.

There is a related problem with anger. Anger can narrow attention and make cruelty feel righteous. It can also alert us to a violation and provide energy for protection. A demand to become anger-free before responding can protect the person doing harm rather than the person being harmed.

The question is therefore not whether you can eliminate anger on command. It is whether you can listen to what it is registering without allowing it to dictate every action. The witness can help create that space. Service can give the energy a concrete direction. Love can limit the wish to dehumanize.

This is a more difficult position than either pure hostility or pleasant detachment. You may need to say no without enjoying the other person's humiliation. You may need to support consequences without pretending you know the whole of a person's being. You may need to act while still feeling conflicted.

The idea of being here now becomes useful again. The present includes the person in danger, the facts that need checking, the law that may need changing, and the fatigue of people doing the work. It does not consist only of your preferred inner atmosphere.

Maharajji's remembered emphasis on feeding people supplies a welcome correction to abstraction. Whatever your account of ultimate reality, there is a meal to serve. There are also questions about why people remain hungry, whose work is unpaid, and who gets to decide how help is distributed. Practical compassion can become more intelligent without becoming less loving.

The strongest version of this teaching is not that everything is already fine. It is that hatred does not have to be the fuel for everything worth doing.

Now we can return to Ram Dass's body, where the difference between an attractive teaching and a lived response became impossible to keep theoretical.

### 16. The person spirituality did not erase

One reason Ram Dass remained interesting was that the polished spiritual persona never entirely covered the unruly person. His humor often worked because he included his own desires and contradictions. But there are parts of the story where the laugh needs to slow down.

In a late-life GQ interview, he discussed his sexuality more openly, including his attraction to men. The profile also describes relationships and experiences with both men and women. We do not need to choose a permanent label on his behalf in order to recognize that same-sex desire belonged to the life of this teacher.

That matters partly because a generic spiritual biography can quietly remove whatever complicates the saintly image. It can make a person's body and sexuality seem like unfortunate preliminary material, to be discarded on the way to universal love. But the life we are following does not support so clean a division.

There is nothing spiritually inferior about same-sex desire. The ethical questions concern consent, honesty, power, and the treatment of actual people. They do not concern whether someone's attractions fit a preferred public image.

At the same time, acceptance of sexuality must not blur professional obligations. In that same late interview, Ram Dass connected his attraction with the episode involving an undergraduate that preceded his Harvard dismissal. His retrospective account does not by itself establish details of a sexual relationship, and we should not invent one. What is established in Harvard's institutional account is the breach of the agreement about giving psychedelics to undergraduates.

We can therefore hold two things clearly. Shame about sexuality should not be encouraged. Boundaries around research and unequal power should not be relaxed. Confusing these issues would fail both the person and the people affected by his choices.

The wider spiritual question is how love relates to the parts of ourselves we find difficult to present. If belonging depends on hiding every inconvenient desire, the spiritual identity can become another guarded room. If acceptance is made to mean acting out every desire, it becomes dangerous in a different way.

A more useful distinction is between having an experience and making a decision. Desire can be noticed. Fear can be understood. Neither gets automatic permission to decide what happens to somebody else.

This brings us back to the witness, but with more human warmth. The purpose is not to become a surveillance system that catches every impure thought. It is to make honesty possible before the impulse turns into a story that excuses the action.

Maharajji's remembered acceptance mattered to Ram Dass partly because it seemed to reach the person beneath the presentation. That acceptance can be understood as a source of courage for truthfulness. It should never be used as proof that a beloved person's behavior is therefore beyond criticism.

A full account of Ram Dass needs this ordinary, awkward humanity. Without it, the later lessons about aging and dependence would sound as though an abstract consciousness were encountering a temporary equipment problem. In fact a whole person was aging: a person with history, desire, achievement, regret, humor, and relationships.

That is the person the stroke would change. And that is the person who would have to discover whether receiving care could belong to freedom as fully as giving a brilliant lecture.

### 17. The teacher loses his fluent sentence

In nineteen ninety-seven, Ram Dass had a severe stroke. It left him with major physical disability and expressive aphasia, which affected his ability to produce speech. For a man whose public life depended so much on language, the change was immense.

It is tempting to tell this as the perfect final examination. The teacher spoke about surrender, and then life forced him to practice it. There is truth in that interpretation as a way he later understood his own experience. There is also a danger. It can make another person's injury look like an elegantly designed lesson for our benefit.

A stroke is a medical event with real losses. Rehabilitation, equipment, access, money, and sustained human care matter. Disability is not a metaphor someone else's spiritual story gets to consume.

Ram Dass eventually resumed teaching, with a changed pace and a changed body. Pauses became part of his public presence. Later accounts describe a life in which he continued to meet students and speak, often with assistance. The famous voice had not disappeared, but fluency could no longer be taken for granted.

He came to give the stroke a spiritual meaning. That meaning belonged to him. It does not create a duty for someone else to regard illness or disability as grace. A person can seek treatment, resent a limitation, grieve a loss, and still live with depth and dignity. There is no requirement to become inspirational.

This is where the course's opening question changes most sharply. Earlier, Alpert wanted never to come down from an expanded state. Now Ram Dass had to meet a life he could not arrange around his previous capacities. The challenge was no longer how to preserve a glorious experience. It was how to remain in relationship when control had become visibly limited.

And the relationship was practical. Somebody helped him move. Somebody coordinated the day. Somebody waited while a word took time. The ability to receive care became as important as the wish to offer it.

That is harder than it sounds. The helper can secretly believe that needing help would make him less valuable. The generous person can become uncomfortable when generosity flows the other way. Accepting care can expose a hidden bargain: I am allowed to belong because I am useful.

Maharajji's importance returns here, in the meaning Ram Dass gave the relationship. Being loved was not supposed to depend on keeping the professor's credentials, the lecturer's speed, or the body's independence. The teaching had always pointed beyond those conditions. Now it had to survive their loss.

We should leave room for struggle. A person can understand something one day and resist it the next. Spiritual maturity is not a permanent mood.

The change in Ram Dass's later teaching was therefore more than slower speech. It brought the question of presence close to dependence. Can another person wait with me without reducing me to what I can no longer do? Can I let myself be here, even when here is not the place I would have chosen?

That is not an escape from embodiment. It is the work of returning to it.

### 18. Nobody gets home alone

In his later years Ram Dass lived on Maui. He continued to teach, write with collaborators, and receive people. A late-life profile shows him surrounded by care and by images of Maharajji. The contrast with the young academic is striking, but the central question remains: what is left when the performance of being somebody can no longer do all the work?

A sentimental answer would say that only love remains and stop there. A more honest answer includes the people making love practical. Caregiving requires time, skill, patience, coordination, and rest. If we praise the luminous teacher while overlooking the person arranging transport or helping with a daily routine, we have failed to understand the teaching's material conditions.

Remember Siddhi Ma maintaining the ashram community. Remember Lisa Bieberman doing information work in a history usually told through famous men. Remember the clinicians and communities behind Seva. Again and again, the story becomes more truthful when the frame widens.

Ram Dass's late work included conversations about aging and dying. Walking Each Other Home, with Mirabai Bush, made companionship central even in its title. We can hear the title without converting it into a claim about what follows death. The immediate fact is relational: human beings face uncertainty in one another's company.

Ram Dass died at home on Maui on December twenty-second, twenty nineteen. He was eighty-eight. His official obituary and independent reporting agree on those basic facts. For devotees, the relationship with him continues in ways that may resemble his own continuing relationship with Maharajji. Others encounter him through recordings and books without adopting a devotional bond.

Both paths raise a question about legacy. A recording preserves a voice, not a living capacity to correct every interpretation. A quotation can be detached from its context and made to approve almost anything. The person becomes a brand, and the brand becomes unusually good at confirming what its audience already wants to hear.

A serious legacy needs more than circulation. It needs context, criticism, and people willing to say: this part is valuable, this part is uncertain, and this part should not be repeated unexamined.

For Ram Dass, one valuable part is the refusal to make spiritual life depend on pretending to have no embarrassing human material. Another is the movement from private experience toward service. Another is the late confrontation with dependency, where receiving love became inseparable from teaching it.

For Maharajji, the English-language listener has to work against a different distortion. His presence must not disappear behind Ram Dass's storytelling. He belongs to a living Indian devotional history, sustained by devotees and practices that cannot be replaced by our interpretation. We can appreciate the stories without claiming ownership of the world they come from.

The relationship between these two figures was not a transfer of a finished spiritual product from East to West. It was an encounter that kept generating new questions in new circumstances.

We have nearly completed that arc. Now we will bring the strands together, and ask what can be carried into an ordinary week without pretending that listening to a course has made us disciples, clinicians, historians, or saints.

### 19. An ordinary week, without a halo

What follows is an original reflection built from the questions in this course. It is not a prescribed practice from Maharajji, a substitute for mental health care, or a test of spiritual attainment. Take what is useful and leave the rest.

Start with attention. Once in a while, notice where you actually are. The sensation of sitting. A sound in the room. The fact that you are rehearsing an argument with someone who is not currently present. You do not need to win a struggle against thought. The small recognition is already a change in your relationship to it.

Then notice the costume. Which description of yourself feels endangered today? Helpful person? Intelligent person? Advanced practitioner? Person who is definitely handling everything well? You do not have to destroy that identity. Just see how much work is going into defending it.

Next, introduce a little kindness without making a claim about the universe. Perhaps the frightened part is frightened. Perhaps the wish to look impressive belongs to someone who has never felt securely welcome. Understanding that does not excuse harmful action. It can make honest correction less terrifying.

Then do something useful and specific. Ask what help is wanted before supplying it. Finish a task you agreed to do. Give credit to the person whose work made your contribution possible. If the task requires skills you do not have, support someone who has them rather than improvising expertise.

For a devotional listener, remembrance of the divine may belong throughout that day. For someone else, the meaningful anchor may be a commitment to truth, compassion, or service. Those are not identical religious positions. They can still support a practical conversation about conduct.

Include a boundary. If you need to say no, try saying no without adding a performance of contempt. If someone else says no, see whether your feeling of affection survives their independence. That can be a revealing test of what kind of love you meant.

Include receiving. Let somebody explain a thing you do not know. Accept ordinary assistance without immediately repaying it in order to erase the vulnerability. People need not be self-sufficient to be worthy of respect.

And include correction. At the end of a difficult exchange, you might ask: what did I miss? Not as a ritual of self-attack, but as a real question. The answer may require an apology, a change of plan, or more information. A peaceful feeling does not prove the decision was right. A painful feeling does not prove it was wrong.

The measure is not whether you can maintain a serene state for seven days. That would bring us straight back to Alpert's original trap. The measure is whether you can return a little more honestly when the state changes.

Return to the body. Return to the person in front of you. Return to evidence when the story gets too convenient. Return to the meal, the phone call, the correction, the care.

No halo is required. In fact, the halo can become one more object somebody has to carry.

### 20. The way back is part of the way

We began with a man who wanted never to come down. Richard Alpert found that his familiar identity could loosen dramatically, but that the loosening did not automatically become a durable way of life. Psychedelic experience opened a question. It did not settle the ethics of research, the work of relationship, or the problem of wanting to remain extraordinary.

In India, his encounter with Neem Karoli Baba changed the center of the search. In Ram Dass's own account, the decisive experience was being known and loved. The story includes claims of extraordinary knowledge. Those claims remain testimony, not established scientific fact. Its effect on his life is much easier to document.

Maharajji must also be seen beyond that effect. His devotional world was associated with Hanuman, temples, community, service, and remembrance of God. It did not begin when an American arrived, and it did not end when he left. Siddhi Ma and many others sustained a continuing life that a two-man biography could easily hide.

Hanuman gave us an image of power directed beyond itself. Bhakti challenged the idea that knowledge must remain emotionally defended. The witness opened space around thought and identity. Each required a correction from the others. Detachment without care could turn cold. Love without judgment could become unsafe. Service without listening could become another expression of control.

Be Here Now carried these possibilities into a wide American audience. Its openness made it influential. Its mixture of traditions also required care, because openness is not the same as proving that every tradition teaches the same thing.

Ram Dass's mistakes kept the story from becoming a simple ascent. His account of being misled in a later spiritual relationship showed that insight does not confer infallibility. The appropriate response is not to abandon every teacher or excuse every teacher. It is to keep truth, consent, accountability, and the freedom to question inside the spiritual life.

Service brought the teaching into concrete work, including Seva. The stroke brought it into dependence. The man who had helped others had to receive help. The teacher famous for fluency had to leave room for the word that would not arrive on command.

This is the arc that makes the two lives belong in one course. Maharajji's remembered love was not merely an extraordinary moment in Alpert's past. It became a reference point through fame, disappointment, work, aging, and loss. Whether we interpret that ongoing bond devotionally or psychologically, we can see how much more it asked than the preservation of a peak experience.

And that is the final change in our opening question. Perhaps coming down is not always a failure of the path. Perhaps the return is where a glimpse becomes a responsibility.

The breakfast still needs making. The person you hurt still needs an answer. The body still needs care. The world has not become less real because your account of the self has become more spacious.

You do not have to choose between being moved and being careful. You can love a story and ask what supports it. You can honor a teacher and notice a mistake. You can discover something larger than your usual identity and still remember to do what you promised.

The point is not to become somebody who never comes down.

It is to find out how to come back with less armor, better judgment, and something useful in your hands.
