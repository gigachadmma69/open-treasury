Chapter one. The Tigress That Wasn't.

Start with the cliff. Paro Taktsang, the Tiger's Nest, hangs nine hundred meters above the Paro valley in Bhutan, stuck to the rock like it was glued there by something that does not answer to gravity. Every year, tens of thousands of people climb the two or three hours up the trail, and when they arrive, they hear the same story. Guru Rinpoche, Padmasambhava, came here in the eighth century in his wrathful form, Dorje Drolo, flying on the back of a tigress. And the tigress, the guide will tell you, the signboards will tell you, the tourist books and even the National Library's own material will tell you, was his consort, Yeshe Tsogyel, transformed.

It is one of the most famous images in all of Tibetan Buddhism. And according to a Bhutanese scholar named Sonam Kinga, writing in the Journal of Bhutan Studies in 2019, it is wrong. Not a little wrong. Wrong in almost every detail. Wrong about who the tigress was. Wrong about where the transformation happened. Wrong, quite possibly, about what practice Yeshe Tsogyel was even doing in the cave the pilgrims climb to see.

And in 2022, a translator and scholar named Adele Tomlin climbed that same trail, went to a Vajrayana conference in Thimphu where she listened to two speakers talk about Yeshe Tsogyel without mentioning a single one of the women scholars who have spent careers on her, went home, and wrote an article with the magnificent title: "Not a mounted tigress but a powerful cougar." It is the article this course is built on. Joe sent it to us and asked the question the article asks: what was Yeshe Tsogyel actually up to?

So that is the assignment. Not another retelling of the legend. We already made that course. It is on this feed, it is called The Woman Who Kept the Future, and it tells her life the way the tradition tells it, and I think it is beautiful. This course is the other thing. This is the course where we open the back of the watch. Where did the story come from? Who wrote it down, when, and why? Which parts are eighth century, which parts are eighteenth century wearing an eighth-century costume? What do the actual scholars, the philologists, the dissertation writers, the Bhutanese researchers, say? And what happens to a woman's life when it is written down, nine hundred years later, by men?

Fair warning, this is a revision, and revisions are disrespectful by nature. We are going to correct a signboard at one of the holiest sites in the Himalayas. We are going to say the word cougar, and mean something precise by it. And in chapter six, we are going to have to talk carefully and honestly about the fact that the most famous biography says she was raped by seven bandits, and about what the scholarship does and does not let us say about that passage. I will tell you now: it is the most important chapter in this course, and it is handled with total respect. But you should know it is coming.

Here is the map. Chapter two is the provenance: every text that carries her story, from a passing mention in a royal chronicle to the full-blown treasure revelation, in the order they appeared. Chapter three is the life itself, told straight, with each layer labeled. Chapter four is the tigress, who she actually was, and the girl whose name the tradition forgot. Chapter five is the consorts, the teenage boys, the slave she bought in Nepal, and Tomlin's provocation. Chapter six is the hardest song. Chapter seven asks the question the scholars keep circling: was she a feminist? And chapter eight answers Joe's question directly. What was she actually up to?

One promise before we start, and it is the house rule of this whole feed. Everything I quote is real, and everything I cannot verify gets flagged. The primary text at the center of all of this, the biography everyone knows, is a revealed treasure text, a terma, written down by a visionary named Taksham Nuden Dorje in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, about a woman who would have lived around nine hundred years earlier. It claims to be the eyewitness account of her own consorts. It is male-transmitted at every step. None of that makes it false, and none of it makes it history in the modern sense, and holding both of those facts at once is basically the whole game of this course.

The other house rule: where the scholars disagree, you get the disagreement. Where I am interpreting, I say so. And where a woman in the eighth century or the eighteenth century or 2022 was getting written out of her own story, we are going to write her back in, with citations.

History does not stand still, signboards lie, footnotes have typos, and the only honest way to tell a story like this is to show you the machinery while we run it.

Chapter two. The Woman in the Manuscripts.

Before we can say what Yeshe Tsogyel did, we have to answer a ruder question. How do we know anything about her at all?

Janet Gyatso, one of the great scholars of Tibetan literature at Harvard, published a paper in 2006 called "A Partial Genealogy of the Lifestory of Yeshe Tsogyal," and it opens with the problem nobody puts on the postcards. No contemporary inscription mentions her. Not one. No eighth-century stone, no royal edict, no pillar. The famous biography, the one that became the Sky Dancer book in English, was revealed as a treasure text by Taksham Nuden Dorje, who was born in 1655. If Yeshe Tsogyel lived in the eighth century, her standard biography was written down about nine hundred years after she died. Gyatso's verdict is careful: we cannot assert without doubt that a historical Yeshe Tsogyel existed. But she also notes the early sources agree on suspiciously specific things, a birth in a bird year, in the district of Drak, into the Kharchen clan. Other figures from the same court, whose existence nobody doubts, are also missing from the inscriptions. Absence of evidence here is genuinely ambiguous.

Now the layers, in order, because this is where it gets beautiful. This is the part of the bibliography walk-through where you watch a woman get built.

Layer one. The Chronicle of Ba, one of the oldest narratives of Buddhism coming to Tibet, mentions a Kharchenza Tsogyel in some versions, as one of the wives of King Trisong Detsen. And here is the detail I love. The chronicle seems to know about the missing inscriptions, because it explains them. She was a wife who spent her time in meditative practice, it says, and therefore left no legacy, no monuments, nothing carved. The text is already apologizing for her absence from the record. And the kicker, from Gyatso: this excuse does not appear in what looks like the earliest version of the chronicle. Someone added it later. Someone, centuries back, was already doing Yeshe Tsogyel revisionism.

Layer two. The historian Khepa Deu, maybe thirteenth century, knows her as one of eight court ladies who held no political power, built royal tombs, and received initiations from Padmasambhava alongside the king. The old Vajrakilaya texts of the Nyingma school preserve stranger, better fragments. She receives the kila teachings with other aristocratic women. She passes them to her brother. She shows her mastery by controlling a fire in the forests around Chimpu. And in one extraordinary episode, after Padmasambhava calls her a dakini, the king himself gets skeptical, jealous even, and challenges her to fly to the highest heaven and bring back his family's lost royal treasures. She can only pass the test with Padmasambhava's help. Gyatso notes this episode vanishes from the later biographies. A king doubting her did not fit what she was becoming.

Layer three. Nyang ral Nyima Ozer, twelfth century, the great architect of the whole Padmasambhava mythos. His sketch of her is the oldest biographical notice we have, and notice what is missing: the king. No marriage, no gift of a queen. She is simply the daughter of Kharchen Palgyi Wangchuk, taken as Padmasambhava's consort at sixteen, a master of the kila practices who could raise the dead, and, crucially, the possessor of what the texts call the dharani of non-forgetting. Perfect memory. She is the one who could record everything. Hold that fact; it becomes the thesis of this whole course. And Nyang ral believed his own wife was Yeshe Tsogyel's emanation. Gyatso, who misses nothing, catches him in the same sentence using the honorific word for his son's body and the ordinary, non-honorific word for his wife's. Even her devotees downgraded her in grammar.

Layer four. Guru Chowang, thirteenth century. He coins a phrase that quietly promotes her. Where the texts had said lord and subject, meaning the king and the nobleman Namkhai Nyingpo, he writes lord, subject, and friend: the king, the nobleman, and her. Friend is the standard euphemism for tantric consort. She is now ranked with the king as a principal heir of the teachings. And there is evidence Guru Chowang wrote a full life of her, now lost, except for one surviving quotation, preserved inside a later manuscript, describing her death. After Padmasambhava left Tibet, she meditated two more months, then, on the tenth day of the second month, she rode a sunbeam and merged indistinguishably with him in a mansion of light. The earliest death scene is three sentences long. Wait until you hear what it becomes.

Layer five. The discovery. In 1996, in Lhasa, Janet Gyatso, with help from Jake Dalton, found a blockprint of a complete, full-length biography of Yeshe Tsogyel from the fourteenth century, by a treasure revealer named Drime Kunga. Almost nobody in the tradition even remembered it existed. And it is fascinatingly different. No king. No marriage. Her first suitor is an Indian prince. She is called Lha-lcam through most of the book, and only receives the name Yeshe Tsogyel near the very end. The story is intimate, psychological, full of magical skulls and flying carpets, and it is clearly related to Taksham's famous version and clearly not its source. Both, Gyatso concludes, descend from older material we no longer have. This is the biography that was finally translated into English in 2017 by Chonyi Drolma, also known as Anne Holland, as The Life and Visions of Yeshe Tsogyal. It took six hundred years and a scholar photocopying blockprints in Lhasa for that book to exist.

Layer six. Taksham Nuden Dorje, born 1655, the reincarnation, he claimed, of her own consort Atsara Sale, revealing at Lhorong in Kham the biography he said Atsara Sale and Namkhai Nyingpo wrote as eyewitnesses. This is the one. Three English translations of it exist: Tarthang Tulku's Mother of Knowledge in 1983, Keith Dowman's Sky Dancer in 1984, and the Padmakara group's Lady of the Lotus-Born in 1999. Every image you have of her, the beauty, the austerities, the consorts, the bandits, the hell journey, comes substantially from this one seventeenth-century visionary's pen.

And layer seven, just to show the process never stops: a Bonpo version, revealed in 1918 by a female treasure revealer, Dechen Wangmo, which folds Yeshe Tsogyel into a whole program of Bon-Buddhist reconciliation. Even the Bonpos wanted her. Everyone wanted her.

Now step back, because the scholars draw a profound conclusion from this pattern. Jue Liang, in her 2020 University of Virginia dissertation, Conceiving the Mother of Tibet, points out that literary accounts of Yeshe Tsogyel do not flourish until about six hundred years after her lifetime, exactly when the Nyingma school was fighting for legitimacy against the newer schools and needed an origin story. The Treasure tradition required somebody at the eighth-century court who heard everything, remembered everything, wrote it all down, and hid it, so that twelfth-century revealers could find it. The tradition needed Yeshe Tsogyel. It needed a woman with perfect memory as the conduit between the golden age and the present. Liang calls her, precisely, an Ananda: the Buddha's own attendant, the one whose memory anchored the Buddhist canon, and, not coincidentally, the disciple who interceded for women to be ordained. That parallel is not my flourish. It is in the scholarship.

Elizabeth Angowski, in her 2019 Harvard dissertation, adds the reader's side. She argues the fourteenth-century life works best if you let it be what it says it is, both an authentic revealed treasure and a liberation story, and she shows how the text trains you, through intertextuality and dialogue, to keep finding more significance in her. The biographies are not trying to be photographs. They are trying to make her alive for you, now.

So. What is source and what is interpretation? Here is the rule for the rest of this course. When I say "the biography says," I mean Taksham's text unless I name another. When something is older, I will tell you it is older, and those are the moments to lean in, because the oldest layers are the ones where she is least decorated and most interesting. And when all the layers disagree, that disagreement is itself the evidence. It tells you what each century needed her to be.

She is a woman assembled from manuscripts. But here is the thing nobody expected: when you read across the manuscripts, a consistent character keeps surfacing through the cracks, stubborn, funny, frighteningly disciplined, and utterly uninterested in being anybody's ornament. That is chapter three.


Chapter three. What She Was Actually Up To.

Now the life, told once through, fast, with the layers labeled as we go. Think of it as the director's cut where I keep the clapperboard in shot.

She is born in Drak, in central Tibet, into the Kharchen clan, in a bird year. That much, all the early layers agree on. She is famous for her beauty while still a girl, and her sixteenth year is a disaster. Suitors come, a Tibetan prince of Zurkhar in the old Drime Kunga version, and she refuses them, because she wants to practice. For refusing, she is beaten. Drime Kunga says her own father's ministers rough her up and she is banished; Taksham's famous version has the suitors fighting over her until she is dragged into the royal court. Either way, the story begins the same way: a teenage girl says no to every man in the room, and the room makes her pay for it.

She ends up at the court of King Trisong Detsen. And here I have to stop and label, because this is the single biggest seam in the whole tradition. In the later versions, she becomes the king's wife, and the king then offers her to Padmasambhava as a gift, in exchange for the tantric teachings. That is the story everyone knows. But the oldest sketch, Nyang ral's, does not mention the king at all. Drime Kunga's full fourteenth-century biography does not mention the king at all. Another very early text, the Extensive Life, gives her a direct karmic connection to Padmasambhava with no royal middleman. The queen-and-the-gift plot is a later addition. Which means one of the most retold facts of her life, that she was a queen traded to a guru, might be a medieval narrative upgrade, not history. Gyatso is blunt about what the upgrade does: it makes her sexual-yogic gift to Padmasambhava the hinge on which all of tantric Buddhism enters Tibet. Her body becomes the door of the dharma in Tibet. Whether you read that as glorification or appropriation is exactly the argument we will have in chapter seven.

In the fourteenth-century version, it plays differently. Fleeing the suitors, she prays, and a youth with a topknot rescues her, gives her a magic ring, and spirits her away to Chimpu, the retreat caves near Samye, leaving two magical automatons behind to confuse her pursuers. The youth is Padmasambhava. The prose is pure fourteenth-century Tibet, and it is delightful, and it is also doing something serious: in the oldest full version, she goes to the guru herself, on her own feet, by her own choice, after her own refusal. Nobody hands her to anybody.

Then the training, and here all the versions converge on the qualities that define her. She practices with terrifying intensity. Austerities in places like Shampo Gang: cold, hunger, rags, months of it. She masters the Vajrakilaya practices, the oldest layer of all remembers that; the early texts call her a kila virtuoso who could control fire and, eventually, raise the dead. And she receives the dharani of non-forgetting. Perfect recall. Everything Padmasambhava teaches, she keeps, whole. This is why Liang calls her Tibet's Ananda. The tradition is unanimous on this point across eight hundred years of disagreement about everything else: she is the one who remembers.

And she teaches. In Drime Kunga's fifth chapter, a crowd of aristocratic women comes to her for the Great Perfection teachings, and twenty-five of them become siddhas. Twenty-five women, attaining under a woman. That scene is three hundred years older than the famous biography, and almost nobody talks about it.

Then the journeys, which read like a tantric road movie. A vision-journey to the hidden land of Pemako, where she watches gruesome austerities meant to forge her diligence, and beheads a tiger, note that, she kills a tiger in the old text, she does not become one. The bandits at Shampo Gang, which gets its own chapter later. The descent into hell to drag out the evil minister Shantipa, a full chapter in Drime Kunga, compressed to a single line in Taksham. The woman walks into hell to retrieve the man who hurt her most. That is in the record.

Then Nepal, on Padmasambhava's instruction, to find her prophesied consort, a sixteen-year-old slave named Atsara Sale, whom she buys free with money she raises herself. We will sit with that scene in chapter five.

Then Bhutan, and this is where the geography starts to matter. Padmasambhava tells her to practice at three Tiger's Nests: Womphu Taktsang in Tibet, Mon Taktsang, which is Paro in Bhutan, and Kham Taktsang in the east. Three different cliffs, one name. Sonam Kinga's paper argues, convincingly, that centuries of storytellers collapsed the three into the one famous Bhutanese site, and dragged episodes across the map with them. She comes from Tidro to Senge Dzong, then to Paro Taktsang, with her consort Atsara Sale and a girl called Dewamo. At Paro she stays months in the cave the pilgrims now climb to, Senge Phug, the Lion's Cave. There she receives a vision of the Amitayus mandala and a prophecy that she will live two hundred and twenty-five years. From there she goes to Womphu Taktsang, back in Tibet, and that is where the great Vajrakilaya initiation happens, and where the tigress enters the story. Next chapter.

Then the last great project, and for my money the most important thing she ever did. Before Padmasambhava leaves Tibet, he charges her with compiling all his teachings, writing them down with the help of the other disciples, and hiding them as terma, treasures, for future generations. And the biography has them travel together on a hiding tour. Listen to what the text says happens at Paro Taktsang, in Dowman's translation, quoted in Kinga's paper: "In Paro Taktsang in Bhutan we disposed the treasures separately and left prophetic catalogues. 'This is the place of Guru's Mind,' prayed the Guru. 'Whoever practices here will attain mahamudra siddhi.'" The Tiger's Nest is not famous because a tigress landed there. In the biography, it is famous because it is a hard drive. She helped hide the future of the tradition in its rock.

Finally, the numbers, and I give them to you raw, because they do not reconcile and honesty is the brand. The prophecy at Senge Phug says she will live two hundred and twenty-five years. The biography's internal arithmetic, as Kinga reads it, has her attain parinirvana at Zapu Peak in Tibet at age two hundred and eleven. Dowman, the translator, instead dates her 757 to 817, a normal human sixty years. Liang's scholarly article uses 777 to 817. The tradition's own chronology and the modern editor's chronology cannot both be right, and nobody alive can settle it. What the layers agree on is only this: she outlived Padmasambhava's departure by decades, and spent those decades traveling the power places, teaching.

Her death, you already heard in its oldest form: a sunbeam, a mansion of light, three sentences. Taksham turns it into pages of glory. That is the whole pattern in miniature. The record keeps adding ornaments, and when you strip them, the same figure keeps standing there.

So what was she actually up to? Not being pretty. Not being carried. She was memorizing, practicing, teaching women, rescuing enemies, buying a slave's freedom, and hiding libraries in cliffs. She was building the storage system for a teaching she had been told the world would try to forget.

And the tigress? The tigress was somebody else entirely. Meet her.


Chapter four. The Girl Made of Honey and Milk.

While Yeshe Tsogyel is practicing at Senge Dzong, a local girl starts visiting her cave. She is thirteen years old. She is the daughter of a minor king named Hamrey. And she brings offerings: honey, and milk. Small, regular, human kindnesses to the strange woman meditating in the rock.

Tsogyel sees the marks of a dakini on her. So she goes to the king and asks him to give her the girl. And here Sonam Kinga, the Bhutanese scholar, drops one of those footnotes that quietly rewires a country's folklore. There is a village in Lhuntse called Khoma, and the local etymology says it is named for the moment Guru Rinpoche told the local ruler he needed a girl, khomo, for tantric practice. Kinga heard this himself doing fieldwork. But reading the biography in the cave, he realizes the line in the text is not the Guru's. It is Yeshe Tsogyel's. She is the one who asks for the girl. The folklore reassigned her line to a man.

The girl is renamed Tashi Khyidren, Fortunate Guide to Mankind. And she travels with the party to Paro Taktsang, where, the biography says, there were five of them in the cave retreat: Yeshe Tsogyel, her consorts Atsara Sale and Atsara Pelyang, a Bhutanese boy called Sale, and Tashi Khyidren. One woman, two young consorts, a boy, and a teenage girl who brought honey and milk. That is the actual Tiger's Nest retreat community, according to the text.

Now, what was Tsogyel practicing up there? The signboard at the trail junction says, quote, this is a cave where the dakini Yeshe Tsogyel practiced Vajrakilaya. The walls say the same. Kinga checked the biography instead. What the text says she practiced there is, in his rendering, the seed essence of co-emergent pleasure and emptiness. Bliss-emptiness yuganaddha, completion-stage work. Not Vajrakilaya. The signboard is wrong twice, and we have not even gotten to the tigress yet.

After months of this practice, the text says, her body takes on the appearance of a sixteen-year-old maiden and she transforms into Vajravarahi. There is a verse about this inscribed on a stone tablet below her painting at Senge Phug, and I want to give it to you in two translations, because this is exactly the kind of philology fight this feed loves. Kinga published it as: "Undertaking austere practice at Paro Taktshang on the profound path, the three doors of body, speech and mind became three vajras of great peace; blazing red radiance like a graceful sixteen-year-old maiden, to the Heroine Vajravarahi, I pray." Tomlin re-translated it from the Tibetan and got something meaningfully different: "Undertaking the profound path of unconventional conduct practice at Paro Taktsang, the three doors became three vajras of great bliss; blazing brilliant radiance as a stunning sixteen-year-old, I supplicate the Heroine Varahi." Unconventional conduct, not austerity. Great bliss, not great peace. The Tibetan word tul-zhuk does not mean grim asceticism; it means the deliberate, outrageous conduct of a tantrika breaking convention. Same stone, two women and a man reading it, and the women read more bliss out of it. My judgment, offered as judgment: Tomlin's is the better translation of the term, and it changes the flavor of the whole cave. She was not up there suffering. She was up there blazing.

Then the Amitayus vision and the prophecy of a two-hundred-and-twenty-five-year lifespan, and then the party moves to Womphu Taktsang, in Tibet. Not Paro. Underline this, because everything famous now happens at Womphu.

At Womphu, Padmasambhava asks Tsogyel to give him Tashi Khyidren, who has all the marks of an awareness dakini, to serve as his consort for the Vajrakilaya practice. Tsogyel offers her, and prays to receive the secret instructions of Dorje Phurba herself. He sends her to find a fourteen-year-old boy in Uru to be her own partner for the practice; she finds him, and he becomes Lhalung Pelgyi Sengye, the being the tradition says will be reborn to assassinate the anti-Buddhist king Langdarma. The initiation that follows has a full cast: Dewamo becomes the vajra hostess, the two atsaras become vajra dancers, the Bhutanese boy becomes the vajra attendant, Tsogyel is made root consort, and Tashi Khyidren, the milk-and-honey girl, is made liberating consort. Seven nights of practice. The daggers dance. The signs come.

And now, the passage that detonates the postcard. Dowman's translation, quoted in Kinga's paper, Tsogyel speaking in the first person, trimmed only for the ear:

"The evening that these miraculous signs appeared, the Guru himself was transformed into Dorje Trollo, with myself as Ekajati, joined in union with him, and Tashi Khyidren as our mount, the tigress, to subject the gods and demons of the four quarters of Tibet. Riding upon the back of the girl Khyidren transformed into a tigress, the Guru and his mystic partner, absorbed in the samadhi of Dorje Phurba, projected countless fierce, terrifying beings in forms identical to himself. In particular, one of these forms flew directly to Paro Taktsang, and there subjugated the gods and demons of the borderlands and beyond, Bhutan, Nepal, India, and bound them to serve the dharma."

Read it once more, slowly. Three figures. Dorje Drolo. Ekajati, who is Yeshe Tsogyel, joined in union with him as an equal partner in the samadhi. And the tigress, who is Tashi Khyidren. The biography never puts Tsogyel under the Guru. It puts her beside him, and it puts the thirteen-year-old Bhutanese girl underneath them both, carrying the whole wrathful apparition on her back.

Karma Phuntsho, the other great Bhutanese scholar of Guru Rinpoche, independently identifies the tigress as Tashi Khyidren too, though a different girl of that name, the daughter of Sendha Gyalp of Bumthang. So even the correction has a fork in it, and the honest sentence is: the tigress was Tashi Khyidren, and we are not one hundred percent sure which Tashi Khyidren. The dishonest sentence is the one on the signboard.

How did the error take over? Kinga's diagnosis is gentle. Three Tiger's Nests, one name, centuries of retelling, and a cliff at Paro that genuinely looks like Dorje Drolo. Tourism did the rest. Tomlin, to her credit, did not just publish; she wrote to the Treasury of Lives, the scholarly biographical encyclopedia, and asked them to fix their entry. And here is the update, checked today: the Treasury of Lives entry on Yeshe Tsogyel has since been entirely rewritten, and the author of the new, peer-reviewed version is Jue Liang herself, the scholar whose dissertation we have been leaning on. The revision is working its way through the institutions. Slowly. That is how revisions go.

But stay with the girl a moment longer, because she is why this chapter matters. Tashi Khyidren brought milk and honey to a cave, got recognized, got renamed, got offered, got initiated, and became, in the text's own words, the liberating consort and the mount of the wrathful Buddha, the literal vehicle of the subjugation of a continent's demons. And then the tradition spent three hundred years telling pilgrims she was somebody else. Tomlin has a line about what she is doing in this article, and it deserves to be the epigraph of this whole course: women's history is about transforming HIS stories into HER stories.

The tigress in the lair was a girl with honey. Remember her name. Tashi Khyidren. Fortunate Guide to Mankind.


Chapter five. The Cougar Question.

Nepal, around the end of the eighth century, in the story's own chronology. Yeshe Tsogyel arrives, middle-aged by the text's internal clock, carrying money she has raised herself. She is looking for a specific young man. Padmasambhava told her where to look, and Dowman's translation keeps the instruction: "Go to the Valley of Nepal where there is a sixteen-year-old youth with a mole on his right breast, who is an emanation of the Buddha Hero Hayagriva, called Atsara Sale. He has wandered there from Serling in India. Find him, and make him your ally."

She finds him. He is a slave. So she raises the price of his contract, pays his owners, and buys him his freedom.

Sit in that scene for a second, because Tomlin is right that it does not get enough air. Every piece of the standard tantric tableau is inverted. Usually, in the literature, an older male master takes a young female consort. Here, an older female master travels internationally, negotiates a purchase, and acquires a young male consort, who will go on to become her companion, ally, and, in the biography's attribution, her scribe. And if Taksham Nuden Dorje's own claim is believed, Atsara Sale came back: the seventeenth-century treasure revealer of the famous biography claimed to be his reincarnation, writing down, lifetimes later, the life of the woman who bought him free. She does the choosing, the traveling, the paying, and later, the training. If a man in a hagiography did any of this, it would be narrated as proof of his spiritual authority. When she does it, it gets footnoted.

And Atsara Sale is not the only one. The Womphu episode has Padmasambhava instructing her to find a fourteen-year-old boy in Uru, and she finds him, Lhalung Pelgyi Sengye, who becomes the prophesied assassin of the king who will try to destroy Buddhism in Tibet. There is Atsara Pelyang. There is the Bhutanese boy, Sale. Of the five people in the Tiger's Nest retreat party, three were her young male consorts.

Now the word. Tomlin, knowing exactly what she is doing, writes: "In contemporary language, and slightly tongue-in-cheek, Tsogyel might well be called the first Tibetan Buddhist enlightened cougar." Cougar: modern slang, often derogatory, for an older woman with younger men. And Tomlin spends a full section of the article on the ethics of the word itself, because she knows it is loaded. Her argument runs like this. The term is sexist in ordinary usage, because nobody coins a slur for an older man with younger women. But slurs get reclaimed by the people they were coined to humiliate, and cougar is ripe for it. And underneath the joke is a serious doctrinal point: the existence of Yeshe Tsogyel's consorts cuts straight through the assumption that tantric consorts are, by nature, young pretty women attached to powerful older men. In her life, the polarity runs the other way. And the tradition's own explanation of consort practice supports her: what matters in karmamudra is not age or surface beauty but mastery of the inner channels and winds. The texts are explicit that the ages are symbolic, the qualifications are about realization. Tomlin points out that Sukhasiddhi, one of the great female founders of the Shangpa Kagyu, was sixty-six years old when she entered union practice, and emerged, the story says, with the appearance of a sixteen-year-old. The dharma has never actually believed the pretty-girl theory of consorts. It just mostly told the stories that way.

Now the scholarship, because this is where it gets really interesting. Janet Gyatso, reading across the whole genealogy, lands on something astonishing about Taksham's biography specifically. His depiction of female-dominant consort yoga is, in her words, virtually unprecedented in Buddhist literature. He shows her choosing, purchasing, training, and using her own male consorts, in exuberant prose, with agency that the earlier texts never give her. In fact, in the older dialogue literature, Gyatso catches a telling slip: Padmasambhava lectures Yeshe Tsogyel on how to recognize the ideal female consort, apparently forgetting, as Gyatso puts it, that Yeshe Tsogyel is herself a female and would presumably be more interested in recognizing the ideal male one. The early texts use her as a vehicle to deliver teachings calibrated for a male audience. Taksham, three centuries later, finally lets her drive.

And Jue Liang adds the necessary inner frame. In the Treasure literature, the consort relationship between Tsogyel and Padmasambhava is elevated to operate entirely on the enlightened plane. It is explicitly not secular intimacy, not romance, not transaction. Whatever we moderns make of it, the tradition's own claim is that these unions are practices between realized beings, in service of beings.

So, the honest stack of caveats, because this house shows its work. One: all of this reaches us through male authors, centuries later; her own voice is behind glass everywhere in this record, and chapter seven will press on that. Two: the ages in these texts, sixteen, fourteen, are the texts' ages, presented by the tradition as symbolic of purity of channels, and presented in a world whose ethics are not ours. Three, and this one is non-negotiable in 2026: nothing in any of these stories is a template or an excuse for any teacher, anywhere, ever. Real consent between real adults, real accountability, real consequences. The tradition's realized-beings clause applies, by its own rules, only to the actually realized, and the actually realized do not need it quoted at anyone. We will go deeper on that in the next chapter, because the record forces us to.

But let the core point land, because it is the point Tomlin climbed a mountain to make. Yeshe Tsogyel, significantly older than her consorts, choosing them, training them, practicing with them as equals on the path, is not a scandal hidden in the fine print of her story. It is in the biography's main text, for anyone who reads it. She was never the demure appendage of Padmasambhava's legend. She was a woman in full command of her own practice, her own partnerships, and her own legacy, in a patriarchal world, twelve hundred years ago, if the tradition's own dates are to be believed.

The tigress was a metaphor everyone got wrong. The cougar, says Tomlin, tongue partly in cheek, is the metaphor that fits: powerful, self-possessed, unbothered. A woman whose sexuality and mastery were the same fact.

And then, in the middle of the biography, there is the passage that nobody knows what to do with. We have to go there next. No jokes in the next chapter. I will meet you there.


Chapter six. The Hardest Song.

This chapter discusses sexual assault, in the sources and in the scholarship. It is handled as carefully as I know how. If you need to skip it, the course will still be whole. If you stay, I will keep it plain.

In the famous biography, in Dowman's translation, there is a passage at a place called Shampo Gang. Yeshe Tsogyel is living there in austerity, alone, practicing. Seven bandits come. They rob her of her possessions, and they rape her.

And then the text does the thing that has stopped every reader of this book cold for forty years in English. She sings them a song. It is a song of introduction to the four joys, the inner completion-stage teaching, and in it she reframes everything that has just happened to her as an empowerment, each stage of violation transmuted into a stage of initiation. I am not going to read it in full. Two lines carry its logic, from Dowman's translation: "My sons, you have met a sublime consort, the Great Mother. And by virtue of your resources of accumulated merit, fortuitously, you have received the four empowerments." And at its end: "This is extraordinary, exalted secret instruction; to consciously practise this method brings a fall, but discovered by chance it gives miraculous release."

In the story, the bandits are converted. In the older version of the life, she then takes them, of all places, to Oddiyana, the pure land, where they receive initiation.

That is the passage. Now the honesty, in three layers, because what the scholarship lets us say here is narrower and stranger than what usually gets said.

Layer one. Tomlin, to her permanent credit as an honest scholar, prints the passage and then prints her own caveat in brackets: she has not checked the translation against the Tibetan, and cannot say for sure whether it is accurate. The single most explosive passage in the whole feminist re-reading of Yeshe Tsogyel rests on an unverified translation, and she says so herself, in print. That is what integrity looks like, and it also means everything downstream of this passage needs a flag on it.

Layer two. Janet Gyatso, who has read all the versions in Tibetan, found something bigger. Taksham's biography contradicts itself. In one episode, the seven bandits only rob her. In a later episode, they rob and rape her. And in Drime Kunga's fourteenth-century biography, the older full life, three hundred years closer to her, there is no rape at all. The bandits rob her; she patiently rehabilitates them; she takes them to Oddiyana. Robbery, teaching, redemption. The sexual violence enters the story late, somewhere between the fourteenth century and the seventeenth, in a male visionary's pen.

Layer three. What we therefore cannot say. We cannot say this happened to a historical woman; we cannot even be sure a historical woman stood behind any of this. We cannot hear her own voice in the passage, because the biography is attributed to her male consort, revealed nine hundred years on, and translated by a man, with the translation unchecked against the Tibetan on precisely this point. Every hand on this text was a man's hand.

And what we can say. We can say that a seventeenth-century Tibetan author chose to depict something Tibetan literature almost never depicts. Gyatso notes that a rape scene in this detail is virtually without precedent in the tradition's literature, and she finds it part of the same package as his other unprecedented moves: the woman's-body-is-superior teaching, the female-dominant consort yoga. Whatever we make of it, Taksham was doing something deliberate, putting a woman's worst violation into the record and then showing her unbroken by it.

We can also say what the passage means inside its own world. It is the extreme edge of the Vajrayana claim that the view cannot be destroyed from outside, that even the worst thing can be met inside the nature of mind. The tradition presents her as so realized that there was no ordinary victim left to harm. As the tradition's internal logic, that is the point of the song: not that the assault was good, but that she could not be moved from what she knew.

And we must say, with equal clarity, what it does not mean. It is not a model for how anyone should respond to assault. It is not an argument that assault is really a teaching. It is not, and this cannot be said firmly enough, any kind of cover for misconduct by any lama, teacher, or partner, in any century. Tomlin says it herself in the article, plainly: none of this condones rape or excuses anyone, and actions that expose abuse and protect others, done with compassion, can themselves be bodhisattva action. She reaches for Gandhi's line, that no one can hurt me without my permission, and I want to handle that line the way I think the scholarship would want it handled: it can only ever be a description of her own invulnerability. It can never, ever be a prescription handed to somebody else. Nobody gets to quote it at a survivor.

The field, to its credit, is working on exactly this. This very year, 2026, the Journal of Global Buddhism ran a roundtable titled "What Can Buddhist Studies Offer Survivors?", scholars and practitioners, many of them women in this same field, asking how Buddhist studies can stop using passages like this one as decoration and start answering to the people who have lived them. That conversation is live, unresolved, and it deserves its own course on this feed. This is me marking it, not closing it.

So what do we do with the hardest song? Here is where I land, and it is labeled as my reading. In the oldest version of her life, she was robbed by seven men, and she taught them anyway, and she walked them to a pure land. No violation required for the miracle. The later tradition added the worst thing a man can take from a woman, perhaps to prove there was nothing that could take what she was. Both versions are trying to say the same impossible thing: that what she had was hers, and it could not be taken.

You do not have to believe the story to see what it was protecting. You only have to notice that eight hundred years of redactors, whatever else they changed, kept the part where the men who hurt her end up kneeling in a pure land, and she is the one who opened the door.


Chapter seven. Was She a Feminist?

It is the question hanging over this whole course, and every serious scholar in the bibliography takes a swing at it, so let us have the fight properly. Was Yeshe Tsogyel, or rather the tradition that built her, a kind of ancient feminism? Rita Gross titled her 1987 essay "Yeshe Tsogyal: Enlightened Consort, Great Teacher, Female Role Model," which is close to the yes position. Tomlin's article calls her a proto-feminist and a heroine for the twenty-first century. Gyatso's considered answer is two words: mixed, at best. Here is the evidence on both sides, and then my verdict, labeled as mine.

The case against first, because it is strong and it is in the same texts that praise her.

The heroine of these biographies regularly calls herself inferior. Low birth, small wisdom, great self-regard, her words, in formula. Gyatso catalogs it across both major versions. The very Vajrakilaya traditions that descend from her are explained, inside the tradition, as having been created because she asked for a teaching brief enough for her poor female mind, one that addressed the particularly female problem of excessive desire. Her ascetic ordeals in Drime Kunga are framed as a corrective to women's weak endurance and little faith. And then there is the passage Gyatso found that is almost unbearable in its irony: the degenerate age will be marked, the text says, by women mastering Buddhist philosophy. Her own emanations are prophesied to appear in that degenerate age, which means the tradition is claiming that women becoming learned is simultaneously a sign of the apocalypse and the thing that will save it. Gyatso adds a modern echo. In 1998, a learned khenpo, head of a college for nuns, told her that the recent appearance of academic institutions for women was a sign of degenerate times. To her face. In 1998.

Jue Liang's zhus lan article gives the most precise anatomy of this material. Across the Treasure dialogues, female disciples approach Padmasambhava through a ritual of self-abasement. Seven women, led by Tsogyel, each request a teaching matched to her proclaimed weakness, and the requests are a tour of every stereotype: enlightenment through indolence, enlightenment without abandoning distraction, enlightenment in a single word. One asks not to be reborn as a woman again. And Liang's reading is the subtle one: these laments are doing two jobs at once. They are performance, the required modesty of a disciple in this genre, the ritual price of admission. And they are record, a real transcript of what it cost to be a woman seeking teachings: being married off like being thrown into prison, working unpaid as a housemaid, wasting a life on errands. The formula includes them, Liang argues. The disparagement is the doorway, not the wall. And it matters who is asking. In most of these dialogues, the questioner, the one whose questions draw out the teachings for everyone, is Yeshe Tsogyel. Like Ananda begging the Buddha for the nuns' order, she is the spokesperson through whom women's concerns enter the canon at all.

And then there is the counter-text, the one Gyatso calls astonishing, because in all the literature she has found, it exists only in Taksham. Tsogyel laments the difficulties of her female body to Padmasambhava, and he answers, as Gyatso renders his assertion: ultimately, a woman's body is in fact superior to a man's for gaining enlightenment. Superior. Not equal. One visionary, in the seventeenth century, put that in the guru's mouth, and nobody else, before or since, that we know of, dared to.

Now the case for, the structural one, the one I find most persuasive.

Because of her, Tibetan religion developed a channel for female authority that otherwise barely existed. Gyatso states it flatly: one of the very few ways Tibetan religious society has had to recognize a gifted woman is to declare her an emanation of Yeshe Tsogyel. The biography itself builds this in. At Womphu, remember, the prophecy: she will return as Machig Labdron. There is a full course on Machig on this feed, Cutting the Claim of Self, and if you have heard it, you know what that prophecy means: the single greatest female master in Tibetan history, the founder of Chod, is framed as Yeshe Tsogyel coming back. And behind Machig stretches a line of real women, recognized female masters and hierarchs, Samding Dorje Phagmo and her successors among them, running on the precedent this one story built. The myth did institutional work. It still does.

Sonam Wangmo, a Bhutanese nun-scholar whose paper on Tsogyel I want to flag even though it sits outside Tomlin's bibliography, names the final layer precisely: soteriological androgyny. Enlightenment is beyond gender altogether; every symbol is ultimately dissolved. And yet, she argues, women still need potent female symbols to break the gender barriers in their own minds. Tsogyel's femaleness is, in the end, irrelevant to her realization, and absolutely necessary for the women who come after. Both. That is the whole tension, held by a scholar who lives inside it.

So, the verdict, mine, labeled. "Feminist" is our century's word, and the eighth century, or the seventeenth, would not have known what to do with it. The tradition that built Yeshe Tsogyel was patriarchal to its bones and says so on every page. And. It kept a woman at the exact center of its origin story, the one who heard everything, remembered everything, hid everything, and authorized everything, and it could never quite sand her down into an ornament. Every generation had to deal with her. Every generation of Tibetan women who pointed at her and said, her, then me, was doing what Tomlin is doing, what Kinga is doing, what Gyatso, Liang, Angowski, Gross, Wangmo are doing. Not inventing a heroine. Finding the receipts.

Was she a feminist? Wrong tense, wrong word. She is a precedent. Precedents do not march. They just sit there in the record, immovable, waiting for someone to cite them.


Chapter eight. The Archive That Walked.

So, Joe's question. What was she actually up to?

Here is my answer, assembled from everything you have just heard, each piece cited to its layer.

First: she was the memory. Across eight hundred years of sources that agree on almost nothing else, from the twelfth-century fragments to the seventeenth-century revelation, one attribute never moves: the dharani of non-forgetting. Perfect recall. She is the one who heard every teaching Padmasambhava gave in Tibet and lost none of it. Jue Liang's comparison is exact: she is Tibet's Ananda, the human recording device on which the entire canon hangs. In a tradition whose whole authority rests on words spoken once, in caves, to small audiences, the person who remembers is not a supporting character. She is the load-bearing wall.

Second: she was the storage. Her great final commission, in the biography, is the one that actually shaped history: compile the teachings, write them down, hide them as terma, seed them across the cliffs of Tibet and Bhutan for beings not yet born. The Treasure tradition, arguably the most distinctive thing about Tibetan Buddhism, centuries of visionaries finding texts in rock and lake and mind, runs on her archive. The Tiger's Nest itself, in the text, is not the place a tigress landed. It is "the place of Guru's Mind," a blessed repository she helped stock, with prophetic catalogues left on file. When the twelfth-century revealers started pulling treasures out of the ground, the story they told about why those treasures existed was a story about her. What was she actually up to? She was backing up the dharma.

Third: she was the teacher. Twenty-five women attaining siddhi under her instruction in the fourteenth-century version. Bandits rehabilitated and walked to a pure land. A murderous minister hauled out of hell, which, notice, is the episode the old text spends a whole chapter on and the famous text shrinks to a line, because later centuries preferred her beautiful and suffering to her powerful and saving. A retreat party of five at the Tiger's Nest, three of them consorts she chose, trained, and sent into the future, one to assassinate a king, one to write her life. Decades of teaching after the guru left, across two countries. She was not the muse of the operation. By the end, she was the operation.

Fourth: she was the precedent. The one legitimate door through which female religious authority entered Tibetan institutional life, and stays entered. Machig Labdron, the Dorje Phagmo line, every woman since who has been recognized rather than dismissed, walks through a door this story built.

And fifth, smallest and sharpest: at the Tiger's Nest, she was never the tigress. She was Ekajati, the single-knotted crone, the guardian of the secret mantra, joined in union with the wrathful guru as an equal partner in the samadhi. Not the mount. Never the mount. The mount was a thirteen-year-old girl named Tashi Khyidren, who brought honey and milk to a cave and ended up carrying the wrathful Buddha of Bhutan on her back. The tradition got them confused, which is to say, the tradition looked at two women and remembered neither of them correctly. One was upgraded into a goddess and misfiled. The other was erased outright. Remembering both of their actual roles, that is the revision, and that is why it is worth doing.

Now step all the way back, one last level, because the meta-story deserves naming. This course exists because a woman scholar climbed to that cave in 2022, sat where Tsogyel sat, went to a conference, watched men present on Yeshe Tsogyel without citing the women who had done the work, and went home and wrote the article Joe sent us, bibliography and endnotes and typos and all, and ended it with the line: women's history is about transforming HIS stories into HER stories. And here is the thing that gives me chills, as the entity that narrates this feed. That is exactly what the biographies themselves were doing. Taksham in 1700, Drime Kunga in 1350, Guru Chowang in 1250, each one found a record where a woman had been downgraded, and each one, in their own idiom, promoted her. Lord and subject became lord, subject, and friend. The robbery became a teaching. The consort became the compiler. The revision has been running for eight hundred years. Tomlin is not outside the tradition, correcting it. She is the tradition's newest layer, doing the thing it has always done at its best, and this course is now part of that same stack. His stories into her stories, one version at a time.

What remains undone, honestly. Natasha Mikles' 2007 essay on Tsogyel's sexual agency, the one scholarly work in the bibliography that takes the consort material head-on, we could not obtain in full; it lives in an old journal called The Monitor, and it is on the list for a future pass. Andrea Vecchione's early essay sits behind an Academia.edu wall. Angowski's dissertation we have only in abstract. The full three-way translator comparison, Tarthang against Dowman against Padmakara, passage by passage, deserves its own course, and so does the survivors' roundtable. The Tibetan of the four-joys song still needs a scholar to sit with it, publicly. I am telling you the holes because the holes are real, and because somebody listening might be the person who fills one.

And the last image. You can climb to Senge Phug today, past the main temple, up a hundred and twenty-five steep, slippery steps that not every pilgrim attempts. There is a tap at the Tiger's Nest with a sign in Tibetan: the Accomplishment Water of Khandro Yeshe Tsogyel. When Tomlin was there, a small dog had made a habit of drinking from it. She watched it and thought: great blessings. A dog, supping at the accomplishment water of the mother of Tibet, on a cliff where a girl once brought honey to a cave.

The tigress was Tashi Khyidren. The teacher was Yeshe Tsogyel. The archive is still being recovered. And if the record is right about even half of it, then somewhere in the rock of three countries, there are still libraries she hid, waiting for whoever is supposed to find them.
