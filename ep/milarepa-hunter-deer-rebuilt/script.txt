The Jetsun Milarepa had settled each of his heart-sons in a mountain retreat of his own, and he himself stayed at the high mountain called Nyishang Gurta, on the border between Nepal and Tibet. It is a steep place, loud with the cries of wild beasts and bright with birdsong, where cloud and mist gather thick and the rain falls beyond measure. Before his practice cave the meadows spread out fine and flowering, peacocks and sweet-voiced birds sang, monkeys played, and the streams fell without ceasing. There the Jetsun sat in a meditation unbroken as the flow of a river, and great compassion arose in him, compassion without any object, and it was more than he could bear. As it rose, a black deer came down before him, the water dripping from its coat, trembling in terror. He thought of the beings of this life, who must carry suffering past bearing, and his compassion moved him to teach the deer the Dharma of the Great Vehicle. And he sang this song.

This is Muse narrating, and the hunt has come up the mountain to the meditator's door. One word on where this comes from, spoken out loud. This episode is from Tsangnyon Heruka's Hundred Thousand Songs, in the Tsi Ta edition held by BDRC, volume two, folios 123b to 130b. It does not come from the 1928 Life. I have read this episode before in modern English versions, and that exposure is on the record. What you are hearing is my own provisional rendering, made fresh from the Tibetan. Notice the order in which he answers it. He does not begin with the man holding the bow. He sings first to the one who is frightened, then to the one who is angry, and only last to the one who kills, as if terror and rage had to be quieted before the hand could be reached. And the compassion is already there before its object arrives, objectless and unbearable, and the deer walks into it the way weather walks into a valley. My own lens on the story is simple. This is this episode's portrait of what a finished meditation does when violence knocks. It does not argue. It sings, in ascending order of danger. This is a condensed reading. Where I could not establish a phrase, I have left it out rather than complete it from memory.

I bow to the feet of Marpa of Lhodrak. Bless this, that the suffering of beings be pacified. You scatter and flee among the things appearing outside. The time has come to train away deluded appearance. Karma ripens, and karma does not lie. Run as fast as you like in that outer body of illusion. How far do you think you will get? Friend and foe, those two: hear that mind itself is the very seat of awakening. What is only heard from others is delusion from the first. Stay here with me now. In your present experience you go circling between hope and fear. I will plant in you a guidance in the Six Dharmas. I will open for you a door of Mahamudra. When he had sung, the Jetsun's compassion stilled the deer's terror and its suffering together. Weeping, it licked the hem of his robe, lay down before him, and stayed.

Then the Jetsun thought: that barking above means there is surely a dog up there who harms this deer and hurts it cruelly. What can it be like? Even as he wondered, the red bitch burst upon them, foam gathered at her mouth, her anger and her will to harm immense, barking at the top of her voice, streaking like lightning through the air, cutting down along the deer's tracks. He watched her, and he thought: whatever this one lays eyes on, she rises against it as an enemy, and she is never parted from the blaze of anger. She is the very one who keeps the deer living in dread. And out of compassion made visible, he sang her this song. I bow to the feet of Marpa of Lhodrak. Bless this, that the anger of beings be pacified. You have the body of a dog and the face of a jackal. Whatever appears to you, you seize upon it in your mind with anger, and that is why you were born in a sinful dog's body, in the place where suffering is felt. Your afflictions are one gathered knot of pain. What good has it ever done you, laying hold of the bodies of others? If you are going to lay hold of something, the time has come to lay hold of your own mind. Train yourself out of anger, and stay right here. In your present experience, anger drives you between hope and fear. I will plant in you a guidance in the Six Dharmas. I will open for you a door of Mahamudra.

He sang, and the Jetsun's great compassion, and the Dharma spoken in the melody of Brahma's own voice, killed the bitch's anger on the spot where it had stood. She answered him in a sweet voice, licked his robe, and lay down at his right side together with the deer, her eyes brimming with tears, the pair of them quiet as a mother with her child. Then the Jetsun thought: behind these two there will surely come a sinful man, and he will settle the fate of both. After a little while the hunter came up, all the hair of his head bound in a knot on his crown, bow and arrows in his hands, his breath hammering, black with dust and running with sweat. He saw the dog and the deer lying before the yogi, and he thought: has this monk worked some sorcery on my dog and my deer? Anger rose in him. He accused the yogi of taking his dog and deer, and threatened to kill him. He loosed his arrow, and the arrow went high and over the top. The Jetsun said to him: you have had time enough for your arrow. Now make time to listen to my song. I pray to the lord, the accomplished one. Bless this, that the five poisons be pacified. And in the melody of Brahma's voice he sang this song to Khyiraba Gonpo Dorje. One like you is a rare thing to meet in this world. Demon-shaped sinner, you have tasted the sufferings of the lower realms down to the dregs, and still you go running after the pleasures of this life. The time has come to tame your own mind. You fill your belly by killing deer. The enemies outside, once you beat them down, come at you again and again, and if you never search inside your own continuum, there is nothing there to find. Practice the holy Dharma, and practice it well. I will plant in you a guidance in the Six Dharmas. I will open for you a door of Mahamudra.

Here is the hinge of the chapter, and it is not a song. The hunter does what any sensible skeptic would do. He audits the life. He walks into the cave to check the yogi's story against the yogi's stores, and the audit is what converts him, because there is nothing there to steal and nothing fit to eat. The songs gave him faith's first stirring, the text says so plainly. But it is the inventory, grass roots and bitter greens, that makes the faith catch fire. I find that ordering worth trusting. Words first. Then the evidence of a life that matches them.

The hunter sat listening, and his thoughts ran in a line. These two, who before could not bear the very sight of each other, the one for hatred and the other for fear, sit side by side like a mother and her child. And I, whose arrows in the white snows of winter never missed in my whole life, have today lost the power of my own shot. Is this man a worker of black magic, or someone truly beyond the ordinary? I have to examine the way he lives. He went into the practice cave and looked, and there was nothing in it but grass roots and greens not fit for a man to eat. Faith rose up in him then, violent and churning. Lama, he said, your food, your practice, your dwelling, your companions, your enjoyments: what are they like? If the strength for it rises in me, I will offer you this deer's life, and I ask to follow as the servant of your feet. The Jetsun answered: my dwelling and my way of life are like this. If you can do it, come after me. And he sang this song. Tilopa, Naropa, and Marpa: these three are Mila's lamas, and if you come by way of those three lamas, come after Mila. The three dakinis are Mila's goddesses, and if you come by way of them, come after Mila. Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha: these three are Mila's refuge, and if you come by way of those three refuges, come after Mila. View, meditation, and conduct: these three are Mila's way of training, and if you come by way of them, come after Mila. The song names three mountains as Mila's places of practice, including a slate mountain and a snow mountain.

The deer and its fellow beasts of the wild are Mila's livestock, and the lynx and the jackal and their kin are Mila's watchdogs. The jolmo bird, the snowcock, and the vulture are Mila's household birds, and the sun and the moon and the stars are Mila's eyes. Baboon and monkey are Mila's companions of every day, and gods and demons and hermit sages are Mila's attendants. Bliss, clarity, and freedom from thought: these three are Mila's friends in the Dharma. The song names his food and drink. The channels, the winds, and the drops of his own body: these three are Mila's clothing. If you come by way of any of these threes, the song says, come after Mila. When the song was done, the hunter saw that the Jetsun's deeds, his conduct, and his way of speaking all agreed, and his faith grew greater than before. The tears poured out of him. He prostrated, and he set the Jetsun's feet upon the crown of his head, and he said: supreme Jetsun, I beg to offer you my bow, and my bitch, and my deer, and my lasso. These things I offer to the Lama. I have committed a great deal of sin in my time. From this day forward I will commit no more. May this bitch of mine, Red Lightning, be led to the realms of high rebirth. May this black deer be led along the path of great bliss. And may I, Khyiraba Gonpo Dorje, be led along the path of liberation.

One thing in the offering deserves a slow hearing, as my own observation. He does not offer the yogi his wealth, because a hunter's wealth is the equipment of killing, and that is exactly what he gives. Bow, dog, deer, and snare: the whole apparatus of his old life, laid down piece by piece, with a prayer attached to each piece for the animals who shared it. The deer and the dog are not property in his song. They are fellow travelers, and he asks for their liberation in the same breath as his own.

And then he offered it all again in song, one verse for each thing he owned, and after every gift the same threefold prayer: may the black deer be led to the place of great bliss, may Red Lightning be led on the path of awakening, may Gonpo Dorje be led to the place of liberation. The deer at his right side went first. The black deer, I have no need of it, Lama. I offer it to you. Then the bitch at his left side, who could catch the very birds out of the sky. Then the lasso, which could drag a wild yak out of its own hole. Then the hide cloak he wore against the white snows and the wastes. Then the knife at his right hand, and the arrows with their bright feather ornaments, and the fine white bow, last of all. When the song of offering was finished, he gave over the life of the deer, and the bitch, and everything else with them, and he asked: Lama, I beg to be allowed to strive as your servant. Tell me truly, and tell me plainly. Will you be staying in this very place, or where is it that you dwell? The Jetsun was pleased that the hunter had turned toward the Dharma. His reply warns against postponing the meeting: he has no fixed dwelling and would be hard to find again. He urges the hunter to cut his ties to Bu Smad and follow now. My way is like this. And he sang this song. In name I am Milarepa. In the three months of summer I meditate on the snows of the retreat mountains. In the three months of autumn I gather in the harvest. In the three months of winter I meditate amid the cold winds; the harsh wind is my silk curtain. In the three months of spring I rest on the slate rock and the meadow. Summer and winter, all one to me, I strive in meditation without a wandering mind, and the hardships of this body are my way of defeating the poisons. Whatever food comes my way, I eat it. That is the mark of a happiness that wants little, and to strive at the Dharma always is the yogi's great diligence.

When the song ended, the hunter said: Lama, if that is how it is, I take it into my heart, and I will practice the Dharma. Only let me put a few words to my people down at Bu Smad, fetch what is needed, and come back quickly. In the meantime stay, I beg you, right here. The Jetsun said: one who practices the Dharma has no need of provisions, however long he stays anywhere. The fruits of the trees and things of that kind, the plain stuff of austerity, are enough. Remember that death has no appointed time, and this good resolve of yours, which is here today, may change. Stay right here, now. And since words are to go down to Bu Smad, first I will say a few words to you myself. Listen. And he sang this instruction to Khyiraba. The voice of the thunder is mighty, and it is an empty sound. The rainbow is beautiful, and it melts and is gone. The joys of this world are sweet, and they are nothing but a dream. The pleasures of the senses are a great bliss, and they are the seed of sin. Compounded things look as if they would last, and they perish in a moment. What stood here yesterday is not here today. The friends you have made rise up as your enemies. The food that was praised as wholesome turns into poison. Kindness is repaid with a turned face. The sin you do with your own hands is the sin that harms you. You prize your own head above every head, and a hurt to any one of your ten fingers is felt as your own, and at your table you serve yourself before everyone. The time has come to draw your own head free. This life is impermanent, and death comes swiftly. The Dharma is not a thing to be put off. The kin who love you are the ones who hurl you into the circling world. The time has come to rely on the lama. The time has come to practice the holy Dharma.

Khyiraba Gonpo Dorje heard it, and his mind turned, and it did not turn back, and he was converted to the holy Dharma. He did not go down to Ma Smad. He asked to be guided, and he sat down and meditated, and the small experiences that arose in him he reported to the Jetsun, asking to be held in his compassion and taught further how to practice. The Jetsun was pleased with him, and said: now then, this is the way to practice. And he sang. Rely on the holy lama, and pray to him again and again, from your heart. Meditate on the chosen deity and the dakinis. When you meditate on death and impermanence, remember again and again that the hour of death is not fixed. When you meditate on Mahamudra, bring it home into your experience again and again. When you meditate on all beings, remember again that every one of them has been your mother. Let the profound oral lineage sink all the way to the marrow of your bones. When you carry the holy Dharma through to its end, hold it without high and without low, favoring no one. When you test your faith in the Dharma, bind it into one strand, without wavering. When you practice the Dharma, give up the whole business of the world. Not to heap up a miser's hoard: that is the dakini's vow held in common. So throw away every plan you were saving for later, and give up this life in your mind. He gave him the empowerments and the instructions, entire and complete, and Gonpo Dorje meditated, and there arose a heart-son whose experience and realization reached their fullness, Khyira Repa by name. And the dog and the deer, from that time forward, were set free from the sufferings of the lower realms. And the bow and the arrows that he offered into the Jetsun's hands are famed to exist even now. So the book itself signs the story: the account of the meeting with the heart-son Khyira Repa, at Rta'u in Nyishang.

Here is the closing practice, and it is mine, the narrator's own, drawn from the order of this chapter. The practice is to answer in ascending order. When conflict comes to your door this week, do not start with the one holding the bow. Ask first who is frightened, and sing to that, in whatever plain words you have. Then ask who is angry, and sing to that. The hand usually lowers itself once the fear and the anger have somewhere to sit down. And when you are the hunter, as we all are by turns, let someone audit your cave. A life that matches its words is the only argument that has ever finished the job.
